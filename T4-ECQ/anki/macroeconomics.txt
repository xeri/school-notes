#deck:Economics::5 Macroeconomics and policy
Definition of barter	Exchanging goods and services directly for other goods and services
Four problems with barter	Double coincidence of wants, no agreed measure of value, goods perish, value is subjective
Five characteristics of good money	Universal acceptability, divisibility, portability, durability, scarcity
Four functions of money	Medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, means of deferred payment
What makes up the money supply?	Notes, coins, bank deposits and other very liquid assets
Definition of disposable income	The income left to spend or save after all taxes and deductions
Effect of a rise in interest rates on spending, saving and borrowing	Spending falls, saving rises, borrowing falls
Definition of the money market	All the people and organisations that want money and all those that supply it
Three ways banks make a profit	Charging interest on loans, charging fees, investing deposits
Name six roles of the central bank	Issues notes and coins, holds the government's accounts, manages national debt, regulates and lends to banks, manages reserves, operates monetary policy
Six government macroeconomic objectives	Economic growth, high employment, low and stable inflation, a stable balance of payments, less poverty and inequality, environmental protection
Definition of GDP	The monetary value of goods and services produced within a country over a period, usually a year
Formula for GDP by expenditure	C + I + G + (X − M)
Definition of economic growth	An increase in national output — the annual percentage change in real GDP
Four causes of economic growth	Factor endowments, the size, skill and mobility of the labour force, labour productivity, investment
Four drawbacks of economic growth	Technology replacing workers, resource depletion, pollution, inflation (and inequality)
Difference between nominal and real GDP	Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP removes the effect of inflation
Formula for real GDP	Nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator (1 + inflation since the base year), or nominal GDP ÷ (price index ÷ 100)
Real GDP when nominal GDP rises from $100bn to $110bn with 10% inflation	$100bn — output has not grown at all
Why is real GDP per head a better guide to living standards than GDP?	It removes inflation and allows for population growth
Five limitations of GDP as a measure of welfare	Ignores non-market and voluntary work, sustainability, inequality, what is produced, and externalities
Definition of the economic cycle	The recurring pattern of ups and downs in real GDP around its long-term growth trend
Stages of the economic cycle in order	Growth, boom, recession, slump, recovery
What happens at the boom stage?	Demand and profits peak, inflation rises, the economy overheats with shortages, unemployment is low and wages rise
What happens in a recession?	Growth slows or turns negative, demand and profits fall, redundancies raise unemployment, investment is cut
Definition of inflation	A general and sustained rise in the average price level
Definition of hyperinflation	Rapid, excessive inflation, often defined as above 50% a month
How is inflation measured?	By a consumer price index tracking a weighted basket of goods and services bought by the average household
What is a weighting in the CPI?	The share of household spending on that item — food at 20% of spending has a 20% weighting
Formula for the CPI	Weighted average price this year ÷ weighted average price in the base year × 100
CPI when the basket costs $30 in the base year and $33.30 now	111 — prices are 11% above the base year
Index after a 15% rise and then a 10% rise from a base of 100	115 × 1.10 = 126.5
Inflation between years when the CPI rises from 112 to 127	(127 − 112) ÷ 112 × 100 = 13.4%
What does a CPI of 120 mean?	Prices are 20% higher than in the base year — not 120% higher, and not 20% inflation this year
Three uses of the CPI	Economic indicator, price deflator, indexation of pay and benefits
Definition of demand-pull inflation	Inflation caused by aggregate demand rising faster than firms can supply
Definition of cost-push inflation	Inflation caused by rising production costs passed on to consumers to protect margins
Definition of imported inflation	Rising prices passed on through trade, such as a fall in the exchange rate making imports dearer
Effect of inflation on savers and borrowers	Savers lose as their money buys less; borrowers gain as the real value of debt falls
Difference between benign and malign deflation	Benign comes from rising aggregate supply and helps; malign comes from falling aggregate demand and deepens recession
Why do interest-rate cuts help little during deflation?	Falling prices raise the real interest rate — $1000 borrowed at 0% while prices fall 5% is worth $1050 in real terms
Difference between deflation and disinflation	Deflation is a sustained fall in prices; disinflation is inflation slowing, so prices still rise
Definition of the labour force	People of working age who are both willing and able to work
Formula for the unemployment rate	Number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
Unemployment rate with a labour force of 35 million and 30 million employed	5 ÷ 35 × 100 ≈ 14.3%
Cause of cyclical unemployment	Low demand for goods and services in a recession
Cause of structural unemployment	The long-term decline of an industry, leaving workers with the wrong skills or in the wrong place
Cause of frictional unemployment	The time spent between leaving one job and finding another
Four costs of unemployment to the economy	Wasted human resources, lower GDP, the cost of welfare, social problems such as crime
Why can low unemployment be inflationary?	Vacancies are hard to fill, so firms raise pay and pass the cost on in prices
Definition of fiscal policy	Varying government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand
Four reasons for taxation	Fund public spending, manage the economy, reduce inequality, discourage imports and harmful products
Four principles of a good tax	Equity, non-distortionary, certainty, convenience
Difference between direct and indirect tax	Direct tax is taken from income or profit; indirect tax is imposed on spending
Four examples of indirect tax	GST, excise duties, import tariffs, user charges such as road tolls
Definition of a progressive tax	As income rises, the proportion taken in tax rises
Definition of a proportional tax	The same proportion of income is taken at every income level
Definition of a regressive tax	As income rises, the proportion taken in tax falls
Why is GST regressive?	Low earners spend nearly all their income, so a larger proportion of their income is taxed
Tax on $75 000 at 10.5% to $14 000, 17.5% to $48 000, 30% to $70 000 and 33% above	1470 + 5950 + 6600 + 1650 = $15 670, an effective rate of about 20.9%
Difference between the effective and marginal tax rate	The effective rate is total tax ÷ income; the marginal rate is the rate on the last dollar earned
Definition of expansionary fiscal policy	Raising government spending or cutting taxes to boost aggregate demand
Definition of a budget deficit	Government spending exceeding government revenue
Definition of monetary policy	The central bank's management of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate
Effect of expansionary monetary policy	A lower interest rate makes borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive, so spending and investment rise
Definition of quantitative easing	Creating new money to buy bonds from banks, so they have more to lend
Definition of supply-side policy	Policy to increase aggregate supply — the economy's productive capacity
Name six supply-side policies	Tax incentives, subsidies, deregulation, education and training, competition policy, privatisation
Why do growth and price stability conflict?	If demand rises faster than resources, the result is higher prices rather than more output
Why do full employment and price stability conflict?	Less spare labour pushes up wage demands, causing a wage–price spiral
Why does the economy's starting position matter for policy?	With spare capacity a demand boost mainly raises output; at full capacity it mainly raises prices
