#deck:Economics::1 Scarcity, choice and economic systems
Definition of economics	A social science studying the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services
Definition of scarcity	Limited means against unlimited wants
Three limited means that cause scarcity	Time, money or income, and skill and knowledge
Why are wants described as unlimited?	Once one want is satisfied, another replaces it
Definition of a need	Something people must have to survive, such as food, water, air, shelter or clothing
Definition of a want	Something people would like to have but can survive without
Three questions every economy must answer	What to produce, how to produce it, and for whom
Definition of opportunity cost	The next best alternative forgone
A government ranks healthcare first, police second and education third, then funds healthcare. What is the opportunity cost?	The police spending — only the next best alternative, not police plus education
Definition of a free good	A good so plentiful it has no cost, such as wind or sunshine
Definition of an economic good	A scarce good, which therefore has a cost
Definition of a capital (producer) good	A man-made good used to produce other goods and services, such as a coffee machine or a truck
Definition of the factors of production	The scarce resources, or inputs, that a production process turns into goods and services
Name the four factors of production	Land, labour, capital, enterprise
Reward to each factor of production	Land earns rent, labour wages, capital interest, enterprise profit
Definition of land as a factor of production	The place where business activity happens, and all natural resources
Definition of capital as a factor of production	Man-made resources that help produce other goods and services
Definition of enterprise	The ability to employ and organise the other factors, taking the risk of the venture
Does capital mean money in economics?	No — it means productive equipment; money is only a means of acquiring it
Two features of a public good	Non-excludable and non-rival
Meaning of non-excludable	People who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting
Meaning of non-rival	One person's use does not reduce the amount available to others
Three examples of public goods	Street lighting, lighthouses, police (or national defence)
Definition of a merit good	A beneficial good that consumers undervalue, so it is under-consumed in a free market
Definition of a demerit good	A harmful good that consumers overvalue, so it is over-consumed in a free market
Key difference between merit goods and public goods	Merit goods are excludable and rival; public goods are neither
Definition of division of labour	Splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a specialist worker
Two advantages of division of labour	Higher productivity and efficiency, and easier use of specialised machinery
Three disadvantages of division of labour	Boredom lowers quality, workers feel like a cog and lose motivation, and production stops if a specialist is absent
Definition of derived demand	Demand for something that results from demand for another good, such as lithium for phone batteries
The law of diminishing returns	Adding more of one factor while the others stay fixed eventually adds less and less extra output
What does a production possibility curve show?	The maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced when all resources and technology are used efficiently
Meaning of a point inside the PPC	Production inefficiency — both goods can increase with no opportunity cost
Meaning of a point outside the PPC	Unattainable with current resources and technology
PPC: the firm moves from 90 Coke and 0 Fanta to 60 Coke and 80 Fanta. What is the opportunity cost?	30 cans of Coke for 80 cans of Fanta
What does a PPC bowed outwards show?	Increasing opportunity cost, because resources are better suited to one good than the other
What does a straight-line PPC show?	Constant opportunity cost — resources are equally suited to both goods
What does a steeper section of a PPC show?	A higher opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis
What does an outward shift of the PPC mean?	Economic growth — production possibilities have increased
Five causes of an outward shift of the PPC	Higher productivity, better management, more capital and labour, innovation, discovery of natural resources
Four causes of an inward shift of the PPC	Natural disaster, large outward migration, war, loss of capital
Is moving labour from manufacturing to services a shift of the PPC?	No — a movement along it: more services at the expense of manufacturing
What does a PPC pivoting outwards on one axis only show?	An improvement in one industry only, such as new farming technology
Why does producing more capital goods now matter on a PPC?	It raises future productive capacity, so the PPC shifts out faster later, at the cost of consumption now
Name the four economic systems	Free market, command (planned), mixed, traditional
How a free market economy allocates resources	By price, through demand and supply, with no government involvement
Three advantages of a free market economy	Efficiency, freedom of choice, incentive to work hard
Three disadvantages of a free market economy	Environmental damage, inequality, social problems such as no public goods
Who answers the three economic questions in a command economy?	The government, often through five-year plans
Three advantages of a command economy	Prevents waste, social equality, economies of scale from large national suppliers
Two disadvantages of a command economy	Lack of freedom of choice and career, and no incentive to work harder
Definition of a mixed economy	A combination of free market and command, with the government providing essential services and merit goods
Why are almost all real economies mixed?	Every economy needs some form of government
Definition of a traditional economy	One that answers what, how and for whom by custom and tradition
Difference between the private and public sectors	The private sector is owned by individuals or companies for profit; the public sector is owned by government to provide services
Three functions of the price mechanism	Signalling, incentive, rationing
The signalling function of price	A rising price tells producers buyers want more, and tells consumers to look for substitutes
The incentive function of price	A higher price makes supplying more profitable, drawing resources into that market
The rationing function of price	When a good is scarce its price rises, so it goes to those willing and able to pay most
Four limits of the price mechanism	It ignores external costs, provides no public goods, under-provides merit goods, and allocates by ability to pay rather than need
