#deck:Economics::2 Demand, supply and the market
Definition of demand	The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a stated period
Definition of supply	The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a stated period
The law of demand	As price rises, quantity demanded falls, so the demand curve slopes downward
Two reasons the demand curve slopes downward	The good becomes dearer relative to substitutes, and real income buys less
The law of supply	As price rises, quantity supplied rises, so the supply curve slopes upward
Why does the supply curve slope upward?	A higher price makes production more profitable and covers the higher cost of extra output
Name six conditions of demand	Income, tastes and fashion, price of substitutes, price of complements, population, advertising
Effect of a rise in income on a normal good	Demand increases
Effect of a rise in income on an inferior good	Demand decreases
Definition of a normal good	One whose demand rises as income rises
Definition of an inferior good	One whose demand falls as income rises
Definition of substitutes	Goods where one can replace the other, such as tea and coffee
Definition of complements	Goods consumed together, such as printers and ink
Effect of a rise in the price of a substitute	Demand for this good rises
Effect of a rise in the price of a complement	Demand for this good falls
Effect on demand of expecting a future price rise	Demand increases now
Effect of lower interest rates on demand for credit-financed goods	Demand rises
Name six conditions of supply	Costs of production, technology, indirect taxes, subsidies, number of producers, weather
Effect on supply of a rise in wages or material costs	Supply shifts left
Effect on supply of an improvement in technology	Supply shifts right
Effect on supply of an indirect tax on the producer	Supply shifts left, because the tax acts like a cost
Effect on supply of a subsidy to the producer	Supply shifts right
Effect on supply of more firms entering the market	Supply shifts right
Effect of a drought on agricultural supply	Supply shifts left
What causes a movement along a curve?	A change in the good's own price
What causes a curve to shift?	A change in anything other than the good's own price
Correct term for a movement along the demand curve	An extension or a contraction of demand
Correct term for a shift of the demand curve	An increase or a decrease in demand
Is it correct to say demand increased because the price fell?	No — a price fall causes an extension of demand, not an increase
Definition of market equilibrium	Where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears
Situation at a price below equilibrium	Excess demand (a shortage), so price is pushed up
Situation at a price above equilibrium	Excess supply (a surplus), so price is pushed down
Does equilibrium mean the outcome is fair?	No — it only means the market clears, not that everyone can afford the good
Effect of an increase in demand on equilibrium	Price rises and quantity rises
Effect of a decrease in demand on equilibrium	Price falls and quantity falls
Effect of an increase in supply on equilibrium	Price falls and quantity rises
Effect of a decrease in supply on equilibrium	Price rises and quantity falls
Effect when demand and supply both increase	Quantity definitely rises, but the price change depends on which shift is larger
Two steps to describe after a curve shifts	The shift of one curve, then the movement along the other
Equilibrium when \(Q_d = 100 - 2P\) and \(Q_s = 20 + 2P\)	\(P = 20\) and \(Q = 60\)
Equilibrium when \(Q_d = 120 - 2P\) and \(Q_s = 20 + 2P\)	\(P = 25\) and \(Q = 70\)
Check to perform after solving for equilibrium	Substitute the price back into both equations
Definition of a maximum price	A legal price ceiling, set below equilibrium to make a good affordable
Consequences of a maximum price	Excess demand: shortages, queues, waiting lists, rationing and black markets
Definition of a minimum price	A legal price floor, set above equilibrium to protect producers or discourage consumption
Consequences of a minimum price	Excess supply: surpluses that may have to be bought and stored, and unemployment in a labour market
Two examples of a maximum price	Rent controls and controlled prices for basic food
Three examples of a minimum price	Farm support prices, minimum alcohol pricing, the minimum wage
How to evaluate a price control	Ask who gains, who loses, and whether the intended group actually benefits
Cost-plus pricing	Adding a mark-up to unit cost
Penetration pricing	A low launch price to build market share
Price skimming	A high launch price, lowered later
Definition of price discrimination	Charging different prices to different groups for the same good
What price discrimination requires	Groups that can be kept separate and that have different elasticities of demand
