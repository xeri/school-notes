#deck:Economics::4 Firms, costs and labour markets
Difference between production and productivity	Production measures the value or volume of output; productivity measures efficiency, such as output per worker
Formula for labour productivity	\(\text{labour productivity} = \frac{\text{total output}}{\text{number of workers}}\)
Does a rise in production prove a rise in productivity?	No — output may have risen only because more inputs were used
Four things that raise labour productivity	Training, investment in new capital, better technology, division of labour
Definition of division of labour	Splitting production into separate tasks, each performed by a different specialised worker
Three gains from division of labour	Workers become expert at one task, no time is lost switching tasks, training is cheaper
Two drawbacks of division of labour	Repetitive work lowers motivation, and the whole line stops if one stage fails
Definition of labour-intensive production	Production that uses relatively more labour than capital
Definition of capital-intensive production	Production that uses relatively more capital than labour
Cost structure of labour-intensive versus capital-intensive production	Labour-intensive is mostly variable costs with a lower break-even output; capital-intensive is mostly fixed costs with a higher break-even output
Definition of a sole trader	A business owned and controlled by one person with unlimited liability
Definition of a partnership	A business owned by two or more partners who share profits and, usually, unlimited liability
Definition of unlimited liability	The owner is personally responsible for all the debts of the business
Definition of limited liability	A shareholder can lose no more than the amount invested
What makes limited liability possible?	Separate legal identity — the company is a legal person distinct from its owners
Difference between an Ltd and a plc	An Ltd sells shares privately with all shareholders agreeing the buyer; a plc trades shares openly on the stock exchange
Is a plc a public-sector business?	No — it is private sector; public refers to the sale of shares, not state ownership
Definition of a franchise	An agreement in which the franchisee pays the franchisor to trade using its brand, products and systems
Difference between franchisor and franchisee	The franchisor is the original business selling the rights; the franchisee is the new outlet buying them
Definition of a co-operative	A business owned and democratically run by its members, who share the profits
Four functions of the stock exchange	Raising share capital, facilitating growth, trading government bonds, pricing shares by demand and supply
Difference between a share and a government bond	A share gives ownership, a vote and a dividend; a bond gives no ownership but fixed interest and repayment at maturity
Five ways to measure business size	Number of employees, sales revenue, capital employed, value of output, market share
Definition of internal or organic growth	Expanding the firm's own operations, usually funded from retained profit
Two forms of external growth	A merger and a takeover
Definition of horizontal integration	Joining a firm in the same industry at the same stage of production
Definition of vertical integration, backwards and forwards	Backwards is joining a firm closer to the raw materials; forwards is joining one closer to the end consumer
Definition of conglomerate integration	Joining a firm in a completely unrelated industry, which spreads the risk of failure
Three reasons for the growth of a firm	Economies of scale, affordable specialist managers, market power and easier access to finance
Three reasons against the growth of a firm	Diseconomies of scale, slower decision-making, loss of personal service and flexibility
Definition of economies of scale	Average cost per unit falls as the scale of production rises
Name six internal economies of scale	Purchasing, marketing, financial, technical, managerial, risk-bearing
Definition of external economies of scale	Cost advantages that arise outside the firm but within its industry
Three external economies of scale	A pool of skilled workers, nearby ancillary suppliers, improved shared infrastructure
Definition of diseconomies of scale	Average cost rises because the firm has grown too large to coordinate
Name four diseconomies of scale	Communication, management, labour, agglomeration
Definition of fixed cost	A cost that does not change with output, such as rent or insurance
Definition of variable cost	A cost that changes with the level of output, such as materials
Formula for total cost	\(TC = FC + VC\)
Formula for average cost	\(AC = TC / Q\)
Formula for total revenue	\(TR = P \times Q\)
Formula for profit	\(\text{profit} = TR - TC\)
Why is the average cost curve U-shaped?	Economies of scale lower it at first, then diseconomies of scale raise it
What does SMART stand for?	Specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-specific
Five business objectives	Survival, profit maximisation, profit satisficing, growth or market share, social objectives
Why is the demand for labour a derived demand?	Labour is demanded for the goods and services it produces, not for itself
Three things that shift the demand for labour right	Higher total demand in the economy, higher labour productivity, capital becoming relatively dearer
Who counts in the supply of labour?	People of working age who are both willing and able to work
Three things that shift the supply of labour right	Population growth or net immigration, a higher retirement age, more training places
Formula for the labour force participation rate	\(\text{LFPR} = \frac{\text{labour force}}{\text{working-age population}}\times100\)
Definition of the equilibrium wage	The wage at which the demand for labour equals the supply of labour
Five causes of wage differentials between occupations	Qualifications and skill, length of training, danger and unsociable hours, job satisfaction, scarcity of the skill
Three causes of pay differences within the same occupation	Regional demand and supply conditions, experience and length of service, discrimination
Definition of a trade union	An organisation that works to improve its members' pay and conditions and provides them with support and legal services
Definition of collective bargaining	Negotiation by a union on behalf of all its members rather than worker by worker
Four types of trade union	Craft, industrial, general, white-collar
Four types of industrial action	Strike, sit-in, go-slow, work-to-rule
Two ways stronger trade unions may help and two ways they may harm	Help: higher wages and safer conditions. Harm: higher firm costs causing cost-push inflation and unemployment
What the effect of a union wage rise depends on	Whether productivity rises with it, and the elasticity of demand for the firm's product and for labour
Effect of a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage	Excess supply of labour: more workers offer themselves than firms wish to hire, so those still employed gain but some lose their jobs
