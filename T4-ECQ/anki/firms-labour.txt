#deck:Economics::4 Firms, costs and labour markets
Difference between production and productivity	Production is the value of output; productivity is the efficiency of the factors of production, such as output per worker
Formula for labour productivity	Total output ÷ number of workers
Does a rise in production prove a rise in productivity?	No — output may have risen only because more inputs were used
Five factors that raise labour productivity	New technology, training, investment in capital, division of labour, better management
Definition of labour-intensive production	Production that uses more labour relative to capital, such as fruit picking or hairdressing
Definition of capital-intensive production	Production that uses more capital relative to labour, such as oil refining or car manufacturing
Break-even output of labour-intensive compared with capital-intensive firms	Labour-intensive firms have mainly variable costs and a lower break-even output; capital-intensive firms have mainly fixed costs and a higher one
Definition of unlimited liability	The owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts and may lose personal possessions
Definition of limited liability	Shareholders can lose only the amount they invested
What makes limited liability possible?	Separate legal identity — the company is a legal person distinct from its owners
Three disadvantages of being a sole trader	Unlimited liability, limited access to finance, no continuity
Three contents of a Deed of Partnership	Voting rights, each partner's responsibilities and authority, the profit and loss split
Is a Deed of Partnership a legal requirement?	No — without one, everything is shared equally
How shares in a private limited company are sold	Privately, and every shareholder must agree to the buyer
Three advantages of becoming a private limited company	Limited liability, more capital from more shareholders, continuity
Definition of a dividend	A payment to shareholders out of after-tax profit, usually in proportion to the shares held
Three disadvantages of a public limited company	Huge set-up costs, control can be lost, all financial information is public
Definition of divorce of ownership and control	Shareholders own the company but directors and managers run it, and they may want different things
Is a plc a public-sector business?	No — "public" means its shares are sold to the public, not that the state owns it
Definition of a franchise	An agreement where a franchisor sells the right to use its name, logo, products and methods to a franchisee for a fee
Difference between franchisor and franchisee	The franchisor is the original business selling the rights; the franchisee is the expanding new outlet buying them
What the franchisee pays the franchisor	A start-up fee plus a percentage of revenue or profit
Two advantages of franchising for the franchisor	Quick, cheap expansion without borrowing, and the skills of franchisees
Which businesses can sell shares on the stock exchange?	Public limited companies only
Five functions of the stock exchange	Raising share capital, facilitating growth, selling government bonds, pricing shares by supply and demand, safe regulated trading
Why is share capital called permanent capital?	The firm never repays it; a shareholder must sell the shares to get money back
Difference between preference and ordinary shares	Preference shares get a fixed dividend first but no vote; ordinary shares get what profit remains and one vote per share
Definition of a government bond	A loan to the government paying fixed interest and repaid at maturity, with no ownership
Formula for market capitalisation	Share price × number of shares issued
Name five measures of business size	Number of employees, sales revenue, capital employed, value of output, market share
Formula for market share	The firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100
Definition of internal (organic) growth	Expanding from within, usually funded by retained profit, such as opening another outlet
Difference between a merger and a takeover	A merger is two firms mutually joining to form a new one; a takeover is one firm buying control of another
Definition of horizontal integration	Joining a firm in the same industry at the same stage of production
Difference between backward and forward vertical integration	Backward joins a firm closer to the raw materials; forward joins one closer to the end consumer
Definition of conglomerate integration	Joining a firm in an unrelated industry, which spreads the risk of failure
Definition of economies of scale	Average cost of production falls as the business grows
Name six internal economies of scale	Purchasing, marketing, financial, technical, managerial, risk-bearing
Definition of purchasing economies of scale	Buying in bulk at a discount, so the cost per unit falls
Name four external economies of scale	Access to skilled workers, ancillary firms, joint marketing benefits, shared infrastructure
Definition of diseconomies of scale	Average cost rises because the business has grown too large to run efficiently
Name four diseconomies of scale	Communication, management, labour, agglomeration
Definition of fixed costs	Costs that do not change with output, such as rent, insurance and advertising
Definition of variable costs	Costs that change with output, such as raw materials and hourly wages
Formula for break-even output	Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Break-even output with fixed costs of $4000, a price of $5 and variable cost of $3 a unit	4000 ÷ 2 = 2000 units
Why is the average cost curve U-shaped?	Fixed costs are spread and economies of scale lower it at first; then diseconomies of scale raise it
Why does average revenue usually fall as output rises?	To sell more units the firm must lower its price
What does SMART stand for?	Specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-specific
Most likely primary objective of a new business	Survival — no other objective is possible without it
Four factors that determine a firm's objectives	Corporate culture, legal structure, public or private sector, years in business
Difference between a wage and a salary	A wage is paid by the hour (a variable cost); a salary is an annual amount (a fixed cost)
Five non-wage factors in choosing an occupation	Challenge, career prospects, danger, length of training, personal satisfaction (and recognition)
Why is the demand for labour a derived demand?	Labour is demanded for the goods and services it produces, not for itself
Why does the demand curve for labour slope downward?	Workers are more affordable at lower wages, so firms employ more
Three things that shift the demand for labour left	A recession, higher non-wage employment costs, capital becoming relatively cheaper
Who is excluded from the supply of labour?	People in full-time education and those who choose not to work
Three things that shift the supply of labour right	Population growth or net immigration, a higher retirement age, more provision of education and training
Why can the supply of labour bend backwards?	At high wages a worker can earn more in fewer hours and chooses more leisure
Formula for the labour force participation rate	Labour force ÷ working-age population × 100
LFPR with a labour force of 35 million and a working-age population of 50 million	70%
Definition of the equilibrium wage	The wage at which the demand for labour equals the supply of labour
Five causes of wage differentials between occupations	Abilities and qualifications, unsociable hours and danger, job satisfaction, lack of information about jobs, labour immobility
Three causes of pay differences within the same occupation	Regional demand and supply conditions, experience and length of service, discrimination
Three reasons for a national minimum wage	Equilibrium pay below subsistence, to stop exploitation, to reduce income inequality
Effect of a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage	Those in work earn more, but more people offer to work than firms want to hire, so unemployment may rise
Definition of a trade union	An organisation that aims to improve its members' conditions and provides support and legal services
Definition of collective bargaining	Negotiation by a union on behalf of all its members rather than worker by worker
Four types of trade union	Craft, industrial, general, white-collar
Four grounds for a pay claim	Cost of living rising faster than pay, comparable jobs paying more, high industry profits, higher productivity
Four types of industrial action, most to least severe	Strike, sit-in, go-slow, work-to-rule
Two ways stronger trade unions may harm an economy	Higher costs cause cost-push inflation and unemployment, and industrial action disrupts output and investment
