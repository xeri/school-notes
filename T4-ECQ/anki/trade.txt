#deck:Economics::6 International trade and exchange rates
Definition of specialisation (countries)	Concentrating resources on producing the goods a country's land, climate, skills and capital best suit it to
Why does specialisation lead to trade?	A specialised country is short of everything it has stopped producing, so it must exchange its surplus for them
Six reasons countries trade	Goods they cannot produce, goods others make more cheaply or better, selling a surplus for foreign currency, more choice and competition, larger markets for economies of scale, political ties
Definition of globalisation	The process by which economies become increasingly interconnected through trade, investment, migration and the flow of information
Three drivers of globalisation	Cheaper transport, faster communication, rising incomes
Main risk of specialising in one or two exports	Over-dependence: a fall in world demand or price for those exports hits the whole economy
Who bears the adjustment costs of trade?	Particular workers, industries and regions, not the nation evenly
Definition of absolute advantage	Producing more of a good from the same resources than another country
Definition of comparative advantage	Producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country
Can a country with an absolute disadvantage in everything gain from trade?	Yes, by specialising where its comparative advantage lies
Opportunity cost of 1 tobacco if 200 tobacco costs 250 beef	\(250 / 200 = 1.25\) beef
Three conditions for comparative advantage gains to hold	Opportunity-cost ratios differ, transport costs are not prohibitive, resources can be switched between industries
Definition of a multinational company (MNC)	A firm with its headquarters in one country and operations in others
Three gains to a host country from an MNC	Jobs and transferred skills, capital investment adding to GDP, tax revenue
Three costs to a host country from an MNC	Local firms forced out, profits sent home, tax avoided through transfer pricing
Definition of free trade	Trade with the barriers removed, so goods and services move freely between countries
Definition of protectionism	Restricting trade to shield domestic producers from foreign competition
Definition of a tariff	An indirect tax on imports
Definition of a quota	A physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported
Key difference between a tariff and a quota for the government	A tariff raises tax revenue; a quota raises none
How does a subsidy to domestic producers protect them?	It lowers their costs, shifts domestic supply right and lets them undercut imports
Examples of administrative barriers	Paperwork, licensing, safety and labelling standards, slow customs
Definition of an embargo	A total ban on importing a good
Infant industry argument and its weakness	A new industry needs shelter until it reaches efficient scale; the protection may become permanent
Definition of dumping	Foreign firms selling below cost to destroy local rivals
Strategic industry argument	Food, energy and defence should not depend on foreign supply
Why does protecting jobs in one industry cost jobs elsewhere?	Input costs rise for other firms and exporters face retaliation
Four costs of protectionism	Higher prices and less choice, dearer imported inputs, inefficient firms kept alive, retaliation against exports
What a free trade agreement does	Removes tariffs, bans subsidies, simplifies customs and eases movement of businesspeople
Difference between a free trade agreement and a customs union	A customs union also applies a common external tariff to non-members
Four things the judgement on protection depends on	How long it lasts, which industry and whether it can become competitive, the risk of retaliation, whether a cheaper alternative exists
Definition of an exchange rate	The price of one currency in terms of another
Four sources of demand for a currency	Foreigners buying exports, foreign investors and savers, incoming tourists, speculators expecting a rise
Four sources of supply of a currency	Residents buying imports, residents investing abroad, residents travelling abroad, speculators expecting a fall
Effect of a rise in relative interest rates on the currency	It appreciates, as foreign savers seek the higher return
Effect of higher relative inflation on the currency	It depreciates, as exports become uncompetitive and demand for the currency falls
Effect of a rise in demand for imports on the currency	It depreciates, as more of the currency is supplied to buy foreign currency
Definition of appreciation	A rise in a currency's value, so one unit buys more foreign currency
Definition of depreciation	A fall in a currency's value, so one unit buys less foreign currency
Fixed-rate equivalents of appreciation and depreciation	Revaluation and devaluation
Cost in domestic currency of a 120000 foreign good at 1 domestic = 0.60 foreign	\(120\,000 / 0.60 = 200\,000\)
Floating exchange rate: strength and weakness	Adjusts automatically with no reserves needed; volatile and uncertain for traders
Fixed exchange rate: strength and weakness	Certainty for trade and investment; needs large reserves and ties interest rates to the peg
What SPICED stands for	Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear
Effect of a depreciation on export and import prices	Export prices abroad fall; import prices at home rise
Effect of a depreciation on inflation	It rises, as imports become dearer (imported inflation)
Effect of an appreciation on firms using imported inputs	They gain, because their materials become cheaper
When does a depreciation raise export revenue?	Only when demand for exports is price elastic
Four reasons a depreciation may not improve the current account	Inelastic demand, time lags in contracts, capacity constraints, dearer imported inputs
Definition of the balance of payments	A record of all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period
Four components of the current account	Balance on goods, balance on services, net primary income, net secondary income
Definition of the balance of trade	Visible exports minus visible imports
What goes in net primary income?	Interest, dividends and profits from abroad, and wages sent home
What goes in net secondary income?	Aid and grants, migrant remittances, contributions to international organisations
Is a goods-trade deficit the same as a current-account deficit?	No, services and income flows can offset it
Five causes of a current-account deficit	Uncompetitive exports, an overvalued currency, strong income growth pulling in imports, loss of an export market, a fall in a key export price
When does a current-account deficit matter less?	When it is financed by equity, buys capital goods, and is small and temporary
Three possible problems with a current-account surplus	Weak domestic demand, inflationary pressure and appreciation, retaliation from deficit partners
Four policies to correct a current-account deficit	Depreciation, contractionary fiscal or monetary policy, protectionism, supply-side policy
Why choose supply-side over demand-reducing policy for a deficit?	It attacks structural uncompetitiveness rather than the symptom, but takes years
