#deck:Economics::6 International trade and exchange rates
Definition of globalisation	The process by which the world becomes increasingly interconnected through increased trade and cultural exchange
Two benefits of globalisation for consumers	More choice, and lower prices from specialist producers
Two benefits of globalisation for producers	Larger markets, and the chance to locate where labour and materials are cheaper
Why does specialisation lead to trade?	A specialised country produces little of other goods, so it must trade to obtain them
Main risk of specialising in one or two exports	Over-dependence — a fall in world demand or price for them hits the whole economy
Definition of absolute advantage	Producing more of a good than another country using the same resources
Definition of comparative advantage	Having a lower opportunity cost of producing a good than another country
Australia makes 250 beef or 200 tobacco with half its resources. Opportunity cost of 1 tobacco?	250 ÷ 200 = 1.25 beef
Malawi makes 100 beef or 150 tobacco with half its resources. Opportunity cost of 1 tobacco?	100 ÷ 150 = 0.67 beef
Australia (250 beef, 200 tobacco) and Malawi (100 beef, 150 tobacco): which advantages does each have?	Australia has an absolute advantage in both; Malawi has the comparative advantage in tobacco, Australia in beef
Can a country with an absolute disadvantage in everything gain from trade?	Yes — if it specialises where its opportunity cost is lower and trades at a rate between the two countries' opportunity costs
Four conditions for gains from comparative advantage	Opportunity-cost ratios differ, the trading rate lies between them, transport costs are not prohibitive, resources can switch industries
Definition of a multinational company	A business with headquarters in one country and operations in others
Four advantages of an MNC to a host country	Jobs and training, investment that adds to GDP, competition that improves local firms, tax revenue
Four disadvantages of an MNC to a host country	Local firms forced to close, exploitation where labour law is weak, profits sent home, tax avoidance through transfer pricing
Definition of free trade	The elimination of trade barriers to create large open markets
Definition of protectionism	Restricting trade with other nations to protect domestic firms
Definition of a tariff	An indirect tax on imported goods
Definition of an import quota	A limit on the quantity of a product that can be imported
Definition of an embargo	A total ban on importing a good
Difference between a tariff and a quota for the government	A tariff raises tax revenue; a quota raises none
Infant industry argument for protection	New industries need shelter until they are big enough to compete
Sunset industry argument for protection	Protecting declining industries avoids sudden large-scale unemployment
Five arguments for protection	Infant industries, sunset industries, limiting over-specialisation, correcting a trade imbalance, others are doing it
Four arguments against protection	Less consumer choice and higher prices, protects inefficient firms, fewer new opportunities, retaliation
How can protection harm the protected country's exporters?	Other countries retaliate, raising the price of its exports, and imported materials cost more
What does a free trade agreement do?	Removes tariffs, bans subsidies, simplifies customs and eases business travel between members
Definition of a customs union	A group of countries with no tariffs between them and a common external tariff
Definition of an exchange rate	The value of one currency compared with another
Definition of appreciation	A rise in a currency's value, so one unit buys more foreign currency
Definition of depreciation	A fall in a currency's value, so one unit buys less foreign currency
Convert NZ$25 into US dollars at NZ$1 = US$0.70	25 × 0.70 = US$17.50
A car costs €120 000. Price in NZ$ at NZ$1 = €0.60 and at NZ$1 = €0.62	NZ$200 000, then NZ$193 548 — the NZ dollar has appreciated
An American car's NZ price rises from NZ$50 000 to NZ$60 000 with no change in its US price. What happened?	The NZ dollar depreciated against the US dollar
How are floating exchange rates determined?	By the demand for and supply of the currency on the foreign exchange market
Who demands NZ dollars?	Foreigners buying NZ goods, services or assets, or wanting to hold NZ dollars
Effect of higher relative interest rates on a currency	It appreciates, as savers seek the higher return
Effect of higher relative inflation on a currency	It depreciates, as exports become dearer and demand for the currency falls
Effect of rising demand for a country's exports on its currency	It appreciates
Difference between floating, fixed and managed exchange rates	Floating: set by demand and supply alone; fixed: pegged to an anchor currency; managed: steered by the central bank around a preferred rate
What does SPICED stand for?	Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear
Effect of a depreciation on export and import prices	Export prices fall abroad; import prices rise at home
Effect of an appreciation on firms that use imported materials	They gain, because their materials become cheaper
Effect of a depreciation on inflation	It rises — imported inflation from dearer imports and materials
Effect of an appreciation on jobs and growth	Export jobs are likely lost and net exports fall, so growth is likely to slow
Four reasons a depreciation may not improve the current account	Inelastic demand for exports or imports, time lags, capacity constraints, dearer imported inputs
Five ways exporters can cope with a high exchange rate	Cut prices and margins, find cheaper suppliers, raise efficiency, compete on quality and brand, focus on inelastic goods
Definition of the balance of payments	A record of a country's transactions with the rest of the world over a period, usually a year
Three accounts of the balance of payments	Current account, capital account, financial account
Difference between visible and invisible trade	Visible trade is in goods; invisible trade is in services such as tourism, banking and insurance
Is a New Zealander paying for a hotel in Europe an import or an export?	An invisible import for NZ — money leaves NZ for a service
Formula for the balance of trade	Visible exports − visible imports
Four components of the current account	Balance on goods, balance on services, primary income, secondary income
What is in primary income?	Interest, dividends, profits and wages earned abroad, minus those paid to foreigners
What is in secondary income?	One-way transfers such as foreign aid, migrants' remittances and contributions to international organisations
Current account with trade −446.2, services +66.5, primary income +21, secondary income −58.6 ($bn)	−417.3, a deficit of $417.3bn
What does the financial account record?	Financial investment flows — foreign direct investment, shares and bonds, and hot money
When might a current account deficit not matter?	When it is small (under about 5% of GDP), financed by equity investment, or funds capital goods
When is a current account deficit a problem?	When it is large and persistent, financed by borrowing, or caused by uncompetitive exports
Four policies to correct a current account deficit	Depreciation, contractionary fiscal or monetary policy, protectionism, supply-side policy
