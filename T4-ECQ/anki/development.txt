#deck:Economics::7 Development, population and poverty
Difference between economic growth and economic development	Growth is a rise in real output; development is an improvement in quality of life and living standards
Does economic growth guarantee development?	No — the gains can be wasted or kept by a wealthy elite
How does growth enable development?	Higher real GDP means more can be spent on healthcare and education
Definition of economic welfare	The level of prosperity and the quality of living standards in an economy
Three development objectives	Producing more necessities, raising living standards through education and healthcare, expanding economic and social choice
Why should several development indicators be used together?	Each single indicator misses something, and they often disagree
Main weakness of GDP per head as a development indicator	It is an average, so it hides how income is distributed
Three dimensions of the Human Development Index	Income (GDP per head), education (years of schooling), health (life expectancy)
Range of the HDI	0 to 1, with higher meaning more developed
Four limitations of the HDI	Ignores income distribution, gender equality and human rights, the environment, and cultural differences
What does the Gini coefficient measure?	How evenly income (or wealth) is distributed across a population
Gini coefficient of 0 and of 1	0 is perfect equality; 1 is perfect inequality
Does a high Gini coefficient mean a country is poor?	No — it measures distribution, not the level of income
Purpose of the Human Poverty Index	To measure deprivation directly, with separate versions for developing (HPI-1) and developed (HPI-2) countries
Three measures in HPI-1 for developing countries	Probability of dying before 40, adult illiteracy, population without clean water (also income poverty and underweight children)
Meaning of MEDC and LEDC	More Economically Developed Country and Less Economically Developed Country
Four features of an MEDC	Large modern farms, many firms of different sizes, good transport and communications, a stable government and healthy, educated population
Four features of an LEDC	Low-output farming, few firms, poor transport, little education, healthcare and clean water
Definition of an emerging economy	A country developing rapidly, also called a newly industrialised country, such as China or India
Features of an underdeveloped economy	Very low GDP per head, high population growth, poor infrastructure and services, low literacy and productivity, a large primary sector
Features of a developing economy	Industry expanding and extreme poverty falling, rising foreign investment, but possibly still low living standards
Name five reasons for low economic development	Reliance on agriculture, lack of capital, too little investment in education and health, poor infrastructure, high population growth (also unstable government and unfair trade)
Why does a lack of capital keep development low?	Low incomes leave little to invest in machinery, so too little is produced
Limitation of the MEDC and LEDC labels	They force a continuous spectrum into two boxes and hide differences within countries
Four internal development gaps within a country	Urban against rural, region against region, formal against informal work, and between social groups
Definition of birth rate	Live births per 1000 of the population per year
Definition of death rate	Deaths per 1000 of the population per year
Formula for natural increase	Birth rate − death rate
Definition of net migration	Immigration − emigration
Formula for population change	Natural increase + net migration
Growth of a population of 10 million with birth rate 22, death rate 9 and net migration +20 000	130 000 + 20 000 = 150 000, or 1.5%
Five factors that lower the birth rate	Fewer child deaths, contraception, female employment, later marriage, changing customs
Why do birth rates stay high in many LEDCs?	Many children still die, children work and support parents, and contraception is less known or accepted
Three factors that lower the death rate	Better food, sanitation and housing; medical advances; access to healthcare
Three factors that raise the death rate	Disease such as HIV and malaria, famine and war, unhealthy diets and smoking
Two benefits of higher net migration to a receiving country	Fills skill shortages, and adds tax revenue and entrepreneurship
Two costs of higher net migration to a receiving country	Pressure on house prices and services, and lower wages in some occupations
Definition of the brain drain	Skilled workers leaving a country for higher-paid work overseas
Four features of a population	Age, sex, geographic and occupational distribution
Shape of an LEDC population pyramid	Wide base and narrow top
Shape of an MEDC population pyramid	Narrow base with a bulge in the middle and upper age groups
Formula for the dependency ratio	(Population aged 0–14 + aged 65 and over) ÷ population aged 15–64 × 100
Dependency ratio with 3 million under 15, 1 million over 64 and 6 million aged 15 to 64	(3 + 1) ÷ 6 × 100 ≈ 66.7
Three effects of an ageing population	More pressure on pensions and healthcare, a shrinking labour force, a heavier tax burden on workers
Two consequences of urbanisation	Higher production and living standards, but more use of land and water and more pollution
Definition of optimum population	The population at which output per head is maximised
Definition of over-population	Too many people for the available resources
Definition of under-population	Too little labour to make full use of the available resources
Three measures to reduce population growth	Improve education, provide or subsidise contraception, remove financial incentives to have children
Three measures to increase population growth	More immigration, financial incentives such as paid parental leave, flexible work for families
Definition of poverty	Lacking the financial resources and essentials for a minimum standard of living
Definition of extreme (absolute) poverty	Being unable to meet basic needs for food, water, clothing and shelter — below about US$1.25 a day
Definition of moderate poverty	Basic needs met but no resources to improve life — below about US$2.50 a day
Definition of relative poverty	Lacking the resources that others in the same country normally have
Four groups more affected by poverty	Women, people with disabilities, minority groups, rural people
Name six causes of poverty	Poor access to water and food, no access to jobs, conflict, inequality, poor education, climate change
The poverty cycle	Low income → too little food and healthcare → malnutrition → sickness and poor child development → low energy and concentration → few work opportunities → low income
Four criteria for assessing a poverty-reduction policy	Access, quality, implementation capacity, time before benefits arrive
How does education help reduce poverty?	It raises skills, productivity and earnings, and lowers birth rates
Main risk of relying on aid	Dependency, misdirection, or conditions attached
