#deck:Economics::7 Development, population and poverty
Is economic growth quantitative or qualitative?	Quantitative: a rise in real output
Is economic development quantitative or qualitative?	Both: an improvement in living standards and opportunity
Three reasons growth may not bring development	Gains captured by an elite, spent on consumption rather than capacity, or offset by environmental damage
Definition of economic welfare	The level of prosperity and the quality of living standards in an economy
Three typical development objectives	Producing more necessities, raising living standards through education and healthcare, widening economic and social choice
Limitation of real GDP per head as a development indicator	An average conceals distribution and ignores unpaid work, externalities and what is produced
Limitation of life expectancy as an indicator	It lags and says nothing about quality of life while alive
Limitation of the literacy rate as an indicator	It measures access to education, not the quality of schooling
Limitation of doctors per 1000 people as an indicator	It says nothing about distribution between city and countryside
Why use several development indicators together?	Single indicators mislead, and countries can score well on one and badly on another
Three dimensions of the Human Development Index	Income, education, health
What measures each HDI dimension?	GDP or GNI per head, years of schooling, life expectancy at birth
Range of the HDI	0 to 1, higher meaning more developed
Four limitations of the HDI	Ignores income distribution, ignores gender equality and freedom, ignores the environment, weighting is a judgement
What the Gini coefficient measures	The distribution of income across a population
Gini coefficient values for perfect equality and perfect inequality	0 and 1
Why can Gini and GDP per head disagree?	Gini measures distribution, not the level of income, so a poor country can be equal and a rich one unequal
What MEDC and LEDC stand for	More Economically Developed Country, Less Economically Developed Country
Four limits of the MEDC and LEDC labels	Two boxes for a continuous spectrum, many countries fit neither, implies one path, hides within-country variation
Definition of a newly industrialised or emerging economy	One that is industrialising rapidly
Five typical features of an LEDC	Low-output farming, large primary sector, poor transport and communications, weak government, low life expectancy and schooling
Six reasons some countries have low development	Dependence on agriculture, lack of capital, little investment in skills and health, poor infrastructure, high population growth, weak government or conflict
Four types of within-country development gap	Urban and rural, region and region, formal and informal employment, ethnic, gender and disability groups
Definition of birth rate	Live births per 1000 of the population per year
Definition of death rate	Deaths per 1000 of the population per year
Formula for the natural increase rate	\(\text{birth rate} - \text{death rate}\)
Definition of net migration	Immigration minus emigration
Formula for population growth	\(\text{natural increase} + \text{net migration}\)
Natural increase for 10 million people, birth rate 22 and death rate 9 per 1000	\(13/1000 \times 10\,000\,000 = 130\,000\)
Definition of life expectancy	The average number of years a newborn is expected to live
Four factors that lower the birth rate	Fewer child deaths, contraception, female education and employment, later marriage
Two factors that raise the birth rate	Custom and religion favouring large families, children needed as farm labour and old-age support
Three factors that lower the death rate	Better food, water and sanitation, medical advances and vaccination, healthcare access
Definition of brain drain	The emigration of skilled workers, losing the sending country the cost of training them
What a sending country gains from emigration	Remittances sent home
Definition of population structure	The distribution of the population by age and sex
Shape of a young population pyramid	Wide base, narrow top
Shape of an ageing population pyramid	Narrow base, bulge in the middle and upper age groups
Formula for the dependency ratio	\(\frac{\text{aged } 0\text{–}14 + \text{aged } 65+}{\text{aged } 15\text{–}64} \times 100\)
Dependency ratio with 3 million under 15, 1 million over 64 and 6 million of working age	\((3+1)/6 \times 100 = 66.7\)
Why is the dependency ratio not an employment measure?	It is an age calculation and does not show who actually works or who is dependent
Two causes of an ageing population	A falling birth rate and rising life expectancy
Three pressures from an ageing population	Pensions, healthcare, residential care
Three pressures from a young population	Schools, maternity care, enough jobs to avoid youth unemployment
Three government measures to slow population growth	Provide contraception, educate about family planning, raise female education and employment
Three government measures to raise population growth	Open borders to immigration, paid parental leave and child support, flexible working
Definition of over-population	Too many people for the available resources
Definition of under-population	Too few people to use the available resources
Definition of optimum population	The population at which output per head is maximised
Is a densely populated country automatically over-populated?	No, it depends on its resources and technology
Definition of absolute poverty	Income too low to meet basic needs such as food, clean water, clothing and shelter
Definition of relative poverty	Income low compared with others in the same society
Which poverty measure can be eliminated in principle?	Absolute poverty; relative poverty exists while incomes differ
Six causes of poverty	No work or livelihood, low insecure wages, poor education, poor health, conflict, discrimination
The poverty cycle in one line	Low income, poor food and healthcare, illness, poor schooling and work, few skills, low-paid work, low income
Why does education reduce poverty?	It raises skills, productivity and earning power, and lowers birth rates
Three drawbacks of aid	It may create dependency, be misdirected, and carry conditions
Limitation of a minimum wage as an anti-poverty policy	It does nothing for the informal sector and may cut formal employment
Four tests for any anti-poverty policy	Access, quality, implementation capacity, time before benefits arrive
