#deck:Economics::3 Elasticity, market failure and market structures
Definition of price elasticity of demand	The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price
PED formula	% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
Formula for a percentage change	(new − original) ÷ original × 100
PED value showing elastic demand	Greater than 1 (ignoring the minus sign)
PED value showing inelastic demand	Less than 1 (ignoring the minus sign)
Meaning of unitary elasticity of demand	PED = 1: price and quantity change by the same percentage, so total spending stays the same
Meaning of perfectly inelastic demand	PED = 0: quantity does not change at all; a vertical demand curve
Meaning of perfectly elastic demand	PED is infinite: any price rise makes quantity demanded fall to zero; a horizontal demand curve
PED when price falls from $5 to $4 and quantity demanded rises from 100 to 110	10% ÷ 20% = 0.5, so inelastic
PED when price falls from $40 to $30 and quantity demanded rises from 1000 to 1500	50% ÷ 25% = 2, so elastic
How should a firm with elastic demand change its price to raise revenue?	Lower it — quantity rises by a bigger percentage than price falls
How should a firm with inelastic demand change its price to raise revenue?	Raise it — quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises
Effect of a price rise on total revenue when demand is elastic	Revenue falls
Is a rise in revenue the same as a rise in profit?	No — profit also depends on costs
Seven determinants of PED	Substitutes, necessity, addiction, proportion of income spent, cost of switching, brand loyalty, time
Effect of addiction on PED	Demand is inelastic, as with cigarettes
Why is demand for matches price inelastic?	They take a tiny proportion of income, so a price rise is barely felt
Effect of high switching costs on PED	Demand is less elastic
Effect of time on PED	Demand becomes more elastic the longer consumers have to find alternatives
Definition of price elasticity of supply	The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price
PES formula	% change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
PES when price rises from 100 to 200 cents and quantity supplied rises from 10 000 to 12 000	20% ÷ 100% = 0.2, so inelastic
PES when price rises 10% and quantity supplied rises 20%	2, so elastic
Difference between inelastic and perfectly inelastic supply	Inelastic: quantity supplied changes by a smaller % than price (PES below 1); perfectly inelastic: no change at all (PES = 0, vertical curve)
Effect of time on PES	Supply is fixed at a moment, inelastic in the short run and elastic in the long run
Effect of unused resources on PES	Plenty of spare resources make supply elastic; near full employment it is inelastic
Effect of low barriers to entry on PES	Supply is more elastic, as new firms can enter easily
Four further factors that make supply more elastic	A shorter production period, easier storage, new sources of raw materials, mobile factors of production
When does an indirect tax raise the most revenue?	When demand is price inelastic
When does an indirect tax cut consumption the most?	When demand is price elastic
Effect of an increase in demand when supply is inelastic	Price rises a lot and quantity only a little
Definition of market failure	When supply and demand fail to allocate resources in the way best for society, causing a loss of welfare
Difference between complete and partial market failure	Complete: the market supplies nothing at all; partial: it supplies the wrong quantity or at the wrong price
Six causes of market failure	Public goods, information failure, imperfect market structures, factor immobility, externalities, inequity
Four sources of information failure	Lack of knowledge, misvaluing the future, believing "it won't happen to me", misleading advertising
How factor immobility causes market failure	Workers cannot move to where jobs are or lack the skills, causing unemployment and lost efficiency
Definition of the free-rider problem	People benefit from a public good without paying, so no firm can charge for it and the market provides none
Definition of an externality	A cost or benefit that falls on a third party outside the transaction
Definition of an external cost	A harmful side-effect on third parties that the decision-maker does not pay for
Definition of an external benefit	A positive side-effect enjoyed by third parties
Formula for social cost	Private cost + external cost
Formula for social benefit	Private benefit + external benefit
Example of a negative externality of production	Air pollution from factories (or aircraft noise, fertiliser run-off)
Example of a negative externality of consumption	Traffic congestion and fumes from car use (or second-hand smoke)
Example of a positive externality of consumption	Vaccination protects other people (or education creates a better-informed society)
Why do negative externalities lead to over-production?	Decisions are based on private costs only, so social cost exceeds private cost
Effect of an indirect tax on the cigarette market diagram	Supply shifts left; price rises and quantity falls
Two weaknesses of taxing cigarettes	Demand is inelastic, so consumption falls little; the tax is regressive
Effect of a minimum purchase age on the cigarette market diagram	Demand shifts left; quantity falls
Three weaknesses of rules and regulations	Black markets develop, people break the rules, enforcement is expensive and slow
Three weaknesses of education and advertising campaigns	Opportunity cost, they may be ignored, ingrained habits change slowly
Effect of a subsidy for public transport on the market diagram	Supply shifts right; price falls and quantity rises
Two weaknesses of subsidies	Opportunity cost of government spending, and firms may become less efficient
Two problems with free government provision	Free riders benefit without paying tax, and excess demand creates shortages
Price competition versus non-price competition	Competing on price, against competing on location, convenience, quality, features, after-sales care and advertising
Four features of perfect competition	Many small firms, identical products, no barriers to entry, firms are price takers
Features of monopolistic competition	Many smaller firms selling close substitutes, weak barriers to entry, competing on location, service and goodwill as well as price
Definition of an oligopoly	A market with a few large firms selling close substitutes, with strong barriers to entry
Definition of a duopoly	An oligopoly with only two firms
Definition of a monopoly	One seller of a product with no direct substitutes
Definition of a monopsony	A market with many sellers but only one buyer
Definition of barriers to entry	Factors that prevent or impede new firms from entering a market
Four natural barriers to entry	Economies of scale, capital size, history and brand loyalty, legal requirements
Four artificial barriers to entry	Restricting supplies, predatory pricing, exclusive dealing, full-line forcing
Definition of X-inefficiency	Wasteful use and management of resources by a firm with no competition
Three ways a monopoly may benefit consumers	Economies of scale can lower prices, it avoids wasteful duplication, its profits fund innovation
Three ways a monopoly may harm consumers	It restricts supply to raise prices, offers less choice, and innovates less so quality suffers
