# Economics diagrams

Every diagram you can be asked to draw, what goes on each axis with its units, what must be labelled, and the labelling mistakes that lose marks. Diagram marks are awarded mechanically — axes, curves, equilibria, shifts — so they are the cheapest marks on the paper and the easiest to throw away.

## Rules that apply to every diagram

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Title** | state the market: "Market for tomatoes", not "Diagram 1" |
| **Axes** | drawn with a ruler, meeting at a clearly marked origin **0** |
| **Vertical axis** | the price-type variable, **with its unit** — \$ per kilogram, \$ per hour, \$bn |
| **Horizontal axis** | the quantity-type variable, **with its unit and period** — kilograms per week, number of workers, years |
| **Every curve** | labelled at its end: $D$, $S$, $D_1$, $D_2$, $S_1$, $S_2$, $MPC$, $MSC$ |
| **Every equilibrium** | marked with a dot and named $E_1$, $E_2$ |
| **Every price and quantity** | dropped to the axis with **dashed** guide lines and labelled $P_1$, $P_2$, $Q_1$, $Q_2$ |
| **Every shift** | shown with an arrow and the direction stated in words |

Four errors that cost marks on any diagram: axes unlabelled or missing units; a curve drawn but not labelled; a new equilibrium drawn but not named; and a shift with no arrow, so the examiner cannot tell which curve moved. Label values on the axes as $P_1$, $P_2$ rather than inventing numbers unless the question supplies data.

Use numbers **only** when the question gives you a schedule. Then the scale must be even and the plotted points accurate.

## Production possibility curve

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | good A (e.g. capital goods) | units per year |
| Horizontal | good B (e.g. consumer goods) | units per year |

Draw a curve **concave to the origin** (bowed outwards) meeting both axes. Mark four kinds of point:

| Point | Position | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ | on the curve, on the vertical axis | all resources in good A, none of good B |
| $B$ | on the curve, between the axes | both goods produced, resources fully and efficiently used |
| $C$ | **inside** the curve | **productive inefficiency** — resources unemployed or badly used; both goods can rise with **no** opportunity cost |
| $D$ | **outside** the curve | **unattainable** with current resources and technology |

**Movement along** the curve reallocates resources from one good to the other — this is where opportunity cost is visible, and it is a **movement**, not a shift. State the sacrifice in figures: "moving from A (90 Coke, 0 Fanta) to C (60 Coke, 80 Fanta) gains 80 cans of Fanta at the cost of 30 cans of Coke."

**Plotting from a schedule.** When a table of combinations is given, a typical 5-mark allocation is one mark each for a **title** ("PPC for …"), **labelled axes**, an **accurate, even scale**, **points plotted** and labelled, and an **accurately drawn curve** through them. Show inefficiency with a labelled point inside the curve and state the figures: "60 Coke and 60 Fanta — Fanta could rise by 20 with no loss of Coke."

**Shifts.** The whole curve moves **outwards** (right) for economic growth: higher productivity, better management, more capital or labour, innovation, or discovery of natural resources. It moves **inwards** for natural disaster, war, large emigration or loss of capital. The teaching material also lists recession as an inward cause; strictly, a recession first moves the economy to a point **inside** the existing curve, and the curve moves in only if capacity is permanently lost. A shift of **only one axis end** shows growth in one industry alone — for example a breakthrough in farming technology pivots the curve outwards on the agriculture axis only.

**Shape.** Bowed outwards means **increasing opportunity cost** — resources are not equally suited to both goods, so each extra unit of one costs more of the other. A **straight-line** PPC means **constant** opportunity cost. A steeper section means a higher opportunity cost of the horizontal good.

Marks are lost by: drawing the curve bowed the wrong way (concave to the origin is correct; convex is wrong); failing to extend the curve to both axes; calling an inward movement of a point a "shift"; and saying an outward shift "increases demand" — it increases **productive capacity**.

## Demand and supply curves

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | price | \$ per unit |
| Horizontal | quantity | units per week (or per month) |

**Demand slopes downward**, labelled $D$. **Supply slopes upward**, labelled $S$. Both should be straight lines unless the question requires a curve, and both must be labelled at the right-hand end where they will not be confused after a shift.

Marks are lost by: putting quantity on the vertical axis; omitting the time period from the quantity unit; drawing supply downward-sloping by accident; and labelling the curves "demand" and "supply" in the middle of the diagram where a shifted curve then overlaps the text.

## Movement along versus a shift

This is the single most heavily penalised confusion in the subject.

| Cause of the change | What happens on the diagram | Correct wording |
|---|---|---|
| the good's **own price** changes | a **movement along** the existing curve | an **increase** or **decrease in quantity demanded** (or quantity supplied) — also called an extension or contraction |
| **anything else** changes | the **whole curve shifts** | an **increase** (right) or **decrease** (left) in demand or supply |

To show a movement along, mark two points on the **same** curve, join each to both axes with **dotted** lines, label the original price and quantity $P_1$, $Q_1$ and the new ones $P_2$, $Q_2$, and add **arrows** showing the direction of the change. To show a shift, draw a **second complete curve** parallel to the first, label it $D_2$ or $S_2$, and put an arrow between them.

**Never write "demand increased because the price fell."** A price fall causes an **increase in quantity demanded** — a movement along the existing curve. Equally, a shift of demand is never caused by the good's own price.

## Equilibrium, excess demand and excess supply

Draw $D$ and $S$ crossing. Mark the intersection $E_1$ with a dot, and drop dashed lines to $P_1$ and $Q_1$.

| Price | Situation | On the diagram | Pressure on price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Below** $P_1$ | **excess demand** (shortage) | horizontal gap at that price, from $S$ across to $D$ | **upwards** |
| **At** $P_1$ | equilibrium — the market clears | curves intersect | none |
| **Above** $P_1$ | **excess supply** (surplus) | horizontal gap at that price, from $D$ across to $S$ | **downwards** |

To show a shortage: draw a horizontal dashed line at the low price, label where it cuts $S$ as $Q_s$ and where it cuts $D$ as $Q_d$, and label the gap between them "excess demand" with a double-headed arrow. The gap is always measured **horizontally**, never vertically. Then explain the adjustment: buyers bid the price up, which **reduces quantity demanded** and **increases quantity supplied** until the market clears at $E_1$.

Marks are lost by: measuring the shortage vertically; shading an area instead of marking the horizontal distance; and saying "supply increases" when price rises — **quantity supplied** increases; supply itself has not shifted.

## The four single shifts

Each needs the same three-step treatment: shift one curve, show the movement **along** the other, then read off the new price and quantity.

| Change | Curve that shifts | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | $D_1 \rightarrow D_2$, right | rises | rises |
| Demand decreases | $D_1 \rightarrow D_2$, left | falls | falls |
| Supply increases | $S_1 \rightarrow S_2$, right | falls | rises |
| Supply decreases | $S_1 \rightarrow S_2$, left | rises | falls |

Mark $E_1$ and $E_2$, with $P_1$, $P_2$, $Q_1$, $Q_2$ on the axes. A worked example with labels is in [notes/markets.md](notes/markets.md).

**When both curves shift**, one of the two outcomes becomes ambiguous and depends on the relative size of the shifts. Draw both, say which outcome is certain, and state explicitly that the other depends on which shift is larger — that is the evaluation mark. If supply is relatively **inelastic** (steep), an increase in demand has a large effect on price and a small one on quantity; say so.

Marks are lost by: shifting both curves when only one has changed; shifting a curve when the change was the good's own price; and drawing the new curve crossing the old one instead of parallel to it.

## Maximum price (price ceiling)

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | price | \$ per unit |
| Horizontal | quantity | units per week |

Draw the free-market equilibrium at $P_1$, $Q_1$. Then draw a **horizontal solid line below** $P_1$ and label it "maximum price $P_{max}$" — a ceiling only binds if it is **below** equilibrium.

At $P_{max}$, read quantity supplied $Q_s$ off the supply curve (to the left) and quantity demanded $Q_d$ off the demand curve (to the right). **Mark the horizontal gap $Q_s$ to $Q_d$ with a double-headed arrow and label it "excess demand / shortage".**

Consequences to write alongside: queues, waiting lists, rationing, allocation by favouritism, black markets at prices above $P_{max}$, falling quality and reduced investment because producers earn less.

Marks are lost by: drawing the ceiling **above** equilibrium, where it does nothing; labelling the shortage as $Q_1 - Q_s$ instead of $Q_d - Q_s$; and claiming quantity traded is $Q_d$ — the quantity actually traded is $Q_s$, the lesser of the two.

## Minimum price (price floor)

Draw the equilibrium at $P_1$, $Q_1$, then a **horizontal solid line above** $P_1$ labelled "minimum price $P_{min}$". A floor only binds if it is **above** equilibrium.

At $P_{min}$, quantity supplied $Q_s$ is read off $S$ (to the right) and quantity demanded $Q_d$ off $D$ (to the left). **Mark the horizontal gap $Q_d$ to $Q_s$ with a double-headed arrow and label it "excess supply / surplus".**

Consequences: unsold stock the government may have to buy and store, resources wasted on unwanted output, and — in a labour market — unemployment. Quantity actually traded is $Q_d$, the lesser of the two.

Marks are lost by: putting the floor below equilibrium; shading an area rather than marking the horizontal distance; and forgetting that a minimum price benefits producers **only** if someone buys or stores the surplus.

## Elastic and inelastic demand

Draw two separate diagrams, or two demand curves through the same point, and apply the **same** price change to both so the different quantity responses are visible.

| Curve | Appearance | $|\text{PED}|$ | Effect of a price rise on revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | relatively **flat** (shallow) | $>1$ | revenue **falls** |
| **Inelastic** | relatively **steep** | $<1$ | revenue **rises** |
| **Unit elastic** | — | $=1$ | revenue unchanged |
| **Perfectly inelastic** | **vertical** | $=0$ | quantity does not change at all |
| **Perfectly elastic** | **horizontal** | $=\infty$ | any price rise kills demand entirely |

$$\text{PED} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P} \qquad \text{PES} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}$$

To show revenue, shade the rectangle $P \times Q$ at each price and compare the two areas. The same shapes apply to supply: a steep supply curve is **inelastic**, a flat one **elastic**, a vertical one perfectly inelastic (a fixed stock) and a horizontal one perfectly elastic.

Marks are lost by: calling a curve "elastic" or "inelastic" without applying a price change to demonstrate it; drawing two curves of different steepness but applying different price changes, which proves nothing; and describing a vertical curve as perfectly elastic — vertical is perfectly **in**elastic.

## Government intervention in market failure

The teaching material shows intervention with ordinary demand and supply diagrams. Each is a single shift, drawn with the three-step treatment above.

| Policy | Example | Curve that shifts | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Indirect tax** on a demerit good | cigarettes | supply shifts **left** ($S_1 \rightarrow S_2$) — the tax is a cost to the producer | price rises, quantity falls from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$; with inelastic demand the fall is small |
| **Regulation** limiting who may buy | a minimum legal age for buying cigarettes | demand shifts **left** ($D_1 \rightarrow D_2$) — fewer people willing and able to buy at every price | quantity falls from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$ |
| **Subsidy** for a good with external benefits | public transport | supply shifts **right** ($S_1 \rightarrow S_2$) — the subsidy cuts the producer's costs | price falls, quantity rises from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$ |
| **Advertising and education** | anti-smoking campaign | demand shifts left (demerit good) or right (merit good) | consumption moves towards the social optimum |

Marks are lost by: shifting demand for a tax or subsidy (both act on the **producer's costs**, so they shift **supply**); and shifting supply for an age limit (it changes who can **buy**, so it shifts **demand**).

## External costs and external benefits

This is the standard marginal-cost diagram used in most textbooks; the teaching material explains externalities in words and uses the intervention diagrams above, so use this version when a question asks for social and private costs on a diagram.

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | cost / benefit per unit | \$ per unit |
| Horizontal | quantity | units per year |

$$\text{social cost} = \text{private cost} + \text{external cost} \qquad \text{social benefit} = \text{private benefit} + \text{external benefit}$$

**Negative externality (external cost in production).** Draw the private cost curve $MPC$ (this is the market supply curve) and **above** it a parallel $MSC$, with the vertical gap between them equal to the external cost per unit. The demand curve is $MPB = MSB$ (no externality in consumption).

- Market outcome: where $MPB$ cuts $MPC$ — price $P_1$, quantity $Q_1$.
- Socially optimum outcome: where $MSB$ cuts $MSC$ — price $P^*$, quantity $Q^*$, which is **smaller**.
- The market **over-produces** by $Q_1 - Q^*$.
- Shade the triangle bounded by $MSC$, $MSB$ and the vertical at $Q_1$, and label it **welfare loss** (deadweight loss).

**Positive externality (external benefit in consumption).** Draw $MPB$ (the market demand curve) and **above** it a parallel $MSB$, the vertical gap being the external benefit per unit. Supply is $MPC = MSC$.

- Market quantity $Q_1$ is where $MPB$ cuts $MPC$; the social optimum $Q^*$ is where $MSB$ cuts $MSC$, and is **larger**.
- The market **under-produces** by $Q^* - Q_1$.
- Shade the welfare-loss triangle between $MSB$, $MSC$ and the vertical at $Q_1$.

Marks are lost by: drawing $MSC$ below $MPC$ for a negative externality (society's cost is **higher**, so the curve is **above**); shifting only one point rather than drawing a whole second curve; labelling the welfare loss as a rectangle; and forgetting to mark both $Q_1$ **and** $Q^*$ — the diagram's whole point is the gap between them.

## Indirect tax and its incidence

Draw $D$ and $S_1$ crossing at $E_1$ ($P_1$, $Q_1$). A **specific** (per-unit) tax shifts supply **vertically upwards by the tax** to $S_2$, parallel to $S_1$; an **ad valorem** (percentage) tax shifts it upwards and **pivots** it, so the gap widens as price rises. Label the new equilibrium $E_2$ ($P_2$, $Q_2$). Price rises, quantity falls.

Mark the vertical distance between $S_1$ and $S_2$ at $Q_2$ and label it "tax per unit". The price the producer keeps is $P_3$, where the vertical at $Q_2$ meets $S_1$.

| Area to shade | Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer incidence | $(P_2 - P_1)\times Q_2$ | the part of the tax paid by buyers through a higher price |
| Producer incidence | $(P_1 - P_3)\times Q_2$ | the part absorbed by sellers through a lower net price |
| Total tax revenue | $(P_2 - P_3)\times Q_2$ | the two boxes stacked together |

**The incidence depends on elasticity.** The more **inelastic** demand is, the larger the consumer's share — which is why taxes on fuel, tobacco and alcohol fall mainly on consumers. The more elastic demand is, the more the producer must absorb.

Marks are lost by: shifting **demand** instead of supply (an indirect tax is a cost to the producer, so it shifts **supply left/up**); drawing the vertical gap at $Q_1$ rather than $Q_2$; assuming the price rises by the full amount of the tax (it rises by less whenever supply is not perfectly elastic); and reporting tax revenue as tax × $Q_1$.

## Subsidy

A subsidy is the mirror image. Supply shifts **vertically downwards by the subsidy per unit** from $S_1$ to $S_2$, so price falls from $P_1$ to $P_2$ and quantity rises from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$. The price the producer receives, $P_3$, is **above** $P_1$: the vertical gap $P_3 - P_2$ at $Q_2$ is the subsidy per unit.

| Area | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost to the government | $(P_3 - P_2)\times Q_2$ |
| Benefit to consumers | $(P_1 - P_2)\times Q_2$ |
| Benefit to producers | $(P_3 - P_1)\times Q_2$ |

The consumer does **not** get the whole subsidy. How it splits depends on elasticity, exactly as with a tax, and this is the standard evaluation point: a subsidy on a good with inelastic demand mostly raises producer revenue instead of consumption.

Marks are lost by: shifting demand right instead of supply right; drawing the producer's price below the original price; and asserting that price falls by the full subsidy.

## The labour market

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | wage rate | \$ per hour (or \$ per year) |
| Horizontal | quantity of labour | number of workers (or worker-hours per week) |

Draw $D_L$ sloping **downward** (labour is cheaper to employ at a lower wage — and remember the demand for labour is a **derived** demand) and $S_L$ sloping **upward** (a higher wage attracts more workers and raises the opportunity cost of not working). They cross at the **equilibrium wage** $W_1$ and employment $N_1$.

**Shifts.** $D_L$ shifts right with higher product demand or higher labour productivity, and left with recession, higher non-wage employment costs or cheaper capital. $S_L$ shifts right with population growth, net immigration, a higher retirement age or more training places, and left with a higher school leaving age, emigration or worse non-wage conditions. A market with high demand and tightly restricted supply gives a high equilibrium wage — that is how to explain any wage differential on a diagram.

**Minimum wage or union-negotiated wage.** Draw a **horizontal line above** $W_1$ and label it $W_2$ ("minimum wage" or "union wage"). Then read off **two** different quantities at $W_2$:

| Quantity | Read from | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $N_2$ | the **supply** curve, to the right | workers now willing to work |
| $N_3$ | the **demand** curve, to the left | workers firms now wish to hire |

Employment is $N_3$ — the **lesser** of the two. Mark the horizontal gap $N_3$ to $N_2$ with a double-headed arrow and label it "excess supply of labour / unemployment". Those still in work gain a higher wage; $N_1 - N_3$ workers lose their jobs, and $N_2 - N_1$ extra entrants find none.

Then evaluate: the size of the effect depends on **how far above equilibrium** $W_2$ is set and **how elastic the demand for labour** is. If demand for labour is inelastic, the pay rise comes at little cost in jobs; if elastic, the job losses are large. Add that firms may respond by raising prices, cutting hours or switching to capital-intensive methods, and that if productivity rises with the wage, $D_L$ may shift right and offset the loss.

A **backward-bending** supply curve of labour is also examinable: above some wage the worker chooses leisure, so the curve turns back to the left.

Marks are lost by: labelling the vertical axis "price"; drawing only one quantity at the minimum wage, which makes the excess supply invisible; drawing the minimum wage below equilibrium; and claiming unemployment is $N_2 - N_1$ rather than $N_2 - N_3$.

## The economic cycle

| Axis | Variable | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | real GDP | \$bn (constant prices) |
| Horizontal | time | years |

Draw a **straight upward-sloping dashed line** across the diagram and label it **long-term growth rate** (trend). Then draw a **wave oscillating around it** and label the stages in order, as the teaching material's diagram does:

| Stage | Position relative to trend | What to annotate |
|---|---|---|
| **Growth** | rising from below the trend line to above it | output, incomes and employment rising; new firms; profits up |
| **Boom / peak** | the top of the wave, above trend | capacity shortages; rising costs and inflation; low unemployment; interest rates likely raised |
| **Recession / downturn** | falling, crossing below trend | growth slows and may turn negative; redundancies; investment cut; interest rates likely cut |
| **Slump** | the trough, furthest below trend | the deepest, most prolonged part of the downturn |
| **Recovery / upturn** | rising out of the trough back towards trend | confidence returns; output, revenue and profit recover; rehiring |

The **vertical** distance from the wave to the trend line is the **output gap**: above the line is a positive (inflationary) gap, below it a negative (deflationary) gap with spare capacity. This is what lets you say whether a demand boost would raise output or just prices.

Marks are lost by: omitting the trend line altogether, which leaves nothing for the cycle to be a cycle *around*; labelling the vertical axis "GDP" without specifying **real** GDP; drawing the wave with no net upward drift, which denies long-run growth; and labelling the stages out of order. If the vertical axis is the **rate** of growth rather than the level of real GDP, say so — on that version a recession is the part of the curve **below zero**, not merely below the trend.

## Population pyramids

A population pyramid is a pair of horizontal bar charts back to back: **males** on one side, **females** on the other, with **age groups** (0–4, 5–9 … 80+) up the vertical axis and the **number or percentage** of the population along the horizontal axis.

| Shape | Typical of | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Wide base, narrow top** (a true pyramid) | an LEDC with a young population | high birth rate, high death rate, short life expectancy; a large young dependent population |
| **Narrow base, bulge in the middle and upper groups** | an MEDC with an ageing population | low birth rate, long life expectancy; a growing elderly dependent population |

Marks are lost by: mixing up which side is male and female without a key; reading the horizontal axis as age; and describing a shape without saying what birth and death rates it implies.
