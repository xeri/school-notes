## Exam technique

**Command words and depth.** *Identify* or *state* needs a word or phrase. *Define* needs a precise meaning, usually with a key term (for example "the next best alternative **forgone**"). *Explain* needs a reason linked to a result. *Analyse* needs a developed causal chain — "because… which leads to… so…" — usually with a diagram or figures. *Discuss* needs both sides and a judgement.

**Calculations.** Write the formula, substitute, give the unit, and say what the answer means ("PED = 2, so demand is elastic"). Percentage changes are always divided by the **original** value.

**Diagrams.** Label both axes, every curve, every equilibrium and every shift — see [DIAGRAMS.md](DIAGRAMS.md). Then explain the diagram in words: the shift, then the movement along the other curve.

**Use the context.** Most questions carry a short case. Refer to its country, firm, product and figures in every paragraph — a mark scheme can cap an answer that never mentions the case (one revision question limits answers with no reference to cars to 4 of 6 marks).

### Long-answer (8- and 10-mark) questions

These are usually a statement or question with **two sides**, such as *Discuss whether small firms can compete successfully against large firms* or *Discuss whether governments should discourage car use*.

1. **Write formal paragraphs**, essay style — no bullet points, abbreviations, slang or personal statements such as "I think". **No introduction** is needed.
2. **Cover both sides.** Plan what the question is asking: (a) the reasons it is so, (b) the reasons it is not, (c) a judgement. Mark schemes typically allow up to 5 marks for each side, so a one-sided answer cannot score highly.
3. **Develop each point.** State the point, explain the mechanism, then the consequence, with an example. Use definite language where it is justified ("this **will** raise costs", "this is **likely** to…").
4. **Finish with an evaluative conclusion.** It is not a summary, a one-line recap or a repeat of what you wrote — the commonest weakness. It makes a real overall judgement and says **what the answer depends on**: elasticity, time lags, spare capacity, the size of the change, the state of the economy, the type of product. A detailed evaluative conclusion earns the top 1–2 marks; it is what lifts an answer to 7 or 8 out of 8. A good conclusion can also suggest a better alternative — for car use, promoting public transport rather than simply discouraging cars.
5. **Watch for "always".** *Discuss whether a low inflation rate always benefits an economy* or *whether monopolies are always harmful* — if there are two sides, it is almost never "always". Say so in the conclusion.

**What lifts a 7/10 answer higher:** definite statements rather than hedging; specific examples (countries, firms, industries); and the standard evaluation tools — **price elasticity** (a depreciation raises export revenue only if demand is elastic), **time lags** (contracts are fixed in the short run), and **capacity constraints** (factories at full capacity cannot produce more).

### Worked long answer — *Discuss whether demand for cars is likely to increase in the future.* [10]

**Why it might increase** (up to 5 marks): rising incomes, especially in emerging economies, let people switch from buses to cars; car prices may fall; technology (hybrid and electric cars) raises quality and availability; population growth creates more buyers; easier credit lets people buy on loans; effective advertising persuades more people to buy.

**Why it might not** (up to 5 marks): substitutes such as public transport may become cheaper; environmental concern may push people to walk or cycle; the price of complements may rise — petrol, parking, road tolls, congestion charges; governments may tax or restrict car use to cut external costs; a recession and rising unemployment would cut confidence; new forms of transport may appear.

**Evaluative conclusion** (up to 2 marks): for example — global demand is likely to rise because income growth in emerging economies outweighs falling demand in developed cities, but its composition will change towards electric cars; the outcome depends on fuel and battery costs, on how far governments push green policies, and on whether working from home keeps cutting commuting.

## 1. Scarcity, choice and economic systems

- The basic economic problem: unlimited wants against limited means (time, income, skill and knowledge); needs and wants; scarcity.
- Opportunity cost, and applying it to consumers, producers and governments.
- Producers and consumers; goods and services; free and economic goods; consumer and capital goods; natural, human and man-made resources.
- The factors of production — land, labour, capital, enterprise — and their rewards.
- Public goods, merit goods and demerit goods, and why merit goods are not public goods.
- Specialisation and the division of labour; derived demand; the law of diminishing returns.
- The production possibility curve: efficient, inefficient and unattainable points; movements along the curve and shifts of the curve; increasing and constant opportunity cost; capital goods against consumer goods.
- Economic systems — free market, command (planned), mixed and traditional — and how each answers what, how and for whom to produce; the private and public sectors.
- The role of the price mechanism in allocating resources: signalling, incentive and rationing functions.

## 2. Demand, supply and the market

- Demand and quantity demanded; individual and market demand; demand schedules and ceteris paribus; the law of demand and the demand curve.
- The **conditions of demand** (what shifts the curve): tastes and fashion, disposable income, the price of substitutes and complements, population, advertising and events, expectations.
- Normal, inferior, luxury, substitute and complementary goods.
- Supply and quantity supplied; market supply; the law of supply and the supply curve; related goods; price compared with cost.
- The **conditions of supply** (what shifts the curve): costs of production, productivity and technology, indirect taxes, subsidies, tariffs, the number of firms, good and bad seasons, the price of related goods; environmental, legal, political and cultural influences.
- The distinction between a **movement along** a curve (a change in quantity demanded or supplied, caused by the good's own price) and a **shift** of a curve (a change in demand or supply, caused by anything else).
- Market equilibrium: equilibrium price and quantity; excess demand and excess supply; how price adjusts to clear the market.
- The effects of a shift in demand or supply on equilibrium price and quantity; the ambiguity when both shift together.
- Maximum and minimum price controls and their consequences.
- Pricing strategies used by firms, and the factors that influence the price set.

## 3. Elasticity, market failure and market structures

- Price elasticity of demand: calculation, interpretation, elastic and inelastic demand, the determinants of PED, and the effect of a price change on total revenue.
- Price elasticity of supply: calculation, the determinants (time, spare resources, stocks, production period, factor mobility, barriers to entry), elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic supply.
- The usefulness of elasticity to firms and to government (especially indirect taxation).
- Market failure: its meaning; complete and partial failure; its causes — public goods, information failure, imperfect market structures, factor immobility, externalities and inequity.
- External costs and external benefits of production and consumption; private, external and social costs and benefits.
- Merit and demerit goods; public goods and the free-rider problem; information failure.
- Government intervention to correct market failure: indirect taxes, subsidies, regulation, education and advertising, direct provision — with the limits of each.
- Market structures: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly and duopoly, monopoly and monopsony; price and non-price competition; natural and artificial barriers to entry; the effects of market power on price, choice, quality and innovation.

## 4. Firms, costs and labour markets

- Production and productivity; the factors that affect labour productivity.
- Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production.
- Types of business: sole trader, partnership, private and public limited company, franchise, co-operative; unlimited and limited liability.
- The stock exchange: its functions; shares, preference and ordinary shares, government bonds; market capitalisation.
- Measuring business size, the advantages of small and large firms, and internal and external growth (horizontal, vertical and conglomerate integration).
- Economies and diseconomies of scale: internal and external.
- Costs: fixed, variable, direct, indirect, total, average and marginal cost; total and average revenue; profit; contribution and break-even.
- Business aims and objectives: mission statements; SMART objectives; survival, profit maximisation and satisficing, growth, market share, sales revenue, shareholder value, social objectives; what determines them.
- Choosing an occupation: wage and non-wage factors; methods of payment; changes in earnings over a lifetime.
- The demand for labour as a **derived** demand; the supply of labour and its determinants; the labour force participation rate.
- Wage determination; the causes of wage differentials between and within occupations, including discrimination; public- and private-sector pay.
- The minimum wage and the role of government in labour markets.
- Trade unions and collective bargaining; industrial action; the effects on wages, employment and the economy.

## 5. Macroeconomics and policy

- Money: barter and its problems; the characteristics of good money; the functions of money; the money supply.
- Spending, saving and borrowing, and the factors that influence them.
- The money market: commercial banks, investment banks, credit unions and the central bank; how banks make a profit; interest rates.
- The circular flow of income; the government as producer and employer.
- Government macroeconomic objectives: growth, employment, price stability, balance of payments stability, reducing poverty and inequality, protecting the environment.
- GDP and its components; real GDP and real GDP per head; **nominal vs real** and why the distinction matters; the limitations of GDP as a measure of welfare.
- Economic growth: its causes, benefits and costs; the difference between growth and development.
- The economic cycle: growth, boom, recession, slump and recovery, and the effect on firms and households; the shapes of recession.
- Inflation: definition, measurement by a weighted price index, demand-pull, cost-push and imported causes, and the consequences for different groups. Hyperinflation, deflation and disinflation.
- Employment and unemployment: how unemployment is measured; cyclical, structural, frictional and seasonal unemployment; full- and part-time work; the consequences and the policies.
- Fiscal policy: the reasons for taxation, the principles of a good tax, direct and indirect taxes, progressive, proportional and regressive taxes; government spending; budget deficit and surplus.
- Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate; how changes transmit to spending, saving and investment.
- Supply-side policy: education and training, tax incentives, subsidies, deregulation, competition policy, removing trade barriers, privatisation.
- Conflicts between policy objectives, and the significance of the economy's starting position.

## 6. International trade and exchange rates

- Globalisation and its benefits and drawbacks for consumers, producers and governments.
- Specialisation and the gains from trade; absolute and comparative advantage; why countries trade.
- Multinational companies and their effects on home and host countries.
- The benefits and risks of international trade, including dependence on overseas markets and the adjustment costs borne by particular workers and regions.
- Free trade and protectionism: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes and administrative barriers; the arguments for protection (infant and sunset industries, over-specialisation, trade imbalances, employment, dumping, strategic industries) and the counter-arguments, including retaliation; free trade agreements.
- Exchange rates: how they are determined; appreciation and depreciation; floating, fixed and managed systems.
- The effect of exchange-rate changes on export and import prices, on firms using imported inputs, on inflation, employment and growth, and on the trade balance — and why the effect is not automatic.
- The balance of payments: the current account and its components; the capital and financial accounts; the causes and consequences of a deficit or surplus, and the policies to correct one.

## 7. Development, population and poverty

- The meaning of economic development and economic welfare, and their difference from economic growth.
- Indicators of development: income per head, and the health, education and living-condition measures that income alone conceals; the HDI, the Gini coefficient and the Human Poverty Index, and their limitations.
- Comparing countries at different stages of development; the MEDC/LEDC terminology used in the teaching material, developed, developing and underdeveloped economies, and the limits of these labels; the reasons for low development.
- Differences in development **within** a country as well as between countries.
- Population: birth rate, death rate, net migration, natural increase; the causes of population change; the effects of migration.
- Population structure — age, sex, geographic and occupational distribution — and the dependency ratio; the consequences of an ageing and of a young population; urbanisation; optimum, over- and under-population; government population policies.
- Poverty: extreme, moderate, income and relative poverty; the groups most affected; the causes of poverty; the poverty cycle.
- Policies to reduce poverty and promote development: growth programmes, education, healthcare, infrastructure, investment, aid, trade and targeted support — assessed on access, quality, implementation capacity and the time before benefits arrive.
