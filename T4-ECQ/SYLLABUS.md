## Exam technique

### Timing

The paper is 180 minutes for 100 marks — about **1.8 minutes a mark**. A workable plan:

| Part | Marks | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Read the data question and choose your three structured questions | — | 10 minutes |
| Multiple choice | 20 | 20–25 minutes (about a minute each; bank the spare time) |
| Data response | 20 | about 36 minutes |
| Three structured questions | 3 × 20 | about **36 minutes each** — 108 minutes |
| Check | — | whatever is left |

Choose structured questions on the strength of the **(c) and (d)** parts, which carry 14 of the 20 marks — not on the easy (a) definition.

### Multiple choice

- **Cover the options and answer first.** Work out the answer from the stem, then find it — this stops a plausible distractor pulling you in.
- **Read every word of the stem.** Watch for *not*, *least*, *most likely*, *always*, and for whether a question is about a **shift** or a **movement along** a curve, **demand** or **quantity demanded**, **nominal** or **real**, **total** or **average**.
- **Eliminate.** Two options are usually clearly wrong; decide between the last two by the exact wording.
- **Sketch the diagram** in the margin for any question about price, quantity, taxes, exchange rates or costs — it takes ten seconds and settles most of them.
- **Do the arithmetic.** Calculation questions (PED, percentage changes, CPI, real GDP, costs, exchange-rate conversions) are common; write the formula and check the answer is sensible.
- **Never leave a blank** — there is no penalty for a wrong answer unless the paper says otherwise. Mark doubtful questions and return to them.

### Data response

The question gives you **unseen source material** — text plus tables or graphs about a real economy — and a sequence of part questions that rise in difficulty. In Cambridge IGCSE 0455 Paper 2 the compulsory question has several parts, typically:

| Typical part | Command | Marks (IGCSE scale) | What earns them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identify / state from the source | *identify*, *state*, *give* | 1–2 | a precise figure or fact **lifted from the data** |
| Calculate | *calculate* | 1–2 | the right answer **with units**; show the working |
| Explain | *explain* | 2–4 | a point **plus** its reason, linked to the case |
| Analyse — often with a diagram | *analyse*, *draw* | 4–5 | a full causal chain; a correctly labelled diagram |
| Discuss | *discuss* | 5–6 | both sides, developed, and a judgement |

The Pre-Q data question is worth 20 marks, so expect a shorter version of the same ladder.

**Method.**

1. **Read the questions first**, then the source, so you know what to look for. Underline figures and causes in the text.
2. **Read the data carefully.** For a table, check the **units** (\$m or \$bn, %, index numbers), the **time period** and the **base year**. For a graph, read the axis titles and scale before the line. Note whether figures are **levels** (GDP in \$bn) or **rates** (growth in %) — a falling growth rate still means GDP is rising, just more slowly.
3. **Describe trends precisely**: the overall direction, the highest and lowest points with their years and values, and any exception ("rose every year except 2020, when it fell from 4.1% to −1.4%").
4. **Calculate percentage changes** from the original value:
   $$\%\ \text{change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}}\times100$$
   Keep percentage **points** separate from per cent: unemployment rising from 4% to 5% is a rise of **1 percentage point** but **25%**.
5. **"Use the data" / "refer to the source"** marks are only earned by **quoting figures** from the source in your answer. If a question says *using the information*, every paragraph should contain a number or fact from it — answers that ignore the case are capped.
6. **Spot the economics in the case** — a fall in the exchange rate, a new tax, rising unemployment — and apply the theory to *that* country, firm or market.
7. **Finish on the discuss part** with both sides and a conclusion, using the source as evidence where you can.

### Structured (20-mark) questions

Each optional question opens with a short **stimulus** (a few lines of context) and has **four parts**. In Cambridge IGCSE 0455 the pattern is typically:

| Part | Typical command | Marks | What a full-mark answer needs | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | *identify*, *define*, *state* | 2 | a precise definition or two correct points — no essay | 2–3 minutes |
| **(b)** | *explain* | 4 | two points, each **stated and explained** (point + reason), or one point developed with an example | 6 minutes |
| **(c)** | *analyse* | 6 | a developed causal chain — each link explained — usually with a **labelled diagram**, applied to the stimulus | 10–11 minutes |
| **(d)** | *discuss* | 8 | **both sides**, each developed, and an **evaluative conclusion** | 15–16 minutes |

Treat the marks as a guide to length: roughly one developed sentence per mark.

**Command words** (Cambridge definitions):

| Command word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Identify** | name, select or recognise |
| **State** | express in clear terms |
| **Give** | produce an answer from a given source or from memory |
| **Define** | give the precise meaning — usually with a key term (for example "the next best alternative **forgone**") |
| **Describe** | state the points of a topic; give its characteristics and main features |
| **Calculate** | work out from given facts, figures or information — show the working and the unit |
| **Explain** | set out purposes or reasons; make the relationships between things clear; say why and/or how, supported with evidence |
| **Analyse** | examine in detail to show meaning, identify elements and the relationship between them |
| **Discuss** | write about issues or topics in depth, in a structured way |

**What "analyse" needs.** A logical chain of cause and effect in which **every link is explained**: *the Reserve Bank raises the OCR* → *borrowing costs rise* → *households cut spending on credit* → *demand falls* → *firms raise prices less* → *inflation falls*. Use connectives — "because", "which leads to", "so", "as a result". Where a diagram fits, **draw it and refer to it** ("price rises from $P_1$ to $P_2$"). A list of unexplained points scores little however long it is.

**What "discuss" needs.** Two developed sides — why it might, and why it might not — followed by a judgement. Point-based mark schemes typically cap each side (for example up to 5 marks for each side of an 8-mark discussion), so a one-sided answer cannot score full marks. The department's guidance adds that a **conclusion is required**: 1 mark for a limited conclusion, 2 for a strong one.

### Calculations and diagrams

**Calculations.** Write the formula, substitute, give the unit, and say what the answer means ("PED = 2, so demand is elastic"). Percentage changes are always divided by the **original** value.

**Diagrams.** Label both axes, every curve, every equilibrium and every shift — see [DIAGRAMS.md](DIAGRAMS.md). Then explain the diagram in words: the shift, then the movement along the other curve.

**Use the context.** Most questions carry a short case. Refer to its country, firm, product and figures in every paragraph — a mark scheme can cap an answer that never mentions the case (one revision question limits answers with no reference to cars to 4 of 6 marks).

### Long-answer (discuss) questions — the department's guidance

The teaching material describes long answers worth 8 or 10 marks — in the 20-mark structured format these are the **(d)** parts, and the discuss part of the data question. They are usually a statement or question with **two sides**, such as *Discuss whether small firms can compete successfully against large firms* or *Discuss whether governments should discourage car use*.

1. **Write formal paragraphs**, essay style — no bullet points, abbreviations, slang or personal statements such as "I think". **No introduction** is needed.
2. **Cover both sides.** Plan what the question is asking: (a) the reasons it is so, (b) the reasons it is not, (c) a judgement. Mark schemes typically allow up to 5 marks for each side, so a one-sided answer cannot score highly.
3. **Develop each point.** State the point, explain the mechanism, then the consequence, with an example. Use definite language where it is justified ("this **will** raise costs", "this is **likely** to…").
4. **Finish with an evaluative conclusion.** It is not a summary, a one-line recap or a repeat of what you wrote — the commonest weakness. It makes a real overall judgement and says **what the answer depends on**: elasticity, time lags, spare capacity, the size of the change, the state of the economy, the type of product. A detailed evaluative conclusion earns the top 1–2 marks; it is what lifts an answer to 7 or 8 out of 8. A good conclusion can also suggest a better alternative — for car use, promoting public transport rather than simply discouraging cars.
5. **Watch for "always".** *Discuss whether a low inflation rate always benefits an economy* or *whether monopolies are always harmful* — if there are two sides, it is almost never "always". Say so in the conclusion.

**What lifts a 7/10 answer higher:** definite statements rather than hedging; specific examples (countries, firms, industries); and the standard evaluation tools — **price elasticity** (a depreciation raises export revenue only if demand is elastic), **time lags** (contracts are fixed in the short run), and **capacity constraints** (factories at full capacity cannot produce more).

### Worked long answer — *Discuss whether demand for cars is likely to increase in the future.* [10]

**Why it might increase** (up to 5 marks): rising incomes, especially in emerging economies, let people switch from buses to cars; car prices may fall; technology (hybrid and electric cars) raises quality and availability; population growth creates more buyers; easier credit lets people buy on loans; effective advertising persuades more people to buy.

**Why it might not** (up to 5 marks): substitutes such as public transport may become cheaper; environmental concern may push people to walk or cycle; the price of complements may rise — petrol, parking, road tolls, congestion charges; governments may tax or restrict car use to cut external costs; a recession and rising unemployment would cut confidence; new forms of transport may appear.

**Evaluative conclusion** (up to 2 marks): for example — global demand is likely to rise because income growth in emerging economies outweighs falling demand in developed cities, but its composition will change towards electric cars; the outcome depends on fuel and battery costs, on how far governments push green policies, and on whether working from home keeps cutting commuting.

## 1. Scarcity, choice and economic systems

- The basic economic problem: unlimited wants against limited means (time, income, skill and knowledge); needs and wants; scarcity.
- Opportunity cost, and applying it to consumers, producers and governments.
- Producers and consumers; goods and services; free and economic goods; consumer and capital goods; natural, human and man-made resources.
- The factors of production — land, labour, capital, enterprise — and their rewards.
- Public goods, merit goods and demerit goods, and why merit goods are not public goods.
- Specialisation and the division of labour; derived demand; the law of diminishing returns.
- The production possibility curve: efficient, inefficient and unattainable points; movements along the curve and shifts of the curve; increasing and constant opportunity cost; capital goods against consumer goods.
- Economic systems — free market, command (planned), mixed and traditional — and how each answers what, how and for whom to produce; the private and public sectors.
- The role of the price mechanism in allocating resources: signalling, incentive and rationing functions.

## 2. The New Zealand economy

- Microeconomics and macroeconomics, and the decision makers in each.
- The four sectors of the economy (households, firms, government, overseas) and the circular flow; leakages and injections.
- NZ as a mixed economy; private and public sector ownership; state-owned enterprises; the post-1984 reforms.
- The structure of NZ output and exports: primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; dependence on primary exports and tourism; main trading partners.
- The institutions that make and measure policy: the government and Budget, the Treasury, Inland Revenue, the Reserve Bank and the OCR, Stats NZ (CPI, HLFS, GDP), local councils.
- The role of government locally, nationally and internationally.

## 3. Money and exchange

- Barter and its problems; the characteristics and functions of money; the money supply.
- Spending, saving and borrowing, and the factors that influence them; disposable income; interest rates and the real return.
- The money market: commercial banks, investment banks, credit unions and the central bank; how banks make a profit; why banks matter to consumers, producers and government.
- Classifying firms by sector (primary, secondary, tertiary) and by ownership (private, public); why small firms survive and the challenges they face.
- What determines the demand for factors of production.

## 4. Demand, supply and the market

- Demand and quantity demanded; individual and market demand; demand schedules and ceteris paribus; the law of demand and the demand curve.
- The **conditions of demand** (what shifts the curve): tastes and fashion, disposable income, the price of substitutes and complements, population, advertising and events, expectations.
- Normal, inferior, luxury, substitute and complementary goods.
- Supply and quantity supplied; market supply; the law of supply and the supply curve; related goods; price compared with cost.
- The **conditions of supply** (what shifts the curve): costs of production, productivity and technology, indirect taxes, subsidies, tariffs, the number of firms, good and bad seasons, the price of related goods; environmental, legal, political and cultural influences.
- The distinction between a **movement along** a curve (a change in quantity demanded or supplied, caused by the good's own price) and a **shift** of a curve (a change in demand or supply, caused by anything else).
- Market equilibrium: equilibrium price and quantity; excess demand and excess supply; how price adjusts to clear the market.
- The effects of a shift in demand or supply on equilibrium price and quantity; the ambiguity when both shift together.
- Individual and market demand and supply (aggregation).
- Pricing strategies used by firms, and the factors that influence the price set.

## 5. Elasticity, market failure and market structures

- Price elasticity of demand: calculation, interpretation, elastic and inelastic demand, the determinants of PED, the effect of a price change on total revenue (by calculation and on a diagram), and its significance for consumers, producers and government. Income elasticity of demand as an extension.
- Price elasticity of supply: calculation, the determinants (time, spare resources, stocks, production period, factor mobility, barriers to entry), elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic supply.
- The usefulness of elasticity to firms and to government (especially indirect taxation).
- Market failure: its meaning; complete and partial failure; its causes — public goods, information failure, imperfect market structures, factor immobility, externalities and inequity.
- External costs and external benefits of production and consumption; private, external and social costs and benefits.
- Merit and demerit goods; public goods and the free-rider problem; information failure.
- The consequences of market failure: over-consumption of demerit goods and goods with external costs, under-consumption of merit goods and goods with external benefits.
- Government microeconomic intervention: indirect taxes and their incidence, subsidies, maximum and minimum prices (in product, labour and foreign exchange markets) — each with its diagram; regulation, education and advertising, direct provision, privatisation and nationalisation, tradable permits — with the limits of each.
- Market structures: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly and duopoly, monopoly and monopsony; price and non-price competition; natural and artificial barriers to entry; the effects of market power on price, choice, quality and innovation.

## 6. Firms, costs and labour markets

- Production and productivity; the factors that affect labour productivity.
- Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production.
- Types of business: sole trader, partnership, private and public limited company, franchise, co-operative; unlimited and limited liability.
- Classifying firms by sector (primary, secondary, tertiary) and by ownership (private, public); why small firms survive and the challenges they face.
- What determines the demand for factors of production.
- Measuring business size, the advantages of small and large firms, and internal and external growth (horizontal, vertical and conglomerate integration), with the advantages and disadvantages of each type of merger.
- Economies and diseconomies of scale: internal and external.
- Costs: fixed, variable, direct, indirect, total, average fixed, average variable and average total cost, and their curves; total and average revenue; profit; contribution and the break-even chart.
- Business aims and objectives: mission statements; SMART objectives; survival, profit maximisation and satisficing, growth, market share, sales revenue, shareholder value, social objectives; what determines them.
- Choosing an occupation: wage and non-wage factors; methods of payment; changes in earnings over a lifetime.
- The demand for labour as a **derived** demand; the supply of labour and its determinants; the labour force participation rate.
- Wage determination; the causes of wage differentials between and within occupations, including discrimination; public- and private-sector pay.
- The minimum wage and the role of government in labour markets.
- Trade unions and collective bargaining; what makes a union strong; industrial action; the effects on wages, employment and the economy.

## 7. Macroeconomics and policy

- The circular flow of income; the government as producer and employer.
- Government macroeconomic objectives: growth, employment, price stability, balance of payments stability, reducing poverty and inequality, protecting the environment — and the criteria (targets) set for each.
- GDP and its components; real GDP and real GDP per head; **nominal vs real** and why the distinction matters; the limitations of GDP as a measure of welfare.
- Economic growth: measurement by real GDP; recession (two successive quarters of falling real GDP) and the PPC; its causes, benefits and costs; policies to promote it; the difference between growth and development.
- The economic cycle: growth, boom, recession, slump and recovery, and the effect on firms and households; the shapes of recession.
- Inflation: definition, measurement by the CPI (a weighted basket), demand-pull, cost-push and imported causes, and the consequences for consumers, workers, savers, lenders, borrowers and firms. Hyperinflation, deflation (demand-side and supply-side) and disinflation. Policies to control inflation and deflation.
- Employment and unemployment: the claimant count and the labour force survey (NZ's HLFS) and the unemployment rate formula; changing patterns of employment; cyclical, structural, frictional and seasonal unemployment; full- and part-time work; the consequences and the policies.
- Fiscal policy: the government budget; the reasons for taxation, the principles of a good tax, direct and indirect taxes, progressive, proportional and regressive taxes, and the impact of taxation; government spending; calculating a budget deficit or surplus.
- Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate; how changes transmit to spending, saving and investment.
- Supply-side policy: education and training, tax incentives, subsidies, deregulation, competition policy, removing trade barriers, privatisation.
- Which policy affects which objective; conflicts between objectives (including full employment against stable prices, growth against the balance of payments, full employment against the balance of payments), and the significance of the economy's starting position.

## 8. International trade and exchange rates

- Globalisation and its benefits and drawbacks for consumers, producers and governments.
- Specialisation and the gains from trade; absolute and comparative advantage; why countries trade.
- Multinational companies and their effects on home and host countries.
- The benefits and risks of international trade, including dependence on overseas markets and the adjustment costs borne by particular workers and regions.
- Free trade and protectionism: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes and administrative barriers; the arguments for protection (infant and sunset industries, over-specialisation, trade imbalances, employment, dumping, strategic industries) and the counter-arguments, including retaliation; free trade agreements.
- Exchange rates: conversions; how they are determined by demand and supply on the foreign exchange market (with the diagram); the causes of appreciation and depreciation; floating, fixed and managed systems and their advantages and disadvantages.
- The effect of exchange-rate changes on export and import prices, on firms using imported inputs, on inflation, employment and growth, and on the trade balance — and why the effect is not automatic.
- The balance of payments: the current account and its components; the capital and financial accounts; the causes and consequences of a deficit or surplus, and the policies to correct one.

## 9. Development, population and poverty

- The meaning of economic development and economic welfare, and their difference from economic growth.
- Indicators of development: real GDP per head and the HDI (components, advantages and disadvantages), and the health, education and living-condition measures that income alone conceals; the Gini coefficient (with the Lorenz curve as an extension), the Human Poverty Index and its successor the Multidimensional Poverty Index.
- Comparing countries at different stages of development; the MEDC/LEDC terminology used in the teaching material, developed, developing and underdeveloped economies, and the limits of these labels; the causes and impacts of differences in income, productivity, population growth, sector structure, saving and investment, education and healthcare.
- Differences in development **within** a country as well as between countries.
- Population: birth rate, death rate, net migration, natural increase; the causes of population change; the effects of migration.
- Population structure — age, sex, geographic and occupational distribution — interpreting population pyramids, and the dependency ratio; the effects of changes in population size and structure; urbanisation; optimum, over- and under-population; government population policies.
- Poverty: extreme (absolute), moderate, income and relative poverty; the groups most affected; the causes of poverty, including unemployment, low wages, illness and age; the poverty cycle; policies including growth, education, state benefits, progressive taxation and a minimum wage.
- Policies to reduce poverty and promote development: growth programmes, education, healthcare, infrastructure, investment, aid, trade and targeted support — assessed on access, quality, implementation capacity and the time before benefits arrive.
