# Firms, costs and labour markets

## Production and productivity

These two words are not interchangeable, and the exam tests the difference.

| | Production | Productivity |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | the **value of output** of goods and services (for a whole economy, GDP) | the **efficiency** of the factors of production |
| Example | total output | output per person employed per period |
| Rises when | more is produced, by any means | the *same* inputs produce more |

$$\text{labour productivity} = \frac{\text{total output}}{\text{number of workers}}$$

**An increase in production does not always mean an increase in productivity.** If output doubles because twice as many workers were hired, output per worker is unchanged. Say which one the figures show.

**What affects labour productivity:** the degree of competition in the market; advances in technology; specialisation and division of labour; business investment in new capital; apprenticeships and training; the quality of management; the quality of infrastructure; and the strength of demand for the product.

**Why it matters.** Falling productivity raises firms' costs. Rising productivity lets firms pay higher wages **and** earn higher profits, and in the long run it is a major determinant of economic growth and of inflation. Specialisation and the division of labour are covered in [Scarcity, choice and economic systems](../notes/foundations.md).

## Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production

**Labour-intensive** production uses **more labour relative to capital**; **capital-intensive** production uses **more capital relative to labour**. Intensity is about the **ratio**, not the absolute size of either.

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | fruit farming, strawberry picking, tea-leaf farming, hotels, restaurants, hairdressers, clothing shops, handmade shoes | oil extraction and refining, IT, car manufacturing, computer making, panelbeaters, transport infrastructure |
| Cost structure | higher labour costs, lower capital costs; costs mainly **variable**, so a **lower break-even output** | higher capital costs, lower labour costs; costs mainly **fixed**, so a **higher break-even output** |
| Benefits from | access to low-cost labour | access to low-cost, long-term finance |
| Advantages | low unit costs where wages are low; labour is flexible (multi-skilling, training); workers at the heart of production drive continuous improvement | more economies of scale; potentially much higher productivity; better quality and speed; lower labour costs |
| Disadvantages | risk of poor employer–employee relations; high cost if staff turnover is high; continual training costs | huge investment needed; machines can become obsolete; workers may resist change |

The same industry can switch over time. Coal mining in the 1880s was labour-intensive — many miners carrying, sorting and managing, with few tools. Coal mining today is capital-intensive, using advanced machinery relative to the number of workers.

## Types of business organisation

Sole traders, partnerships and limited companies are mostly in the **private sector**, which aims at **profit**; the **public sector** aims to **provide a service**.

| Form | Ownership | Liability | Finance | Key limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | one person, who has sole control and keeps all the profit | **unlimited** | personal savings, bank loan, friends and family | no continuity — if the owner dies the business ends |
| **Partnership** | two or more people sharing investment and usually responsibility | **unlimited** for all partners | partners' capital | profits and decisions shared; ends if a partner leaves; cannot sell shares |
| **Private limited company (Ltd)** | shareholders, often family and friends; directors usually shareholders | **limited** | shares sold **privately**; every shareholder must agree to a sale | cannot advertise shares to the public; more legal procedure; accounts are publicly available |
| **Public limited company (plc)** | shareholders — anyone | **limited** | shares bought and sold **openly on the stock exchange** | very expensive to set up; control can be lost; divorce of ownership and control |
| **Franchise** | franchisee owns the outlet; franchisor owns the brand | depends on the franchisee's own legal form | start-up fee plus a percentage of revenue or profit to the franchisor | franchisee must follow the franchisor's methods |
| **Co-operative** | its members, run democratically | limited | members' subscriptions and retained surplus | slower decisions; limited access to outside capital |

**Unlimited liability** — if the business gets into financial trouble the owner is **personally responsible** for its debts and may lose personal possessions to repay them. This risk puts people off starting a business.

**Limited liability** depends on **separate legal identity**: the company is a legal person distinct from its owners, so a shareholder can lose **only what they invested**.

### Sole traders and partnerships

| Sole trader advantages | Sole trader disadvantages |
|---|---|
| easy to set up, little paperwork | unlimited liability |
| total control; keeps all the profit | must run every aspect alone; long hours; can be lonely |
| chooses own hours and holidays; can respond quickly | limited access to finance |
| close, personal relationships with customers and staff | competition from bigger businesses |
| | no continuity |

| Partnership advantages | Partnership disadvantages |
|---|---|
| partners bring skills and can specialise | unlimited liability for every partner |
| shared decision-making and shared losses | profits are shared; disagreements |
| more finance than a sole trader | no continuity — ends if any partner leaves |
| fewer legal requirements than a company | cannot sell shares; less freedom than a sole trader |

A **Deed of Partnership** sets out **voting rights**, each partner's **responsibilities and authority**, and the **profit and loss split**. It is **not a legal requirement**; without one, everything is assumed to be shared **equally**.

### Limited companies

A limited company is divided into **shares**. A shareholder is a part-owner with the right to vote and to a share of the profits. Shareholders elect **directors** at the **Annual General Meeting (AGM)** to run the company on their behalf, and can vote them off at the next AGM. To form a company you register with the **Companies Office** and draw up two documents:

| Document | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Memorandum of Association** | basic details — name and address, objectives, the shareholders' liability, the number of shares allowed |
| **Articles of Association** | internal running — shareholder rights, how directors are appointed, how often meetings are held, how accounts are prepared |

| Why become an Ltd | Why not |
|---|---|
| limited liability | profits shared among all shareholders |
| more capital from more shareholders | more legal procedure, time and cost |
| control kept — outsiders cannot buy shares | cannot sell shares to the public, so less capital than a plc |
| continuity — shares pass to a new owner | financial information is publicly available |
| taxed at the company rate (28% in NZ) rather than personal income-tax rates | selling shares is slow, since all shareholders must agree |
| looks more professional to banks and suppliers | less freedom than a sole trader or partnership |

**Plcs** are generally the biggest companies; share values rise and fall with the firm's performance and outside events. They pay **dividends** — a payment to shareholders, usually in proportion to the shares held, paid out of after-tax profit, usually at the year-end (sometimes as an interim or quarterly dividend). Dividends are **not** a legal requirement.

| Plc advantages | Plc disadvantages |
|---|---|
| huge sums can be raised, since anyone can buy shares | set-up costs can run into millions |
| economies of scale, such as bulk buying | control can be lost — anyone can buy shares |
| may dominate its market | all financial information is public |
| banks lend more readily | little personal relationship with customers; slow to adapt |
| shareholder pressure pushes managers to perform | company law constrains it; **divorce of ownership and control** — owners and managers may want different things |

A **plc is still a private-sector business** — "public" means its shares are sold to the public, not that the state owns it.

### Franchises

A **franchise** is when the owner of a business (the **franchisor**) sells another person or company (the **franchisee**) the right to use its name, logo, products and methods in return for a fee. Remember: franchis**o**r = the **o**riginal business; franchis**ee** = the **e**xpanding new outlet.

The franchisor provides staff training, marketing support and a tried-and-trusted business model, brand and product. In return the franchisee pays a **start-up fee** (covering initial marketing, training and premises) and a **percentage of revenue or profit**.

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Franchisor** | quick, cheap expansion without bank borrowing — franchisees pay to grow the brand; gains the skills of franchisees | hands control of its reputation to someone else; the franchisee keeps the profit from the outlet; the franchisee may lack the skills |
| **Franchisee** | lower risk — a proven product; predictable costs; ongoing support | pays a start-up fee and a share of revenue, cutting profit; must do things the franchisor's way |

The world's largest franchises by sales include McDonald's, 7-Eleven, Burger King, KFC and Subway.

## The stock exchange

The **stock exchange** (stock market) is where shares and government bonds are bought and sold. New Zealand's is the **NZX**; others include the New York, London and Tokyo exchanges. Both Ltds and plcs sell shares to raise finance, but **only plcs** can sell shares on the stock exchange. The total value of a company's issued shares is its **market capitalisation**.

$$\text{market capitalisation} = \text{share price} \times \text{number of shares issued}$$

| Function | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Raising share capital** | a firm sells shares openly and can raise very large sums; going public for the first time is an **initial public offering (IPO)** — an oversubscribed IPO drives the share price up |
| **Facilitating growth** | existing plcs issue more shares to fund expansion; takeovers and mergers (including hostile ones) happen by buying shares |
| **Selling government bonds** | governments borrow by selling bonds, which can then be resold on the exchange |
| **Price mechanism for shares** | share prices are set by supply and demand, driven by company performance, confidence and rumour; a rising market is **bullish**, a falling one **bearish** |
| **Safety of transactions** | trading is regulated and prices are published, which builds confidence |

Capital raised by selling shares is **permanent capital**: the firm never repays it. A shareholder who wants money back must sell the shares to someone else.

| Type of stock | Return | Vote |
|---|---|---|
| **Preference shares** | a **fixed** percentage dividend, paid **before** other shareholders | **no** vote at the AGM |
| **Ordinary shares** | a dividend from whatever profit remains — possibly nothing | **one vote per share**; the majority shareholder can control policy and the board |
| **Government bonds** | fixed interest, and the face value repaid at **maturity** | no ownership, no vote |

People buy shares through a **broker**, through a managed **fund** that pools many investors' money, or through an online platform. A well-functioning stock exchange supports **business and consumer confidence**, which encourages investment and spending.

## Measuring business size

No single measure settles it, so use **at least two or three together**. Customers, managers, owners, banks and the government all want to know a firm's size.

| Measure | Meaning | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Number of employees** | simplest | a capital-intensive firm may be the biggest in its industry with few staff |
| **Sales revenue** | total sales in a period | poor across industries — one firm sells a few high-value items, another many cheap ones |
| **Capital employed** | total long-term finance (machinery, equipment, property) | only comparable within one industry, since some are more capital-intensive |
| **Value of output** | the value of total production | a tiny firm making very expensive products looks large |
| **Market share** | the firm's sales as a percentage of the whole market | needs a clear definition of the market |
| **Market capitalisation** | total value of shares | only works for companies with traded shares |

$$\text{market share} = \frac{\text{the firm's sales}}{\text{total sales in the market}}\times100$$

*Worked example.* Four stores:

| Store | Employees | Capital employed | Sales revenue | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 300 | \$150m | \$250m | \$100m |
| B | 800 | \$450m | \$1300m | \$600m |
| C | 1200 | \$700m | \$1000m | \$550m |
| D | 1500 | \$400m | \$400m | \$120m |

D employs the most people but has low capital, sales and profit; B has the highest sales and profit; C has the most capital employed and is second on every other measure. Taking the measures together, the ranking is **C, B, D, A** — and you should explain that a different weighting of the measures could change it.

| Small business advantages | Small business disadvantages |
|---|---|
| managed by the owner | limited access to finance |
| adapts easily to customer needs | the manager must do every job without specialists |
| personal service; informal culture staff like | higher risk — cannot spread risk across products |
| | no real economies of scale |

| Large business advantages | Large business disadvantages |
|---|---|
| can afford specialist managers and in-depth market research | hard to manage across many sites and countries |
| economies of scale; can set low prices others must follow | large-scale production can bring large costs |
| several sources of finance | slow decision-making |
| spreads risk over several products and markets | conflict between owners and managers |

## Business growth

**Internal (organic) growth** — expanding from within, usually funded by retained profit: opening another outlet, adding capacity with new capital and technology, launching new products, finding new (export) markets, or growing the customer base through marketing.

**External growth** — a **merger** (two or more businesses mutually join to form a new one) or a **takeover** (one business buys another, which comes under its name and control).

| Type of integration | Meaning | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | same industry, **same stage** of production | less competition, more market share, more economies of scale |
| **Vertical backwards** | towards the **raw materials** (a fast-food chain buys a cattle farm) | guaranteed supply; can stop supplying competitors |
| **Vertical forwards** | towards the **end consumer** (a cattle farm buys the fast-food chain) | a guaranteed outlet; the retailer's profit and expertise; can keep competitors out of the shop |
| **Conglomerate** | a **completely unrelated** industry | spreads the risk of failure; new skills and ideas |

Whether to grow depends on the **owner's objective**. An owner running a business for independence, personal service and control may rationally stay small.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** — as a business grows, its **average cost of production falls**. Not "total cost falls" — total cost almost always rises with output. Lower average cost lets a firm earn higher profit or charge more competitive prices and win customers from rivals.

**Internal economies** come from decisions **within** the firm:

| Internal economy | How it lowers average cost |
|---|---|
| **Purchasing** | buying in bulk at a discount; a larger firm has more storage and output |
| **Marketing** | a large campaign's cost is spread over more units; branded company vehicles advertise as they travel |
| **Financial** | lenders see large firms as less risky and offer cheaper interest |
| **Technical** | larger budgets buy the latest specialised equipment, raising efficiency and productivity |
| **Managerial** | specialist managers can be employed |
| **Risk-bearing** | a wider product range, more customers and more locations spread the risk of failure |

**External economies** arise **outside the firm but within its industry**, so all firms in it gain:

| External economy | Example |
|---|---|
| **Access to skilled workers** | a new pilot-training school benefits every airline |
| **Ancillary firms** | specialist suppliers (a marketing agency) locate nearby and serve the firm cheaply |
| **Joint marketing benefits** | firms clustered together share a reputation and customer base |
| **Shared infrastructure** | new roads, bridges or a power station cut every firm's costs |

**Diseconomies of scale** — the business grows and its **average cost rises** because it has become too large to run efficiently.

| Diseconomy | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Communication** | many sites; language, cultural and time-zone barriers |
| **Management** | many managers with different styles who disagree about direction |
| **Labour** | workers bored by repetitive tasks on machines |
| **Agglomeration** | too many merged or acquired businesses, at too many stages, to understand and coordinate |

*Worked example.* A small doughnut business expands and buys its stock in bulk; its supplier offers a discount for the larger order, so the cost per doughnut falls — a **purchasing economy of scale**.

## Costs, revenue and profit

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Direct cost** | clearly linked to production — raw materials |
| **Indirect cost** | not directly linked to production — the telephone bill |
| **Fixed cost (FC)** | does not change with output — rent, insurance, market research, advertising, senior management salaries |
| **Variable cost (VC)** | changes with output — raw materials, components, ingredients, hourly wages, delivery and transport of ingredients |
| **Total cost** | $TC = FC + VC$ — all the costs the business incurs |
| **Average (unit) cost** | $AC = TC \div \text{total output}$ |
| **Marginal cost** | the extra cost of producing one more unit |
| **Total revenue** | $TR = \text{price per unit} \times \text{quantity sold}$ |
| **Average revenue** | $AR = TR \div \text{units sold}$ |
| **Profit** | $\text{profit} = TR - TC$ |
| **Contribution per unit** | $\text{selling price} - \text{variable cost per unit}$ |
| **Break-even output** | $\text{fixed costs} \div \text{contribution per unit}$ |

*Average cost.* If making 10 000 cars costs \$30m in total, average cost is $30\,000\,000/10\,000 = \$3000$ per car — the car must sell for more than \$3000 to make a profit.

*Worked example — a magazine.* Each copy sells for \$5. Printing-machine hire is \$2400 a month and factory rent \$1600, so FC = \$4000. Materials and wages are \$3 a copy.

| Copies | FC | VC | TC | AC | TR | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | \$4000 | \$0 | \$4000 | — | \$0 | −\$4000 |
| 1000 | \$4000 | \$3000 | \$7000 | \$7.00 | \$5000 | −\$2000 |
| 2000 | \$4000 | \$6000 | \$10 000 | \$5.00 | \$10 000 | **\$0 — break-even** |
| 3000 | \$4000 | \$9000 | \$13 000 | \$4.33 | \$15 000 | \$2000 |
| 4000 | \$4000 | \$12 000 | \$16 000 | \$4.00 | \$20 000 | \$4000 |
| 5000 | \$4000 | \$15 000 | \$19 000 | \$3.80 | \$25 000 | \$6000 |
| 6000 | \$4000 | \$18 000 | \$22 000 | \$3.67 | \$30 000 | \$8000 |
| 8000 | \$4000 | \$24 000 | \$28 000 | \$3.50 | \$40 000 | \$12 000 |

Contribution per copy is \$5 − \$3 = \$2, so break-even output is $4000 \div 2 = 2000$ copies — matching the table. Average cost falls as output rises because the fixed cost is spread over more copies.

**Average cost is normally U-shaped.** It falls at first as fixed costs are spread and economies of scale appear; beyond some output it rises — a car firm expanding from 8000 to 10 000 cars may not find skilled workers and must hire and train unskilled ones, slowing production and raising cost per car. **Average revenue usually falls as output rises**, because to sell more units the firm must cut the price.

Three errors to avoid: revenue is **not** profit, average cost is **not** total cost, and breaking even is **not** making a profit.

## Business aims and objectives

Objectives give direction, a target, motivation and a way to assess performance. Every strategy should be built around an objective, and objectives are reviewed constantly — **a change of objective requires a change of strategy**.

A **mission statement** is a general statement of the business's core aims, worded to motivate staff and interest outsiders (a search company: "to organise the world's information and make it universally accessible"). It informs people quickly, can motivate and guide behaviour — but is often vague, can look like a PR exercise, and is impossible to agree or disagree with.

**Corporate objectives** add the detail mission statements lack. A good objective is **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-specific.

| Objective | Note |
|---|---|
| **Survival** | usually the first aim of a new firm, and of any firm in a recession; nothing else is possible without it |
| **Profit maximisation** | as much revenue as possible at the lowest possible cost |
| **Profit satisficing** | enough profit, but profit is not the only focus |
| **Growth** | measured by value, volume or outlets; growing too fast risks diseconomies of scale |
| **Increasing market share** | linked to growth; a market leader has room to negotiate margins |
| **Maximising sales revenue** | as many sales as possible, or a target amount |
| **Maximising shareholder value** | limited companies only |
| **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** | ethical and environmental aims |

**What determines a firm's objectives:** its **corporate culture** ("the way things are done"); its **legal structure** (a sole trader may be happy satisficing; an Ltd may want to grow; a plc may target market share or shareholder value); whether it is in the **public or private sector** (public-sector bodies measure service, such as waiting times); and **how long it has been in business** (new firms aim to survive, established ones to grow).

Growth can lead to profit maximisation in the long term: a bigger firm gains economies of scale (lower average cost, so higher profit at the same price), a bigger market share and customer base (more revenue), and more market power (it may be able to raise prices).

## Choosing an occupation and methods of payment

People choose a job on **wage and non-wage factors**. Pay matters, but so do: the **level of challenge**; **career prospects**; **danger**; the **length of training and education** required; the **experience** required; **personal satisfaction** (some accept lower pay for fulfilling work); and **recognition** and status.

| Method of payment | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Wage** | paid by the hour; common in lower-paid jobs; a **variable** cost for the firm |
| **Salary** | an annual figure paid fortnightly or monthly; common in skilled full-time jobs; a **fixed** cost |
| **Piece rate** | paid per unit produced (manufacturing, fruit picking) |
| **Commission** | a percentage of the value of sales made (real-estate agents, car sales) |
| **Bonus** | a lump sum on top of pay, usually for good performance |
| **Performance-related pay** | varies with the success of the business |
| **Share options** | shares in the business, giving an incentive to earn dividends and capital gains |
| **Fringe benefits** | non-monetary perks — company car, gym, car park, laptop, phone |

**Earnings over a lifetime** are low at first (little training, experience or qualification), rise through early and middle age as skills, experience and promotion add value (and as housing and family costs push people to work more), may stay high or fall as people approach retirement and work part-time, and drop at retirement, when people rely on pensions, savings and family. Education shapes the path: a doctor earns almost nothing for years in training but far more later; an apprentice builder earns straight away. Having children and moving location also change earnings.

## The demand for and supply of labour

**Wages are set by the demand for labour against the supply of workers.** A neurosurgeon is very highly paid because demand is high and the supply of neurosurgeons is very low.

**The demand for labour is a *derived* demand** — labour is wanted for the goods and services it produces, not for itself. Bakers are demanded for the bread they bake. The demand curve slopes **downward**: as wages rise from $W$ to $W_1$ the number employed falls from $N$ to $N_1$, because workers are **more affordable at lower wages**.

| Shifts demand for labour **right** | Shifts it **left** |
|---|---|
| higher total demand in the economy (a boom) | a recession |
| higher labour productivity (motivation, training) | higher non-wage employment costs (KiwiSaver contributions, sick and annual leave, insurance) |
| capital becoming relatively dearer | capital becoming relatively cheaper through new technology |
| population changes raising demand for that type of labour | a rise in the cost of labour itself |

The **labour supply** is people of working age who are **both willing and able** to work; it excludes those in full-time education and those who choose not to work. The supply curve slopes **upward**: a higher wage attracts more workers and raises the **opportunity cost of not working**.

It can **bend backwards**: a builder works 35 hours at \$7 an hour (\$245), 40 hours at \$10 (\$400), but only 32 hours at \$15 — still earning more (\$480) for less time, because he now values leisure more.

| Shifts labour supply **right** | Shifts it **left** |
|---|---|
| population growth — higher birth rate, lower death rate, higher net migration | net emigration; falling population |
| a higher retirement (superannuation) age | a higher school leaving age |
| more provision of the relevant education and training | education dropped (stop teaching accounting → fewer accountants) |
| a higher participation rate | worse net benefits of the job (no promotion prospects, fewer holidays) |

**The equilibrium wage** is where the demand for labour equals the supply of labour. If the wage is above or below it, market forces push it back; a **shift** in either curve creates a new equilibrium wage and level of employment. The diagram is in [the diagrams page](../DIAGRAMS.md).

## Labour force participation rate

The **labour force** is the part of the working-age population that is **employed or unemployed and looking for work**. The **working-age population** is non-institutionalised people aged 15 and over.

$$\text{LFPR} = \frac{\text{labour force}}{\text{working-age population}}\times100$$

*Worked example.* Country A: labour force 35 million, working-age population 50 million → $35/50\times100 = 70\%$. Country B: $4.5/7\times100 \approx 64\%$. The total population and the number employed are not used.

**Why the LFPR varies:** how easy it is to live on welfare benefits; the changing social climate (more women working and delaying families); demographics (an older population such as Japan's has a lower rate); cultural attitudes to women working; and geographical mobility (willing to work, but unable to move to where the jobs are). NZ men's participation is higher than women's.

## Wage differentials

**Wage differentials** are differences in pay between occupations and employees.

| Why pay differs **between** occupations | Why pay differs **within** the same occupation |
|---|---|
| abilities and qualifications required | regional differences in labour demand and supply |
| unsociable hours and danger | experience and length of service |
| job satisfaction — pleasant work can pay less | local pay agreements |
| lack of information about jobs | different non-monetary rewards |
| labour immobility | **discrimination** by sex, race, religion or age |

**Public against private sector.** Governments compete with private firms for similar workers but often pay less: there is no profit motive to generate revenue; higher pay means higher taxes; and public-sector jobs are traditionally more secure, which is part of the reward.

## Minimum wage and the role of government

A **national minimum wage** is the legal minimum a firm may pay. It is introduced when the equilibrium wage is below **subsistence** (workers cannot afford basic needs), to stop **exploitation** (including of migrant workers), and to **reduce income inequality**.

If the labour market is in equilibrium at $W_1$, $N_1$ and a minimum wage $W_2$ is set above it, more workers offer themselves ($N_2$, read from supply) but firms want fewer ($N_3$, read from demand). The minimum wage **raises the pay of those still in work but can increase unemployment**.

| Positive effects | Negative effects |
|---|---|
| a fair wage; workers not exploited | can cause unemployment |
| a stronger incentive for the unemployed to seek work | workers just above the minimum demand rises to keep their differential |
| low earners spend more, raising demand and possibly creating jobs | firms switch to capital-intensive methods |
| | higher costs, which may be passed on as higher prices |

The **living wage** is a voluntary hourly rate, calculated independently each year, that a worker needs to pay for necessities and take part in the community.

**The government's role in labour markets:** regulate restrictive practices — a dominant employer may hold wages down, and a union controlling most of the supply of a type of worker may push wages up without a rise in productivity; reduce unemployment through retraining; and outlaw **discrimination** in hiring and pay (the "glass ceiling" describes equally qualified women and minority workers being paid less or blocked from promotion).

## Trade unions and collective bargaining

Businesses want to maximise profit and sales and minimise costs; employees want higher wages, safe conditions, good terms and job security. A pay rise is a cost rise — so the two conflict.

A **trade union** is an organisation that aims to improve its members' conditions and provides support and legal services when needed. **Collective bargaining** is negotiation by the union on behalf of all members, rather than worker by worker.

**Roles:** bargaining for better pay, holidays, breaks and health and safety; legal advice; support for members made redundant; financial support for those unfairly dismissed; pressuring governments for favourable laws. Members elect a **worker representative**, who negotiates with management, recruits members and collects subscriptions, passes on union decisions and takes complaints to management.

| Type of union | Who it represents |
|---|---|
| **Craft** | the oldest type — workers with one particular skill (printers) |
| **Industrial** | all workers in one industry, whatever their skill |
| **General** | anyone, regardless of industry |
| **White-collar** | professional, administrative and clerical workers (nurses, teachers) |

A pay claim usually rests on one of four grounds: the **cost of living** has risen faster than pay; **comparable occupations** earn more; **profits** in the industry are high and should be shared; or **productivity** has risen.

**Benefits to a business:** negotiating with one union is cheaper and quicker than with every employee; the union is a channel of communication; and it can be a supportive partner through major change.

| Industrial action (most to least severe) | Effect on the firm | Effect on employees |
|---|---|---|
| **Strike** — refusing to work | production stops; sales may fall | may not be paid |
| **Sit-in** — occupying the workplace without working | production stops | may not be paid |
| **Go-slow** — working deliberately slowly | productivity and efficiency drop; sales may suffer | morale falls further |
| **Work-to-rule** — doing only what the contract requires | loses goodwill and extra effort; service quality and brand image may suffer | cannot be disciplined, since they meet their contract |

**Effects of strikes on the economy:** lost output and productivity, slower growth, higher costs and lower profits, possibly higher prices and unemployment, fewer exports (worsening the current account), less investment by multinationals, and firms replacing workers with machines.

| Stronger unions may benefit the economy | Stronger unions may harm it |
|---|---|
| higher wages raise living standards (better healthcare and education for families) | higher costs cut profits and may cause cost-push inflation and a **wage–price spiral** |
| better conditions and protection from discrimination may raise productivity, export competitiveness and growth | output may fall and unemployment rise |
| better communication and cheaper negotiation; may promote training | more industrial action disrupts production, loses orders and investor confidence |
| | firms find it harder to adapt to changing markets |

The judgement depends on the union's **starting strength** and how much it changes, whether wage rises are matched by **productivity**, the **elasticity of demand** for the product, and the state of the labour market. Very weak unions leave workers exposed; very strong ones can price members out of work.
