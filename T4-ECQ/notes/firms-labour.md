# Firms, costs and labour markets

## Production and productivity

These two words are not interchangeable, and the exam tests the difference.

| | Production | Productivity |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | the **value or volume of output** | the **efficiency** of the factors of production |
| Example | total output of goods and services | output per worker per period |
| Rises when | more is produced, by any means | the *same* inputs produce more |

$$\text{labour productivity} = \frac{\text{total output}}{\text{number of workers}}$$

**An increase in production does not prove an increase in productivity.** If output doubles because twice as many workers were hired, output per worker is unchanged. Say which one the figures show.

What raises labour productivity: training and apprenticeships, investment in new capital, better technology, division of labour, better quality of management, better infrastructure, stronger competitive pressure. Higher productivity lowers unit costs, which allows higher wages *and* higher profits — and in the long run it is the main determinant of economic growth.

## Division of labour and specialisation

**Division of labour** splits production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker; the worker **specialises**.

| Gains | Losses |
|---|---|
| workers become expert at one task | repetitive work lowers motivation |
| no time lost switching between tasks | workers lose flexibility and broader skills |
| training is cheaper and quicker | the whole line stops if one stage fails |
| allows use of specialised capital equipment | over-dependence on a narrow skill if the industry declines |

## Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Uses relatively more | labour | capital equipment |
| Typical examples | fruit picking, hotels, restaurants, hairdressing | oil refining, car assembly, large-scale IT |
| Cost structure | mostly **variable** costs | mostly **fixed** costs |
| Break-even output | lower | higher |
| Benefits from | access to low-cost, flexible labour | access to cheap long-term finance |
| Strengths | flexible, multi-skilling, continuous improvement from the shop floor | economies of scale, speed, consistent quality, low labour cost |
| Weaknesses | staff turnover, industrial relations, continual training cost | huge initial investment, obsolescence risk, resistance to change |

The same industry can switch over time: mining was labour-intensive before mechanisation and is capital-intensive after it. Intensity is about the **ratio** of capital to labour, not the absolute size of either.

## Types and sizes of business

| Form | Ownership | Liability | Finance | Key limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | one owner | **unlimited** | savings, bank loan, family | no continuity; owner does everything; limited finance |
| **Partnership** | two or more partners | usually **unlimited** | partners' capital | profits and decisions shared; ends if a partner leaves; cannot sell shares |
| **Private limited company (Ltd)** | shareholders | **limited** | shares sold privately, all shareholders must agree the buyer | cannot advertise shares publicly; legal paperwork; accounts registered |
| **Public limited company (plc)** | shareholders | **limited** | shares traded openly on the stock exchange | huge set-up cost; control can be lost; divorce of ownership from control |
| **Franchise** | franchisee owns the outlet, franchisor owns the brand | depends on the franchisee's own legal form | start-up fee plus a share of revenue or profit to the franchisor | franchisee is restricted to the franchisor's methods |
| **Co-operative** | the members, democratically | limited | members' subscriptions and retained surplus | slower decisions; limited access to outside capital |

**Limited liability** depends on **separate legal identity**: the company is a legal person distinct from its owners, so a shareholder can lose only the amount invested. A **plc is still a private-sector business** — "public" means shares may be sold to the public, not that the state owns it.

**Franchisor** = the **o**riginal business selling the rights. **Franchisee** = the **e**xpanding new outlet buying them. The franchisor gets fast, cheap expansion but risks its brand in someone else's hands; the franchisee gets a proven model and support but pays fees and loses freedom. **A deed of partnership** sets voting rights, each partner's responsibilities and the profit/loss split; it is **not** a legal requirement, and without one everything is assumed to be shared equally.

## The stock exchange

A market where shares and government bonds are bought and sold. Only **plcs** may sell shares on it. Its functions:

| Function | What happens |
|---|---|
| Raising share capital | a firm sells new shares to the public, potentially raising very large sums (an initial public offering) |
| Facilitating growth | existing firms issue more shares; takeovers and mergers happen by buying shares |
| Trading government bonds | governments borrow by selling bonds, which buyers can then resell |
| Price mechanism for shares | share prices are set by demand and supply, driven by company performance, confidence and expectations |
| Safety and information | trades are regulated and prices published, which raises confidence |

A **share** gives ownership, a vote and a **dividend** from profits (not legally guaranteed). A **bond** is a loan: no ownership, no vote, but fixed interest and repayment at maturity. Capital raised by selling shares is **permanent** — the firm never repays it; a shareholder who wants their money back must sell to someone else.

**Market capitalisation** = share price × number of shares issued.

## Measuring size, and growth

No single measure settles it, so use **at least two or three** together. **Number of employees** understates a capital-intensive giant that employs very few; **sales revenue** is useless across industries, since one firm may sell few high-value products and another many cheap ones; **capital employed** is only comparable within an industry; **value of output** is distorted by a few very expensive products; **market share** needs a reliable definition of "the market".

**Internal (organic) growth** — expanding the firm's own operations, usually from retained profit: more outlets, more capacity, new products, new export markets.

**External growth** — a **merger** (two firms join to form a new one) or a **takeover** (one buys control of another).

| Type of integration | Meaning | Main benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | same industry, same stage of production | less competition, more market share, economies of scale |
| **Vertical backwards** | towards the raw material supplier | guaranteed supply; can cut off rivals |
| **Vertical forwards** | towards the end consumer | guaranteed outlet; captures the retailer's margin |
| **Conglomerate** | an unrelated industry | spreads the risk of failure |

| Reasons **for** growth | Reasons **against** growth |
|---|---|
| economies of scale lower average cost | diseconomies of scale raise average cost |
| specialist managers become affordable | slower decision-making |
| market power and price-setting ability | personal service and local knowledge lost |
| easier and cheaper access to finance | conflict between owners and managers (divorce of ownership) |
| risk spread over several products and markets | harder to adapt to changes in the market |

Whether growth is right depends on the **owner's objective**. An owner running a business for independence and personal service may rationally stay small.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** — **average cost per unit falls** as the scale of production rises. Not "total cost falls" — total cost almost always rises with output.

| Internal economy | How it lowers average cost |
|---|---|
| **Purchasing** | buying in bulk earns a discount per unit |
| **Marketing** | one campaign's cost is spread over more units |
| **Financial** | large firms borrow more cheaply, being seen as lower risk |
| **Technical** | large-scale specialised equipment raises output per input |
| **Managerial** | specialist managers can be employed and their salary spread over more output |
| **Risk-bearing** | a wider product range and more markets spread the risk of failure |

**External** economies arise **outside the firm but inside the industry**, so all firms in it gain: a pool of skilled workers trained by others, nearby ancillary suppliers, a shared industry reputation, improved public infrastructure.

**Diseconomies of scale** — average cost **rises** because the firm has grown too large to run well.

| Diseconomy | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Communication** | many sites, time zones, languages; messages distort |
| **Management** | too many managers, conflicting styles and priorities |
| **Labour** | workers feel remote and unmotivated; repetitive tasks |
| **Agglomeration** | too many acquired businesses at too many stages to coordinate |

## Costs, revenue and profit

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Fixed cost (FC)** | does not change with output — rent, insurance, machinery hire, salaries |
| **Variable cost (VC)** | changes with output — materials, piece-rate wages, delivery |
| **Total cost** | $TC = FC + VC$ |
| **Average (unit) cost** | $AC = TC / Q$ |
| **Marginal cost** | the extra cost of producing one more unit |
| **Total revenue** | $TR = P \times Q$ |
| **Average revenue** | $AR = TR / Q$ |
| **Profit** | $\text{profit} = TR - TC$ |

*Worked example.* A magazine sells at $5. Machinery hire is $2400 a month and factory rent $1600, so $FC = \$4000$. Materials and wages are $3 per copy.

At 5000 copies: $VC = 5000\times3 = \$15\,000$, so $TC = \$19\,000$ and $AC = 19\,000/5000 = \$3.80$. Revenue is $5000\times5 = \$25\,000$, so profit is $\$6000$. Contribution per unit is $5-3 = \$2$, so break-even output is $4000/2 = 2000$ copies — and at 2000, $TR = \$10\,000$ and $TC = 4000+6000 = \$10\,000$, neither profit nor loss.

**Average cost is normally U-shaped.** It falls at first as fixed costs are spread over more units and economies of scale appear; beyond some output it rises as the firm must hire less suitable workers, pay overtime and manage congestion — diseconomies of scale. **Average revenue usually falls as output rises**, because to sell more units the firm must cut the price. Three errors to avoid: revenue is **not** profit, average cost is **not** total cost, and breaking even is **not** making a profit.

## Business aims and objectives

A good objective is **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-specific.

| Objective | Typically pursued by | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Survival** | new and recession-hit firms | overrides everything else while it lasts |
| **Profit maximisation** | established private-sector firms | maximum revenue, minimum cost |
| **Profit satisficing** | owner-managed firms | enough profit, with leisure or independence kept |
| **Growth / market share** | firms seeking scale economies and bargaining power | risk of growing into diseconomies |
| **Sales revenue maximisation** | firms paid or judged on sales | high sales can still mean losses |
| **Social and environmental aims** | co-operatives, social enterprises, firms under public pressure | may cost profit in the short run, build reputation in the long run |

Objectives change with the firm's size, age, legal form, the state of the economy and the strength of competition. A change of objective requires a change of strategy — that is the analysis mark.

## The demand for and supply of labour

**Demand for labour is a *derived* demand** — labour is wanted for the goods and services it produces, not for its own sake. Bakers are demanded for the bread, not for the sake of employing bakers. The demand curve for labour slopes **downward**: at a lower wage, workers are more affordable, so firms hire more.

| Shifts demand for labour **right** | Shifts it **left** |
|---|---|
| higher total demand in the economy (a boom) | recession, falling demand for the product |
| higher labour productivity | rising non-wage employment costs (leave, insurance, pension contributions) |
| capital becoming relatively more expensive | capital becoming relatively cheaper through new technology |
| growth in the market the firm serves | the product going out of fashion |

The labour supply is the people of working age who are **both willing and able** to work; it excludes those in full-time education and those who choose not to work. The supply curve slopes **upward**: a higher wage attracts more workers and raises the opportunity cost of not working. It can **bend backwards** at very high wages, when a worker prefers leisure — at $7 an hour a builder works 35 hours for $245, at $10 he works 40 hours for $400, but at $15 he works only 32 hours and still earns $480.

| Shifts labour supply **right** | Shifts it **left** |
|---|---|
| population growth; higher net migration | falling birth rate; net emigration |
| a higher retirement age | a higher school leaving age |
| more training places or better provision of the relevant education | worse non-wage benefits (fewer holidays, no promotion prospects) |
| higher participation rate | occupational or geographical immobility |

$$\text{labour force participation rate} = \frac{\text{labour force}}{\text{working-age population}}\times100$$

*Worked example.* A labour force of 35 million in a working-age population of 50 million gives $35/50\times100 = 70\%$. The **labour force** is those employed plus those unemployed and seeking work — not everyone of working age.

## Wage determination and wage differentials

The **equilibrium wage** is where the demand for labour equals the supply of labour. A shift in either curve creates a new equilibrium wage and level of employment. High pay for a specialist surgeon reflects **high demand against very restricted supply**; low pay for unskilled work reflects the opposite.

| Why pay differs **between** occupations | Why pay differs **within** the same occupation |
|---|---|
| ability and qualifications required | regional differences in labour demand and supply |
| length and cost of training | experience and length of service |
| danger and unsociable hours (a compensating differential) | local pay agreements and bargaining power |
| job satisfaction — pleasant work can pay less | different non-monetary rewards |
| scarcity of the skill | **discrimination** by sex, race, age or religion |
| labour immobility and poor information about vacancies | part-time versus full-time status |

Public-sector pay may lag private-sector pay for similar skills: there is no profit to fund it, higher wages mean higher taxes, and greater job security is itself part of the reward.

Methods of payment: **wage** (per hour, a variable cost for the firm), **salary** (annual, a fixed cost), **piece rate** (per unit), **commission** (a percentage of sales), **bonus**, **performance-related pay**, **share options**, **fringe benefits** (non-cash perks). Earnings typically rise through early and middle career as skills and experience accumulate, flatten near the career peak, and fall at retirement, when income comes from pensions and savings.

## Trade unions and collective bargaining

A **trade union** is an organisation that works to improve its members' pay and conditions and provides them with support and legal services. **Collective bargaining** is negotiation by the union on behalf of all members, rather than worker by worker.

Union roles: bargaining over pay, hours, holidays and health and safety; legal advice; support for members made redundant or unfairly dismissed; lobbying government. Types: **craft** (one skill), **industrial** (all workers in one industry), **general** (any industry), **white-collar** (professional and clerical). A pay claim is usually argued on one of four grounds: the cost of living has risen faster than pay, comparable occupations earn more, productivity has risen, or industry profits are high.

**Industrial action**, in order of severity: **strike** (refusing to work), **sit-in** (occupying the workplace without working), **go-slow** (working deliberately slowly), **work-to-rule** (doing only the contractual minimum). Each cuts output and productivity; workers may lose pay, and the firm may lose orders and investor confidence.

| Stronger unions may help | Stronger unions may harm |
|---|---|
| higher wages raise living standards | higher wages raise firms' costs and cut profits |
| better and safer conditions | cost-push inflation, and a wage–price spiral |
| protection from discrimination and unfair dismissal | unemployment if wages exceed productivity |
| one negotiation is cheaper than bargaining with every worker | industrial action disrupts production and deters investment |
| better communication can raise productivity | firms switch to capital-intensive methods |

The judgement depends on the union's **starting strength**, whether the wage rise is matched by a **productivity** rise, the **elasticity of demand** for the firm's product (can the cost be passed on?), and the state of the labour market. Very weak unions leave workers exposed; very strong ones can price members out of work.

A **minimum wage** is a legal wage floor set above equilibrium. It raises the pay of those still employed and strengthens the incentive to seek work, but it creates excess supply of labour: more workers offer themselves than firms wish to hire. Whether unemployment actually rises depends on how far above equilibrium it is set and how elastic the demand for labour is.
