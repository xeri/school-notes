# Elasticity, market failure and market structures

## Price elasticity of demand

$$\text{PED} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

PED is normally **negative**, because price and quantity move in opposite directions. Some exercises report the **magnitude** (ignoring the sign) and others keep the sign. **State which convention you are using.**

**Worked example.** Price rises from $10 to $12; quantity falls from 100 to 70.

- % change in price $= 2/10 = +20\%$
- % change in quantity $= -30/100 = -30\%$
- $\text{PED} = -30/20 = -1.5$, magnitude **1.5**

Since the magnitude exceeds 1, demand is **elastic** over this range.

Do **not** mix initial-value percentages with midpoint percentages in one calculation.

| Magnitude | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $>1$ | **elastic** | quantity responds more than proportionately |
| $<1$ | **inelastic** | quantity responds less than proportionately |
| $=1$ | unit elastic | proportionate response |
| $=0$ | perfectly inelastic | quantity does not change at all |
| $=\infty$ | perfectly elastic | any price rise kills demand entirely |

## PED and total revenue

| Demand | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | revenue **falls** | revenue **rises** |
| **Inelastic** | revenue **rises** | revenue **falls** |

In the example above, revenue falls from $10\times100 = \$1000$ to $12\times70 = \$840$. Note that this is **revenue, not profit** — profit also depends on costs, and selling fewer units reduces total variable cost.

**Determinants of PED:**

- **Availability of substitutes** — the strongest factor. Many close substitutes → elastic.
- **Necessity** — essentials (insulin, bread) are inelastic; luxuries are elastic.
- **Proportion of income spent** — a cheap item (a box of matches) is inelastic; an expensive one is elastic.
- **Time** — demand is more elastic in the long run, as consumers find alternatives.
- **Habit or addiction** — makes demand inelastic.
- **Brand loyalty** — makes demand inelastic.

## Price elasticity of supply

$$\text{PES} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity supplied}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

PES is positive. **Determinants:**

- **Spare capacity** — plenty of it makes supply elastic.
- **Time** — supply is inelastic in the short run and elastic in the long run, once production can be expanded.
- **Stock levels** — large stocks of a storable good make supply elastic.
- **Mobility of factors of production** — easily switched resources make supply elastic.
- **Length of the production period** — agriculture is highly inelastic in the short run; a crop takes a season.

## Why elasticity matters

**To firms** — whether a price change will raise or lower revenue, and whether a cost rise can be passed on to customers.

**To government** — an indirect tax raises most revenue when demand is **inelastic** (hence taxes on fuel, tobacco and alcohol), but discourages consumption most when demand is **elastic**. Those two aims pull in opposite directions, which is the standard evaluation point.

## Market failure

**Market failure** is where the free market allocates resources inefficiently — producing too much or too little of something from society's point of view.

| Private cost | the cost to the decision-maker |
| **External cost** | a cost falling on a third party, not paid by the decision-maker |
| **Social cost** | private cost + external cost |

The same structure applies to benefits: **social benefit = private benefit + external benefit**.

**External costs** (negative externalities): a factory's pollution harming neighbours' health; traffic congestion; noise; litter. Because the polluter does not pay for the harm, the market **overproduces**.

**External benefits** (positive externalities): vaccination protecting those not vaccinated; education raising a whole economy's productivity; a restored building improving a street. Because the provider is not paid for the wider gain, the market **underproduces**.

**Merit goods** — goods with external benefits that consumers undervalue, so they are underconsumed (education, healthcare, vaccination).

**Demerit goods** — goods with external costs that consumers undervalue the harm of, so they are overconsumed (tobacco, alcohol, gambling).

**Public goods** are **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce another's) and **non-excludable** (you cannot stop a non-payer benefiting) — street lighting, national defence, flood defences. Non-excludability creates the **free-rider problem**: nobody will pay voluntarily, so the market supplies none at all and the state must provide them.

**Information failure** — consumers cannot judge what they are buying (the long-run harm of a product, the value of a pension), so they choose badly.

## Government intervention

| Policy | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Indirect tax** | raises the price of a demerit good, internalising the external cost | ineffective if demand is inelastic; regressive; may create a black market; hard to set the right level |
| **Subsidy** | lowers the price of a merit good, raising consumption | costs public money, which has an opportunity cost; producers may not pass it all on |
| **Regulation** | sets a direct standard or ban (emission limits, age limits) | needs monitoring and enforcement; may be evaded; blunt — hits compliant firms too |
| **State provision** | government supplies the good free or cheaply | expensive; may be inefficient without competitive pressure |
| **Information campaigns** | corrects information failure so consumers choose better | slow, and often ignored |
| **Tradable permits** | caps total pollution and lets firms trade allowances | needs accurate measurement and a well-set cap |

Evaluate by comparing the **scale of the problem** with the **likely effect** of the policy, and name the **unintended consequences**.

## Market structures

| | Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | many | a few large firms | one dominant firm |
| Barriers to entry | low | high | very high |
| Price control | none — price takers | considerable, interdependent | substantial — price maker |
| Products | similar | differentiated by branding | unique, no close substitute |
| Non-price competition | limited | heavy advertising and branding | little need |

**Barriers to entry** keep new firms out: large capital requirements, economies of scale already enjoyed by incumbents, patents, brand loyalty, control of distribution, legal protection.

**Effects of market power:**

| Argument against monopoly | Argument that it can be beneficial |
|---|---|
| higher prices, so consumers lose | economies of scale may lower unit cost, and some of that can reach consumers |
| less choice | large profits can fund research and innovation |
| less incentive to innovate or improve quality | a **natural monopoly** (water, rail track) avoids wasteful duplication |
| inefficiency without competitive pressure | may compete more effectively in international markets |

So monopoly power **can** support high prices, but scale economies or innovation may benefit customers. Assess the actual evidence in the case and the realistic alternatives — including whether a regulator could get the benefits without the harm.
