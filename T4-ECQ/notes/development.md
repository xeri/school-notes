# Development, population and poverty

## Growth and development are different things

| | Economic growth | Economic development |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | a rise in real output (real GDP) | an improvement in quality of life and living standards |
| Captured by | GDP, real GDP, real GDP per head | literacy, life expectancy, healthcare, nutrition, services, choice |
| Nature | quantitative | qualitative as well as quantitative |

Growth **enables** development — higher real GDP means more can be spent on schools, clinics and roads — but does not guarantee it. The gains can be wasted, captured by a small wealthy elite, spent on consumption rather than capacity, or offset by environmental damage. **Growth without development is a real and examinable possibility.**

**Economic welfare** is the level of prosperity and the quality of living standards in an economy. **Economic development** involves growth in the economic wealth of a region or country to improve people's well-being; governments aim for continuous, sustained growth so their economies move from developing to developed. Development objectives include:

- producing more **necessities** — food and shelter
- raising **living standards** through education and healthcare
- **expanding economic and social choice**

## Indicators of development

| Indicator | What it captures | Its limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Real GDP per head** | average income | an average conceals distribution; ignores unpaid and voluntary work, externalities, sustainability and what is produced |
| **Life expectancy** | cumulative health and nutrition | a lagging indicator; says nothing about quality of life while alive |
| **Infant and maternal mortality** | healthcare access and quality | may be under-recorded where registration is weak |
| **Literacy rate** | basic education | measures access, not the quality of schooling |
| **Mean or expected years of schooling** | education quantity | years attended is not learning achieved |
| **Doctors or hospital beds per 1000 people** | healthcare capacity | says nothing about distribution between city and countryside |
| **Access to clean water and sanitation** | basic living conditions | may be measured as availability, not reliability |
| **Pollution levels** | environmental quality | hard to compare across countries |
| **Employment by sector** | structural change | a large primary sector is a symptom, not a cause |

**Single indicators mislead.** Use **several together**, and expect them to disagree — a country can score well on income and badly on health, or vice versa.

The Cambridge IGCSE syllabus names two indicators of living standards to know in detail — **real GDP per head** and the **HDI** — with their components, advantages and disadvantages.

| | Real GDP per head | HDI |
|---|---|---|
| Components | real GDP ÷ population | income (GNI per head), education (years of schooling), health (life expectancy at birth) |
| Advantages | data widely available and regularly updated; adjusts for inflation and population; measures the goods and services available per person | broader — includes health and education as well as income; shows that countries with similar incomes can have very different living standards |
| Disadvantages | an average that hides inequality; ignores non-market output, leisure, the environment and what is produced; exchange-rate conversion can mislead | ignores distribution, gender equality, human rights and the environment; the weighting is a judgement; data quality varies |

*Worked example — GDP per head.* Country A has real GDP of \$400bn and 10 million people: $400\,000\,000\,000 \div 10\,000\,000 = \$40\,000$ per head. Country B has \$600bn and 30 million people: \$20 000 per head. B's economy is larger, but A's people are on average twice as well off.

## Composite and distribution measures

The **Human Development Index (HDI)** is the United Nations' measure of living standards and welfare. It combines three indicators into one figure between 0 and 1:

| Dimension | Measured by |
|---|---|
| **Income** | GDP (or GNI) per head |
| **Education** | years of schooling — in the teaching material, the average time a 25-year-old has spent in education |
| **Health** | life expectancy at birth |

The highest-ranked countries (Switzerland, Norway, Iceland) score around 0.96; the lowest (South Sudan, Chad, Niger) around 0.4.

| HDI strengths | HDI limitations |
|---|---|
| broader than income alone | ignores income **distribution** — between rich and poor, young and old |
| comparable across countries and over time | ignores qualitative factors such as **gender equality** and **human rights** |
| easy to communicate as a single number | ignores **environmental** issues |
| forces attention onto health and education | ignores **cultural differences** in what people value; the weighting is a judgement |

The **Gini coefficient** (devised by Corrado Gini in 1912) measures the **distribution** of income (or wealth) across a population, from **0** (perfect equality) to **1** (perfect inequality), sometimes written 0–100%. A higher value means greater inequality — high earners receive a much larger share of total income. It says nothing about the *level* of income: a poor country can be equal and a rich one unequal, so Gini and GDP per head answer different questions.

**The Lorenz curve (extension).** The Gini coefficient is read from a **Lorenz curve**, which plots the cumulative percentage of income against the cumulative percentage of the population, poorest first. Perfect equality is the diagonal (the poorest 50% earn 50% of income). The further the curve sags below the diagonal, the more unequal the distribution. Neither the Lorenz curve nor the Gini coefficient is in the Cambridge IGCSE syllabus; the Gini coefficient is in the teaching material.

![Lorenz curve: cumulative percentage of income against cumulative percentage of population. The diagonal line of perfect equality and a Lorenz curve sagging below it; area A between them and area B below the curve; Gini = A divided by A plus B; the poorest 50% earn 19% of income](econ-lorenz.svg)

The UN's **Human Poverty Index (HPI)** counts deprivation directly, with different versions for developing and developed countries:

| HPI-1 — developing countries | HPI-2 — developed countries |
|---|---|
| probability at birth of dying before 40 | probability at birth of dying before 60 |
| % of adults unable to read or write | % of adults unable to read or write well |
| % of the population below the income poverty line | % of the population on less than 50% of average income |
| % without sustainable access to an improved water source | % unemployed for 12 months or more |
| proportion of underweight children | |

Like any index it involves judgements about what to include and how to measure it, and data may be poor where poverty is worst.

In 2010 the UN replaced the HPI with the **Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)**, now published each year by the UN Development Programme with Oxford University. It looks at the same three dimensions as the HDI — **health**, **education** and **living standards** — through ten indicators (such as child mortality, nutrition, years of schooling, school attendance, cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing and assets). A person deprived in at least **a third** of the weighted indicators counts as **multidimensionally poor**. Unlike an income measure, it shows *how* people are poor, so it points to which policy would help.

## Comparing countries

The teaching material uses **MEDC** (More Economically Developed Country) and **LEDC** (Less Economically Developed Country), which replaced the older "First World / Third World" labels.

| Typical of an MEDC | Typical of an LEDC |
|---|---|
| large, modern farms | poor farming methods and low output, sometimes barely enough to feed a growing population |
| many firms of different sizes producing a variety of goods and services | very few firms producing and selling goods and services |
| well-developed road and rail systems and modern communications | poor road and rail systems |
| stable government | poor housing |
| relatively healthy, wealthy and educated population | little education for children; low life expectancy; little healthcare or clean water |

LEDCs often suffer **negative growth** — falling incomes, rising poverty, malnutrition and disease (Zimbabwe's GDP fell by about 40% between 1995 and 2004). Those developing rapidly are called **emerging economies** or **newly industrialised countries**; the teaching material groups Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Korea as the "BRICK" countries.

Economists also use three broader terms:

| Term | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Developed** | high GDP per head, life expectancy and literacy; stable or falling population growth; excellent infrastructure and healthcare; high foreign investment and productivity; a large tertiary sector | Japan |
| **Underdeveloped** | very low GDP per head; high population growth; poor infrastructure, healthcare and education; low literacy, foreign investment and productivity; a large primary sector | Somalia |
| **Developing** | becoming more developed as industry expands and fewer people suffer extreme poverty; may attract high foreign investment and shift towards services, but may still have low living standards because of high population growth | India |

**The terminology has real limits.** It is a two-box classification of a continuous spectrum; many countries fit neither box neatly (South Africa, Brazil, China and Malaysia have rich cities and very poor regions); it implies a single path of development; and it says nothing about *within*-country variation. Use whatever terms the question uses, and say explicitly that the boundaries are arbitrary.

**Reasons some countries have low development:**

- **dependence on agriculture** for income and jobs, many farms producing only enough for one family — and climate change making some crops impossible where rain fails
- **international trade dominated by developed countries**, whose subsidies raise world supply and force down the prices poorer countries receive
- **lack of capital** — machinery and equipment — because incomes are too low to save and invest
- too little investment in **education, skills and healthcare**, so labour productivity stays low (and without family-planning education, birth rates stay high)
- poor **infrastructure** — road, rail and communications — making transport and sharing information difficult
- inefficient production and distribution — low incomes give businesses little incentive to set up, rural areas are hard to reach, and unskilled workers cannot be employed
- high **population growth**, so goods and services are shared among ever more people
- unstable or corrupt **government** and **war**, using up money that could fund development

## Why development differs between countries

The Cambridge syllabus lists seven areas whose **causes and impacts** explain differences in development:

| Area | In a less developed country | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | low income per head | little saving, low spending on health and education, low tax revenue |
| **Productivity** | low output per worker — little capital, low skills, poor technology | low wages and incomes; uncompetitive exports |
| **Population growth** | high birth rate and fast-growing population | output is shared among more people; a high dependency ratio; pressure on schools and services |
| **Size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors** | most workers in the **primary** sector, often subsistence farming | low and volatile incomes, dependence on commodity prices and the weather |
| **Saving and investment** | low incomes leave little to save, so little investment in capital | slow growth in productive capacity — a vicious circle |
| **Education** | low literacy, few years of schooling | low productivity and earnings; high birth rates |
| **Healthcare** | few doctors, little clean water, high infant mortality | a less productive workforce; low life expectancy |

These reinforce each other — low income means low saving, which means low investment and low productivity, which keeps income low. Breaking into the circle at any point (education, aid-funded investment, export growth) can start development.

## Differences within a country

Development is uneven **inside** national borders as well as between them. A capital city may have universities, hospitals and fast internet while a rural region a few hundred kilometres away has none of them. Typical internal gaps: **urban against rural**, **region against region**, **formal against informal employment**, and between **ethnic, gender and disability groups**. A national average can therefore be a poor guide to how most people actually live — which is exactly why distribution measures matter alongside averages.

## Population

**Population** is all the people living in a specific place at a specific time. World population passed 8 billion in the 2020s; NZ's is about 5 million.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Birth rate** | live births per 1000 of the population per year (about 50 is high, 8 is low) |
| **Death rate** | deaths per 1000 of the population per year (roughly 3 at the lowest, 29 at the highest) |
| **Natural increase** | birth rate − death rate |
| **Net migration** | immigration − emigration |
| **Population change** | natural increase + net migration |
| **Life expectancy** | the average number of years a newborn is expected to live |

A population grows through **more births**, **fewer deaths** or **higher net migration**. In most countries births exceed deaths; much of the increase is in LEDCs, where birth rates stay high while death rates have fallen.

$$\text{natural increase rate} = \text{birth rate} - \text{death rate}$$
$$\text{population growth} = \text{natural increase} + \text{net migration}$$

*Worked example.* A country of 10 million has a birth rate of 22 and a death rate of 9 per 1000, with net migration of +20 000. Natural increase is $13/1000 \times 10\,000\,000 = 130\,000$. Total growth is $130\,000 + 20\,000 = 150\,000$, a growth rate of $150\,000/10\,000\,000 = 1.5\%$.

| Factors affecting the **birth rate** | Direction |
|---|---|
| **better living standards** — food, housing, clean water, medical care — so fewer children die | lowers it; where many children still die, families stay large so children can work and earn |
| increased use and knowledge of **contraception** | lowers it; lack of education keeps rates high in many LEDCs |
| **custom and religion** — beliefs against contraception, or a preference for large families | raises it; in MEDCs large families have become less fashionable |
| **female employment** — careers and the cost of children | lowers it |
| later **marriage** | lowers it |

| Factors affecting the **death rate** | Direction |
|---|---|
| better **living standards** — food, clothing, sanitation, shelter, cleanliness | lowers it |
| **medical advances and healthcare** — smallpox and cholera prevented or cured | lowers it |
| widespread disease such as HIV/AIDS and malaria | raises it |
| **famine, war and violence** | raises it |
| malnutrition in LEDCs; smoking and fatty diets causing cancer and heart disease in MEDCs | raises it |

**Net migration.** More people move from LEDCs to MEDCs than the reverse. For a receiving country such as NZ:

| Area | Possible effects of higher net migration |
|---|---|
| **Labour market** | fills skill shortages; more demand for goods raises demand for labour; but may oversupply some occupations and hold wages down |
| **Housing market** | upward pressure on house prices if housing supply is short |
| **Government** | more tax revenue; but may need to support unskilled migrants with transfer payments and help with integration; more demand for education and social security |
| **Other recent migrants** | more competition for jobs in certain trades (chefs, builders), which could drive wages down |
| **Society** | greater diversity, new ideas, innovation and entrepreneurship; but possible friction if the existing population sees immigration as a threat |

A sending country loses skilled workers who leave for higher pay overseas — nurses, doctors, engineers, entrepreneurs — the **brain drain** — but may gain **remittances** sent home.

## Population structure and the dependency ratio

A population can be described by four distributions:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Age distribution** | the number or share in each age group |
| **Sex distribution** | the balance of males and females |
| **Geographic distribution** | where people live |
| **Occupational distribution** | what work people do |

| Age group | 0–14 | 15–59 | 60+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Youngest countries | about 50% | 45% | 5% |
| Oldest countries | about 15% | 60% | 25% |

Age and sex structure is drawn as a **population pyramid**. An LEDC with a young population has a **wide base and narrow top**; an MEDC with an ageing population has a narrow base and a **bulge in the middle and upper age groups**. About a quarter of the world's population is expected to be over 60 by 2050 — one in three in MEDCs.

![Two population pyramids, males on the left and females on the right, by five-year age band from 0-4 to 80+. (a) A young population typical of an LEDC: a wide base narrowing steadily to a small top. (b) An ageing population typical of an MEDC: a narrow base, nearly equal bands through middle age and wide upper bands](econ-population-pyramids.svg)

**Interpreting a pyramid** (the Cambridge syllabus requires interpretation, not drawing): read the **base** for the birth rate (wide = high), the **sides** for the death rate (steep narrowing = high death rates at each age), the **top** for life expectancy (wide = long lives), any **bulge or notch** for past baby booms, wars or migration (a bulge of working-age males often means labour immigration), and compare the two **sides** for the gender balance. Then state the consequence: a young population needs schools and jobs; an ageing one needs pensions and healthcare and has fewer workers per dependant.

**Effects of changes in population size and structure.**

| Change | Possible effects |
|---|---|
| **Population increases** | a larger labour force and market, more economies of scale, more tax revenue; but pressure on housing, infrastructure, the environment and resources, and lower GDP per head if output does not keep pace |
| **Population decreases** | labour shortages, a smaller market, empty infrastructure and less tax revenue; but less congestion and pressure on resources, and possibly higher GDP per head |
| **Ageing** | higher spending on pensions and healthcare; a heavier tax burden on fewer workers; demand shifts to healthcare and retirement services |
| **More young people** | spending on education and childcare; a future workforce if jobs can be created |
| **Gender imbalance** (e.g. more males after sex-selective births, or male labour migration) | fewer marriages and births later; a labour force skewed towards particular occupations; social problems |

$$\text{dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged } 0\text{–}14 + \text{population aged } 65+}{\text{population aged } 15\text{–}64}\times100$$

This uses the standard 15–64 working-age band; the age table above uses 60 as the boundary instead, so always use the bands a question gives.

*Worked example.* With 3 million under 15, 1 million over 64 and 6 million of working age: $(3+1)/6\times100 = 66.7$ — about 67 dependants per 100 people of working age.

**The ratio is an age calculation, not an employment one.** It does not prove that every working-age person has a job or that every older person is dependent.

| | Ageing population | Young population |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | falling birth rate, rising life expectancy | high birth rate, falling death rate |
| Pressure on | pensions, healthcare, retirement villages | schools, maternity care, child nutrition |
| Labour force | shrinks; skill shortages; may need a higher retirement age or immigration | grows rapidly; must create enough jobs or face youth unemployment |
| Demand shifts to | healthcare, retirement housing, leisure for older people | education, housing, baby and child goods |
| Tax burden | falls on a smaller working population | falls on a working population supporting many children |
| Possible upsides | experienced workers; high accumulated savings; "silver" markets | a future workforce and a demographic dividend if educated and employed |

**Geographic distribution.** Almost 90% of people live in LEDCs, putting heavy pressure on their scarce resources. Density averages around 50 people per square kilometre, but ranges from over 1000 to under 3. About half the world lives in towns and cities, a share expected to reach 60%. **Urbanisation** has raised production and living standards, but it consumes trees, open space and water, and more energy and car use in cities raises pollution and health risks.

**Occupational distribution.** Most workers in MEDCs are in **services**; most in LEDCs are in **agriculture**. In fast-developing countries such as China and India, people are moving out of agriculture into manufacturing and services.

**Over- and under-population** are about the balance between people and resources, not absolute size. The **optimum population** is the level at which **output per head is maximised**.

| | Over-population | Under-population |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | too many people for the available resources | too little labour to make full use of the resources |
| Symptoms | famine, energy and housing shortages, disease; falling GDP per head; congestion; unemployment | resources unexploited; infrastructure underused; skill shortages |
| Typical causes | high birth rate or large net immigration with limited resources | fertility below replacement level; strict immigration policy; emigration |

A densely populated country is not automatically over-populated, nor a sparse one under-populated — it depends on resources and technology.

**Government measures** to **reduce** population growth: limit immigration; improve education (more educated people have fewer children); teach about contraception and subsidise it or make it free; remove financial incentives to have children. To **increase** it: open borders to more immigration; give financial incentives such as paid parental leave and child support; and help families balance work and family life through flexible hours and working from home.

## Poverty

**Poverty** is a state in which a person or community lacks the financial resources and essentials for a minimum standard of living — income is so low that basic needs cannot be met, so people may go without proper housing, clean water, healthy food and medical care. Each country sets its own poverty threshold.

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Extreme (absolute) poverty** | cannot meet basic needs for food, water, clothing and shelter — an income below about **US\$1.25 a person a day** |
| **Moderate poverty** | basic needs met, but no resources to improve life through healthcare, education or hygiene — below about **US\$2.50 a day** |
| **Income poverty** | household income below the country's official poverty line |
| **Relative poverty** | lacking the resources others in the same country normally have — for example an income below 50–60% of the national average |

| | Absolute poverty | Relative poverty |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold | a fixed subsistence line | a fraction of average or median income |
| Over time | can be eliminated in principle | exists while incomes differ |
| Comparison | comparable across countries | meaningful only within one country |

About 1.2 billion people live in extreme poverty and nearly half the world's population is moderately poor. The countries with the lowest GDP per head are mostly in Africa (Burundi, South Sudan, Malawi, Mozambique) plus Afghanistan.

**Groups more affected:**

- **women** — discrimination in access to resources, education, land, credit, health services, decent work and pay; a mother's situation directly affects her children
- **people with disabilities** — poverty increases the chance of disability, and disability the chance of poverty
- **minority groups** — discrimination by ethnicity, religion or language limits access to resources
- **rural people** — in developing countries 60% of rural people are poor, lacking schools, doctors and roads to market; those who move to cities often end up in slums with irregular, low-paid or dangerous work, so urban poverty grows too

**Causes of poverty:** inadequate access to clean water and nutritious food; little or no access to livelihoods and jobs; conflict; inequality; poor education; climate change; lack of infrastructure; limited government capacity; and no reserves (savings or assets) to fall back on.

The Cambridge syllabus highlights four causes that apply in **every** country, rich or poor: **unemployment** (no earned income), **low wages** (low-skilled, insecure or part-time work), **illness and disability** (unable to work, and higher costs), and **age** (children in poor households; elderly people without adequate pensions or savings).

**Policies to alleviate poverty and redistribute income** — the Cambridge list:

| Policy | How it reduces poverty | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Promoting economic growth** | more jobs and higher incomes | the gains may not reach the poorest |
| **Improved education** | higher skills, productivity and wages | slow — takes years to pay off |
| **More generous state benefits** | raises the incomes of the unemployed, sick, elderly and families | cost to taxpayers; may weaken the incentive to work |
| **Progressive taxation** | takes a larger share from high incomes to fund benefits and services | high top rates may discourage work and investment, or encourage avoidance |
| **A national minimum wage** | raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers | may cause unemployment if set too high — see [Firms, costs and labour markets](../notes/firms-labour.md) |

**The poverty cycle** explains why poverty persists:

low income → not enough food, healthcare and basic needs → hunger and malnutrition → sickness and delays in child development → low energy at work, and children who cannot concentrate at school → few work opportunities as adults → low income again.

It is a tendency, **not** an inevitability every household follows — and it shows where policy can break in.

## Reducing poverty and promoting development

| Policy | How it breaks the cycle | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| **Growth programmes** in health, education, nutrition and sanitation that let the poor take part (studies cited in the teaching material suggest a 10% rise in average income cuts poverty by 20–30%) | raises incomes broadly | whether the gains reach the poor |
| **Education and skills** | raises productivity and earning power; lowers birth rates | quality, not just enrolment; takes a generation to pay off |
| **Healthcare and basic services** (food, sanitation, shelter, clean water) | healthier workers lose fewer days and live longer | access in rural areas; trained staff; running costs, not just buildings |
| **Infrastructure, technology and energy** (roads, power, internet) | links people to jobs, markets and information | huge up-front cost; maintenance; remote areas may be bypassed |
| **Agricultural programmes and fairer access to water and natural resources** | most of the rural poor depend on farming | credit, land rights and investment (China lent heavily to develop rural areas) |
| **Jobs, income and entrepreneurship** (including microfinance) | paid work is the main route out of poverty | stable government, credit for small firms, skilled labour |
| **Trade** | export markets raise incomes and foreign exchange; some countries have traded their way out of poverty | market access abroad; risk of over-dependence on one commodity |
| **Investment in capital** | raises output and quality | savings or foreign investment to fund it |
| **Aid and help from MEDCs** | funds what domestic saving cannot | may create dependency, be misdirected, or carry conditions |
| **Social protection and progressive transfers** | supports those who cannot support themselves | administrative capacity; may weaken work incentives |
| **Empowering the poor and removing barriers** to equal access | programmes reflect what matters to the people they serve | genuine involvement, not tokenism |

Assess any of these on four things: **access** (does it reach the poorest, or only the already-advantaged?), **quality** (a school with no teachers achieves nothing), **implementation capacity** (can the government actually deliver it, free of corruption?), and **the time before benefits arrive** (infrastructure and education pay off over decades, cash transfers within weeks). Also state the **opportunity cost** — a government with limited money, time and resources may manage only one of these, and may need trade or help from richer countries.

## Practice

1. Describe the characteristics of an LEDC. *Model points:* poor road and rail; few firms and a narrow range of goods; little education and healthcare; poor housing; low-output farming; limited clean water.
2. Explain two reasons why some countries have low economic development. *Model points:* any two from the list above, each with its consequence (low investment in education → low productivity; poor infrastructure → costly transport and communication).
3. Discuss how an LEDC could best improve its standard of living. *Model points:* infrastructure (jobs, efficient firms, electricity and internet access), education and healthcare (skills, productivity, lower birth rates), capital investment (more and better output); evaluate on cost, time and the government's limited resources, and on the possible need for trade or outside help.
4. Country X has real GDP of \$90bn and a population of 3 million; country Y has \$300bn and 15 million. Which has the higher real GDP per head? *Answer:* X — \$30 000 against \$20 000.
5. Explain two components of the HDI. *Model points:* income (GNI per head) — the ability to buy goods and services; education (years of schooling) — skills and life chances; health (life expectancy at birth) — the quality of healthcare and nutrition; each with why it reflects living standards.
6. Analyse how long-term unemployment can cause relative poverty. *Model points:* define relative poverty; very low income compared with the average; cannot afford adequate housing or healthcare; savings run down; dependence on benefits; children's education suffers; health and motivation deteriorate, making work harder to find.
