# Development, population and poverty

## Growth and development are different things

| | Economic growth | Economic development |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | a rise in real output (real GDP) | an improvement in living standards and opportunity |
| Captured by | GDP, real GDP, real GDP per head | health, education, nutrition, services, choice, freedom |
| Nature | quantitative | qualitative as well as quantitative |

Growth **enables** development — higher output means more tax revenue for schools, clinics and roads — but does not guarantee it. The gains can be captured by a small elite, spent on consumption rather than capacity, or offset by environmental damage. **Growth without development is a real and examinable possibility.**

**Economic welfare** is the level of prosperity and the quality of living standards in an economy. Development objectives usually include producing more necessities, raising living standards through education and healthcare, and widening economic and social choice.

## Indicators of development

| Indicator | What it captures | Its limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Real GDP per head** | average income | an average conceals distribution; ignores unpaid work, externalities and the composition of output |
| **Life expectancy** | cumulative health and nutrition | a lagging indicator; says nothing about quality of life while alive |
| **Infant and maternal mortality** | healthcare access and quality | may be under-recorded where registration is weak |
| **Literacy rate** | basic education | measures access, not the quality of schooling |
| **Mean or expected years of schooling** | education quantity | years attended is not learning achieved |
| **Doctors or hospital beds per 1000 people** | healthcare capacity | says nothing about distribution between city and countryside |
| **Access to clean water and sanitation** | basic living conditions | may be measured as availability, not reliability |
| **Energy or internet access** | infrastructure | counts connections, not usability or cost |
| **Employment by sector** | structural change | a large primary sector is a symptom, not a cause |

**Single indicators mislead.** Use **several together**, and expect them to disagree — a country can score well on income and badly on health, or vice versa.

## Composite indicators

The **Human Development Index** combines three dimensions into one figure between 0 and 1:

| Dimension | Measured by |
|---|---|
| Income | GDP (or GNI) per head |
| Education | years of schooling |
| Health | life expectancy at birth |

| HDI strengths | HDI limitations |
|---|---|
| broader than income alone | ignores income **distribution** within the country |
| comparable across countries and over time | ignores gender equality, human rights and political freedom |
| easy to communicate as a single number | ignores environmental quality and sustainability |
| forces attention onto health and education | the weighting of the three dimensions is a judgement, not a fact |

The **Gini coefficient** measures the **distribution** of income across a population, from **0** (perfect equality) to **1** (perfect inequality). A higher value means greater inequality. It says nothing about the *level* of income — a poor country can be equal and a rich one unequal, so Gini and GDP per head answer different questions.

A **human poverty index** approach instead counts deprivation directly: the probability of dying young, the proportion unable to read and write, the proportion below the income poverty line, the proportion without safe water, and the proportion of underweight children.

## Comparing countries

The teaching material uses **MEDC** (More Economically Developed Country) and **LEDC** (Less Economically Developed Country), which replaced the older "First World / Third World" labels.

| Typical of an MEDC | Typical of an LEDC |
|---|---|
| modern large-scale farming | low-output farming, sometimes barely at subsistence |
| many firms of many sizes, large tertiary sector | few firms, large primary sector |
| developed road, rail and communication networks | poor transport and communications |
| stable government and institutions | unstable or weakened government; conflict in some cases |
| healthy, educated, long-lived population | low life expectancy, limited schooling, limited healthcare |

**The terminology has real limits.** It is a two-box classification of a continuous spectrum; many countries fit neither box; it implies a single path of development; and it tells you nothing about *within*-country variation. Economists also use **developed**, **developing** and **underdeveloped** economies, and **emerging** or **newly industrialised** economies for those industrialising rapidly. Use whatever terms the question uses, and say explicitly that the boundaries are arbitrary.

**Reasons some countries have low development:** dependence on agriculture, often at subsistence level and increasingly exposed to climate change; lack of **capital** — machinery and equipment — because incomes are too low to save and invest; too little investment in **education, skills and healthcare**, so labour productivity stays low; poor **infrastructure**, making transport and communication costly; high **population growth**, so output has to be shared among more people; weak or corrupt **government**, conflict and war; and a position in **international trade** dominated by richer countries' subsidies and market power.

## Differences within a country

Development is uneven **inside** national borders as well as between them. A capital city may have universities, hospitals and fast internet while a rural region a few hundred kilometres away has none of them. Typical internal gaps: **urban against rural**, **region against region**, **formal against informal employment**, and between **ethnic, gender and disability groups**. A national average can therefore be a poor guide to how most people actually live — which is exactly why distribution measures matter alongside averages.

## Population

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Birth rate** | live births per 1000 of the population per year |
| **Death rate** | deaths per 1000 of the population per year |
| **Natural increase** | birth rate − death rate |
| **Net migration** | immigration − emigration |
| **Population change** | natural increase + net migration |
| **Life expectancy** | the average number of years a newborn is expected to live |

$$\text{natural increase rate} = \text{birth rate} - \text{death rate}$$
$$\text{population growth} = \text{natural increase} + \text{net migration}$$

*Worked example.* A country of 10 million has a birth rate of 22 and a death rate of 9 per 1000, with net migration of +20,000. Natural increase is $13/1000 \times 10\,000\,000 = 130\,000$. Total growth is $130\,000 + 20\,000 = 150\,000$, a growth rate of $150\,000/10\,000\,000 = 1.5\%$.

| Factors affecting the **birth rate** | Direction |
|---|---|
| better living standards, so fewer children die | lowers it — families need fewer births to achieve a surviving family |
| availability and knowledge of **contraception** | lowers it |
| **custom and religion** favouring large families | raises it |
| **female education and employment** | lowers it — careers delay and reduce childbearing |
| later **marriage** | lowers it |
| children needed as farm labour and old-age support | raises it |

| Factors affecting the **death rate** | Direction |
|---|---|
| better food, clean water, sanitation and housing | lowers it |
| medical advances, vaccination and healthcare access | lowers it |
| widespread infectious disease | raises it |
| famine, war and violence | raises it |
| diet-related and lifestyle disease in richer countries | raises it |

**Net migration** has real effects both ways. A receiving country may fill skill shortages, enlarge its working population and gain tax revenue and entrepreneurship, but faces pressure on housing, schools and services and possible downward pressure on wages in the affected occupations. A sending country loses the cost of training its skilled workers — the **brain drain** — but gains **remittances** sent home.

## Population structure and the dependency ratio

**Population structure** is the distribution of the population by age and sex, drawn as a **population pyramid**. A young population gives a **wide base and narrow top**; an ageing population gives a **narrow base and a bulge in the middle and upper age groups**.

$$\text{dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged } 0\text{–}14 + \text{population aged } 65+}{\text{population aged } 15\text{–}64}\times100$$

*Worked example.* With 3 million under 15, 1 million over 64 and 6 million of working age: $(3+1)/6\times100 = 66.7$ — about 67 dependants per 100 people of working age.

**The ratio is an age calculation, not an employment one.** It does not prove that every working-age person has a job or that every older person is dependent.

| | Ageing population | Young population |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | falling birth rate, rising life expectancy | high birth rate, falling death rate |
| Pressure on | pensions, healthcare, residential care | schools, maternity care, child nutrition |
| Labour force | shrinks; skill shortages; may need higher retirement age or immigration | grows rapidly; must create enough jobs or face youth unemployment |
| Demand shifts to | healthcare, retirement housing, leisure for older people | education, housing, baby and child goods |
| Tax burden | falls on a smaller working population | falls on a working population supporting many children |
| Possible upsides | experienced workers; high savings accumulated; "silver" markets | a future workforce and a demographic dividend if educated and employed |

**Government measures** to slow population growth: provide and subsidise contraception, educate about family planning, raise female education and employment, limit immigration, remove financial incentives to have children. To raise it: open borders to immigration, give financial incentives such as paid parental leave and child support, and make it easier to combine work and family through flexible hours.

**Over- and under-population** are about the balance between population and resources, not absolute size.

| | Over-population | Optimum population | Under-population |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | too many people for the available resources | output per head is maximised | too few people to use the available resources |
| Symptoms | food, housing and energy shortages; falling GDP per head; congestion; unemployment | — | resources unexploited; infrastructure underused; skill shortages |
| Typical causes | high birth rate or large net immigration with limited resources | — | fertility below replacement; restrictive immigration; emigration |

A densely populated country is not automatically over-populated, and a sparsely populated one is not automatically under-populated — it depends entirely on the resources and technology available.

## Poverty

| | Absolute poverty | Relative poverty |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | income too low to meet **basic needs** — food, clean water, clothing, shelter | income low **compared with others** in the same society |
| Threshold | a fixed subsistence income line | usually a fraction of median or average income |
| Over time | can be eliminated in principle | always exists while incomes differ |
| Comparison | comparable across countries | only meaningful within one country |

**Causes of poverty:** no access to work or to a livelihood; low wages and insecure, informal employment; inadequate food and clean water; poor education; poor health and disability; conflict and displacement; discrimination and inequality; lack of infrastructure, especially in rural areas; climate change destroying agricultural livelihoods; limited government capacity; and no savings or assets to fall back on.

**Groups more exposed:** women, who face unequal access to land, credit, education and paid work; people with disabilities, since poverty causes disability and disability causes poverty; minority groups facing discrimination; and rural populations, who lack schools, clinics and roads to market — and who then carry poverty into cities as they migrate in search of work.

**The poverty cycle** is the mechanism to be able to explain: low income → inadequate food and healthcare → illness and poor child development → low energy and poor concentration at school and work → few skills and poor qualifications → low-paid, insecure work → low income again. It is a tendency that explains how poverty persists, **not** an inevitability that every household follows — and it tells you where to break in.

| Policy | How it breaks the cycle | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| **Education** | raises skills, productivity and earning power; lowers birth rates | quality of teaching, not just enrolment; takes a generation to pay off |
| **Healthcare** | healthier workers lose fewer days and live longer | access in rural areas; trained staff; running costs, not just buildings |
| **Infrastructure** | roads, power and communications link people to jobs and markets | huge up-front cost; maintenance; benefits may bypass remote areas |
| **Investment and job creation** | paid work is the main route out of poverty | needs stable government, credit for small firms and skilled labour |
| **Trade** | export markets raise incomes and foreign exchange earnings | market access abroad; risk of over-dependence on one commodity |
| **Aid** | funds what domestic saving cannot | may create dependency, may be misdirected, and often carries conditions |
| **Progressive taxation and transfers** | redistributes directly to the poorest | needs administrative capacity; may weaken work incentives |
| **Minimum wage and labour law** | raises the pay of the low-paid in formal work | does nothing for the informal sector; may cut formal employment |
| **Microfinance and property rights** | lets poor households invest and own assets | needs legal systems and financial regulation |

Assess any of these on four things: **access** (does it reach the poorest, or only the already-advantaged?), **quality** (a school with no teachers achieves nothing), **implementation capacity** (can the government actually deliver it, free of corruption?), and **the time before benefits arrive** (infrastructure and education pay off over decades, cash transfers within weeks). Also state the **opportunity cost** — a government that funds one of these cannot fund the others at the same scale.
