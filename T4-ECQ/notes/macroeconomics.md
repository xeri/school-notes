# Macroeconomics and policy

## Money

Before money there was **barter** — exchanging goods directly for goods. Its four problems explain why money exists: it needs a **double coincidence of wants**, there is no agreed **measure of value**, goods are often **perishable**, and value is **subjective**.

| Function of money | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | is widely accepted in return for goods and services |
| **Measure (unit) of account** | lets the value of different products be compared |
| **Store of value** | holds value so spending can be delayed |
| **Standard of deferred payment** | allows buying now and paying later, so debt can exist |

| Characteristic | Why it is needed |
|---|---|
| **Acceptability** | everyone must agree to take it |
| **Divisibility** | prices are not all whole units — you cannot halve a goat |
| **Portability** | it must be easy to carry |
| **Durability** | it must survive handling and time |
| **Scarcity** | anything in unlimited supply has no value |

The **money supply** is notes, coins, bank deposits and other highly liquid assets. Physical cash is only a small fraction of it; most money is a record in a bank account.

Four terms worth keeping straight: **spending** is buying goods and services; **saving** is delaying consumption; **borrowing** is obtaining money from a lender; **disposable income** is what is left of income after taxes and deductions.

What moves them: a rise in **income** raises spending, saving and borrowing together. A rise in **direct tax** cuts spending and saving, though some borrow more to maintain their spending. A rise in **interest rates** cuts spending and borrowing and raises saving. Easier **credit** and higher **consumer confidence** both raise spending and borrowing and cut saving. Age, tastes and family circumstances matter too — the young spend a larger share, and those starting families borrow more.

## Banks and the central bank

Banks are **intermediaries** between savers and borrowers. They earn profit by charging a higher interest rate to borrowers than they pay to savers, by charging fees, and by investing deposits. A riskier loan carries a higher interest rate; a mortgage is cheaper because the house is security.

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **Commercial (retail) bank** | takes deposits, makes loans, provides credit cards and overdrafts, payments and foreign exchange |
| **Investment bank** | helps firms raise finance, issues shares, advises on mergers and takeovers |
| **Credit union** | a not-for-profit co-operative owned by members, offering low-cost loans |
| **Central bank** | issues notes and coins, holds government accounts, manages the national debt and reserves, regulates and lends to commercial banks, and operates **monetary policy** |

The **money market** is simply the market for money: those who want to borrow, and those who supply funds. The **interest rate** is the price of borrowing money and the reward for saving it.

## Government macroeconomic objectives

| Objective | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | a sustained rise in real GDP |
| **Full employment** | unemployment low and stable |
| **Price stability** | low and predictable inflation |
| **Balance of payments stability** | not persistently spending more abroad than is earned |
| **Redistribution of income** | reduced poverty and inequality |

Governments also pursue environmental protection. No government achieves all of these at once — the conflicts are set out at the end of this topic.

## GDP, real GDP and real GDP per head

**Gross domestic product** is the money value of all goods and services produced within a country in a period, usually a year. It is a measure of **output**.

$$\text{GDP} = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

where $C$ is consumer spending, $I$ investment by firms, $G$ government spending, $X$ exports and $M$ imports.

**Economic growth** is the annual percentage change in GDP. Its causes are more or better **factor endowments**, a larger or more skilled **labour force**, higher **labour productivity**, and **investment** in capital.

| Term | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal GDP** | output valued at **current** prices | distorted by inflation |
| **Real GDP** | nominal GDP with the effect of price changes removed | shows whether output genuinely rose |
| **Real GDP per head** | real GDP divided by population | allows for population change; the usual living-standards proxy |

$$\text{real GDP} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{price index}/100}$$

*Worked example.* Nominal GDP rises from $100 bn to $110 bn while prices rise 10%, so the index is 110. Real GDP is $110/1.10 = \$100$ bn — output has not grown at all; the whole rise was inflation. If the population is 5 million and real GDP is $100 bn, real GDP per head is $100{,}000{,}000{,}000/5{,}000{,}000 = \$20{,}000$.

**Never claim people are better off because nominal GDP rose.** Check prices, then check population.

**Limitations of GDP as a welfare measure:** it ignores unpaid and voluntary work and the informal economy; it says nothing about **distribution**, so a rise may go to a small elite; it ignores the **composition** of output (weapons count the same as hospitals); it ignores **negative externalities** such as pollution and resource depletion; and it ignores leisure, health, security and the quality of public services. **Economic growth** is a rise in output, while **economic development** is an improvement in living standards and opportunity — health, education, choice. Growth makes development possible by providing the resources, but does **not** guarantee it; see [development.md](development.md).

| Benefits of growth | Costs of growth |
|---|---|
| more goods and services available | depletion of non-renewable resources |
| higher employment and incomes | pollution and other external costs |
| higher government tax revenue, so better public services | inflation if demand outruns capacity |
| more business opportunity and investment | inequality if gains are unevenly shared |
| rising living standards | technology may displace workers |

## The economic cycle

Short-run fluctuations of real GDP growth around the long-run **trend**, typically over several years.

| Stage | What is happening |
|---|---|
| **Growth** | output, incomes and employment rising; new firms starting; profits rising |
| **Boom / peak** | demand, sales and profits at their highest; shortages of materials and skilled labour; costs and inflation rising; the economy "overheats"; interest rates likely raised |
| **Recession / downturn** | growth slows and may turn negative; demand, profits and investment fall; redundancies raise unemployment; interest rates likely cut |
| **Recovery / upturn** | confidence and spending return; output, revenue and profit rise; firms rehire |

A **recession** is a sustained fall in real GDP; a prolonged, deep one is a **slump**. Recoveries are described by shape: **V** (sharp fall, quick rebound after a one-off shock), **U** (prolonged, with lasting damage), **K** (some industries recover while others keep falling).

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a **sustained rise in the general price level**. It is always expressed as a rate — a percentage change per period. Not every price has to rise.

**Measurement by a price index.** A **consumer price index** tracks the price of a representative **basket** of goods and services bought by a typical household. Each item is **weighted** by the share of household spending it takes: if food is 20% of spending, it carries a 20% weighting. The base year is set to **100**.

$$\text{price index} = \frac{\text{weighted average price this year}}{\text{weighted average price in the base year}}\times100$$
$$\text{inflation rate} = \frac{\text{index}_{\text{new}} - \text{index}_{\text{old}}}{\text{index}_{\text{old}}}\times100$$

*Worked example.* The basket costs $30 in the base year and $33.30 now: index $= 33.30/30\times100 = 111$, so prices are **11% above the base year**. If next year the basket costs $36, the index is $36/30\times100 = 120$ — 20% above the base year, and $(120-111)/111\times100 = 8.1\%$ inflation over the single year.

**An index of 120 means prices are 20% higher than in the base year — not 120% higher, and not 20% inflation this year.** That is the classic mistake.

Uses of the index: as an **economic indicator** guiding policy, as a **deflator** to convert nominal values into real ones, and for **indexation** of wages, pensions and benefits. Its problems: tastes and fashions change so the basket must be revised, new products appear, volatile items may be excluded, and the "average" household does not exist, so the index fits no household exactly.

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Demand-pull** | total demand rises faster than firms can supply, so prices are bid up — often from rising consumer spending, government spending, credit growth or a growing money supply |
| **Cost-push** | production costs rise (wages, materials, energy, indirect taxes) and firms raise prices to protect margins |
| **Imported** | a fall in the exchange rate, or higher world prices, raise import prices and feed through to domestic prices |

| Group | Effect of inflation |
|---|---|
| **Consumers** | real income and purchasing power fall |
| **Savers** | lose — the real value of savings falls unless interest exceeds inflation |
| **Borrowers** | gain — the real value of the debt falls |
| **Fixed and low-income earners** | hit hardest; they cannot negotiate rises and spend most of their income |
| **Firms** | costs rise, uncertainty deters investment, wage demands follow, exports lose competitiveness |
| **The economy** | menu and administration costs, uncertainty, loss of international competitiveness |

**Hyperinflation** is extremely rapid inflation. It destroys money's function as a store of value: people hoard goods, shortages appear, saving collapses, banks fail and tax revenue falls in real terms.

**Deflation** is a **sustained fall** in the general price level. **Benign** deflation comes from rising supply — better technology, higher productivity — and is helpful. **Malign** deflation comes from falling demand and is damaging: consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices, so firms cut output and staff, incomes fall, the real value of debt rises, investment stops and recession deepens. Cutting the interest rate helps less than it seems, because with prices falling the **real** interest rate stays high: borrowing $1000 at 0% while prices fall 5% means repaying debt worth $1050 in real terms. **Disinflation is not deflation** — disinflation is inflation slowing down, so prices are still rising, just more slowly.

## Employment and unemployment

The **labour force** is those of working age who are **both willing and able** to work. The **unemployed** are the part of the labour force without a job and seeking one.

$$\text{unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{number unemployed}}{\text{labour force}}\times100$$

Measurement is contested: the figure excludes those unable to work, those unwilling to work, students and (in many countries) the armed forces, and it misses **under-utilisation** — part-timers who want more hours. So **"unemployed" is not the same as "not in work"**.

| Type | Cause | Policy that fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical** | low total demand in a recession | boost demand — fiscal or monetary expansion |
| **Structural** | long-term decline of an industry leaves workers with the wrong skills or in the wrong place | retraining, relocation help, regional policy |
| **Frictional** | time spent between leaving one job and finding the next | better job information, employment services |
| **Seasonal** | the work only exists at certain times of year | little needed; largely unavoidable |

**Consequences.** For the individual: lost income, falling living standards, loss of skills the longer it lasts, and damage to health and morale. For the government: higher welfare spending and lower tax revenue, so an opportunity cost in forgone spending elsewhere. For the economy: wasted human resources, lower GDP than possible, weaker demand, and social problems including crime.

Low unemployment is a sign of growth but can be **inflationary**: with vacancies hard to fill, firms bid wages up and pass the cost on in prices.

## Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is varying **government spending** and **taxation** to influence total demand.

| Tax type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | taken directly from income, profit or wealth; the burden falls on the person or firm taxed | income tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax, inheritance tax |
| **Indirect** | levied on spending; the consumer pays the producer, who passes the tax to government | general sales tax or VAT, excise duties on alcohol, tobacco and fuel, import tariffs, user charges such as tolls |

| Principle of a good tax | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Equity** | fair, and related to the ability to pay |
| **Non-distortionary** | does not badly distort behaviour or destroy the incentive to work |
| **Certainty** | the taxpayer knows what is due and when |
| **Convenience** | easy and cheap to pay and to collect |

A tax is **progressive** if the **proportion** of income taken rises as income rises (so it reduces inequality), **proportional** or flat if the same proportion is taken at every income, and **regressive** if the proportion taken falls as income rises (so it widens inequality). The test is always the proportion, never the amount — a rich person paying more dollars under a flat rate is not being taxed progressively.

Indirect taxes are **regressive in effect**, even at a single rate: low-income households spend nearly all their income, so nearly all of it is taxed, while higher earners save part of theirs.

*Worked example — a progressive structure.* Suppose 10% on the first $20,000, 20% on income from $20,001 to $50,000, and 30% above $50,000. On an income of $60,000:

$$20\,000\times0.10 + 30\,000\times0.20 + 10\,000\times0.30 = 2000 + 6000 + 3000 = \$11\,000$$

The **effective (average) rate** is $11\,000/60\,000 = 18.3\%$ — far below the 30% **marginal** rate. Marginal and average rates are different numbers; read which the question wants.

| Indirect taxes — advantages | Indirect taxes — disadvantages |
|---|---|
| cheap and easy to collect | regressive, so they worsen inequality |
| everyone pays, including those not earning | inflationary — they raise prices directly |
| can target demerit goods and external costs | revenue is hard to forecast |
| the rate is easy to change | can encourage evasion and black markets |

**Government spending** goes on public goods, merit goods, public services, welfare transfers, infrastructure and debt interest. A **budget deficit** is spending above revenue, the usual result of **expansionary** fiscal policy (raising spending or cutting taxes to boost demand); a **budget surplus** is revenue above spending, the result of **contractionary** fiscal policy (cutting spending or raising taxes to slow demand and reduce debt).

Problems with fiscal policy: the right size and timing are hard to judge, and overshooting overheats the economy; government borrowing may leave less finance for private firms; higher income and profit taxes may blunt the incentive to work and invest; and expectations of inflation can trigger wage claims.

## Monetary policy

Monetary policy is the central bank's management of **interest rates** and the **money supply**, which also influences the exchange rate.

| | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | **lowered** | **raised** |
| Borrowing | cheaper, so consumption and investment rise | dearer, so consumption and investment fall |
| Saving | less attractive | more attractive |
| Money supply | increased, including by buying bonds from banks (quantitative easing) | reduced, including by selling bonds to banks |
| Exchange rate | tends to fall as foreign savers are less attracted | tends to rise as higher returns attract foreign savers |
| Used when | the economy is weak or in recession | the economy is overheating and inflation is rising |

The transmission is not instant or certain. A rate cut only raises spending if households and firms are **confident** and credit is **available**; a heavily indebted household may use lower repayments to pay down debt instead.

## Supply-side policy

Measures to raise the economy's **productive capacity**, not its demand: education and training, infrastructure investment, tax incentives for investment and research, subsidies, deregulation, competition policy, privatisation, removing trade barriers, and reducing the gap between benefits and wages. The gains are usually real but **slow** — training a workforce or building a road takes years, and the money has an opportunity cost.

## Conflicts between objectives

| Conflict | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Growth vs price stability** | if demand rises faster than capacity, the result is higher prices rather than more output |
| **Full employment vs price stability** | low unemployment means less spare labour, so wages are bid up and feed into prices |
| **Growth vs the environment** | more output and consumption means more pollution and resource depletion |
| **Growth vs the balance of payments** | rising incomes raise spending on imports, worsening the current account |
| **Redistribution vs incentives** | higher taxes and benefits reduce inequality but may weaken the incentive to work and invest |
| **Fiscal expansion vs sound public finances** | boosting demand through spending enlarges the deficit and the national debt |

**The starting position decides the outcome.** A demand boost in a deep recession, with spare capacity and unemployed workers, mainly raises **output**. The same boost at full capacity mainly raises **prices**. Always say which case you are in, and add that policies work with a **time lag**, so a measure can arrive after the problem it was meant to solve has passed.
