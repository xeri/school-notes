# Macroeconomics and policy

## Money

**Money** is any agreed commodity that is used as a **medium of exchange** to buy goods and services. It has developed over time. Before money, people were self-sufficient or used **barter** — exchanging goods and services directly for other goods and services (a shell for a goat). Māori custom also rested on reciprocity rather than money: **utu** (returning kind deeds) and **koha** (gift-giving).

| Problem with barter | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Double coincidence of wants** | each side must have what the other wants |
| **Measurement of value** | how many goats is a house worth? |
| **Perishability** | crops fail and food goes off |
| **Subjective value** | each person values goods differently, so how is a price agreed? |

Common valued items (knives, beads) came first, then gold and silver **coins** of guaranteed weight — though people shaved metal off them — and then **paper money** issued by banks and exchangeable for gold, in many competing versions before central banks existed.

| Characteristic of money | Why it is needed |
|---|---|
| **Universal acceptability** | everybody must agree to accept it |
| **Divisibility** | prices are not all whole units — you cannot cut a goat in half |
| **Portability** | it must be easy to carry |
| **Durability** | it must survive handling and time |
| **Scarcity** | anything in unlimited supply has no value |

| Function of money | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | widely accepted in exchange for goods and services; today, mainly currency |
| **Measure (unit) of value** | lets the values of different products be compared |
| **Store of value** | can be kept and spent later while holding its value, so spending can be timed |
| **Means (standard) of deferred payment** | allows "buy now, pay later" — debt — because the seller trusts money will hold its value |

The **money supply** is notes, coins, bank deposits and other very liquid assets. Physical cash is only around **8–9%** of it; the rest is digital or held in financial assets. Cash loses value over time through inflation.

## Spending, saving and borrowing

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Spending** | buying goods and services to satisfy needs and wants |
| **Saving** | delaying consumption by keeping part of income for the future; it earns interest and increases wealth |
| **Borrowing** | obtaining money from a bank, other institution or person for a purpose; it incurs interest and increases debt |
| **Disposable income** | what is left to spend or save after all taxes and deductions |

Saving means spending **less** than income and allows spending later; borrowing means spending **more** than income and allows spending now.

| A rise in… | Spending | Saving | Borrowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **income** | up | up | up |
| **direct tax** | down | down | either — most cut back, some borrow to keep spending |
| **interest rates** | down | up | down |
| **availability of saving schemes** | down | up | down |
| **availability of credit** | up | down | up |
| **consumer confidence** | up | down | up |

A **fall** in each reverses the effect. **Age, tastes and family circumstances** matter too: income usually rises with experience and people save more as they age; the young spend a larger share on wants; people starting a family spend and borrow more.

*Interest-rate cut, analysed.* Saving falls because the return on it falls and the opportunity cost of spending is lower, so people spend and borrow more. **Investment** rises because borrowing is cheaper, which makes investment projects more profitable.

## Banks and the money market

The **money market** is like any other market: everyone who wants to borrow money, and everyone who supplies it. Its main institutions are **banks** — large, profit-making businesses acting as **intermediaries** between savers and borrowers.

**How banks make a profit:**

- **Charging interest** — the **interest rate** is the cost of borrowing and the bank's reward for risk; riskier loans carry higher rates. A home loan is cheaper because the bank can sell the house if the borrower defaults.
- **Charging fees** — for using other banks' ATMs, overseas transfers, account types, or a direct debit the account cannot cover.
- **Investing** deposits in shares, property and foreign currency.

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **Commercial (retail) bank** | high-street banks with branches: takes deposits, makes loans (home, personal, commercial), offers credit cards and overdrafts, internet banking and electronic transfers, term deposits, KiwiSaver, foreign exchange and insurance |
| **Investment bank** | helps large businesses **raise finance**: advises on mergers and takeovers and helps issue and sell shares on the stock exchange |
| **Credit union** | a not-for-profit **co-operative** owned by its members, giving low-cost loans to people on low incomes whom commercial banks will not serve |
| **Central bank** (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand) | the core of the system, keeping the economy and money supply stable: issues notes and coins; creates money (including by **quantitative easing**); holds the government's tax revenue and makes its payments; manages the national debt; supervises and regulates commercial banks and lends to them in trouble; manages gold and foreign-currency reserves; operates **monetary policy** |

**State-owned and private banks have different goals.** A state-owned bank may aim to give a reliable, safe service, promote government objectives (lending to new firms), and serve people private banks avoid. A private bank aims to maximise profit for shareholders, grow, survive hard times, or satisfice. As commercial banks **grow**, customers may gain lower costs from economies of scale, more innovation and services funded by profit, and greater security for their savings.

## What macroeconomics studies

**Macroeconomics** studies how a **national economy** works: the links between total demand, total output, national income, employment and inflation. Treat the country as one big market. Rising total demand, or falling total supply, tends to push prices up; falling total demand lowers prices but raises unemployment.

**The circular flow of income.** In the basic model, **households** supply factors of production to **firms** and receive incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit); households spend that income on firms' goods and services. Total output is GDP, and the incomes paid for producing it are the national income. The fuller **four-sector** model adds the **government** (taxes out, spending in) and **foreign trade** (imports out, exports in). Total spending — **aggregate demand** — is consumer spending, investment, government spending and exports (less imports), and it buys **aggregate supply**, all the goods and services produced.

The **government** is itself a **producer** (public goods such as street lighting, defence and law and order; merit goods such as education and healthcare) and a large **employer** — the NZ government employs over 400 000 people.

## Government macroeconomic objectives

| Objective | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | a sustained rise in real GDP |
| **High and stable employment** | low, stable unemployment — around 4% is usually considered low |
| **Low and stable inflation** | prices rising slowly and predictably |
| **Stable balance of international trade and payments** | not persistently spending more abroad than is earned |
| **Reducing poverty and inequality** | a fairer distribution of income |
| **Protecting the environment** | reducing pollution and waste |

How many it achieves determines how successful a government looks. **No government achieves them all at once** — the conflicts are at the end of this topic.

**Why each matters.** Without growth, employment, incomes and living standards fall, tax revenue falls and spending is cut, profits fall and entrepreneurs stop investing. Inflation that is too fast reduces consumers' purchasing power, hurts people on low incomes, raises business costs as workers demand higher wages, and makes the country's goods dearer than other countries'. Rising unemployment cuts national output, forces higher welfare spending, and leaves the long-term unemployed with obsolete skills. A balance-of-payments deficit can leave a country short of foreign currency to buy imports and push its exchange rate down.

## GDP and economic growth

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the **monetary value of the goods and services produced within a country over a given period**, usually a year. It is a measure of **output**.

$$\text{GDP} = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $C$ — **consumption** | households' spending on goods and services (transport, holidays, food, clothing) |
| $I$ — **investment** | firms' capital spending that expands productive capacity (machines, factories) |
| $G$ — **government spending** | government consumption and investment (infrastructure, new schools and hospitals) |
| $X$ — **exports** | the value of goods and services sold to foreign buyers (NZ dairy to China) |
| $M$ — **imports** | spending on foreign goods and services (cars, technology); this money leaves the economy, so it is **subtracted** |

**Economic growth** is an increase in national output — the **annual percentage change in real GDP**. It raises the economy's long-term productive capacity, and on a PPC it is an **outward shift**.

**Causes of economic growth:**

- **Factor endowments** — the quantity and quality of land, labour, capital and enterprise (NZ's tourist attractions and dairy farms, Saudi Arabia's oil, Australia's minerals)
- **The labour force** — its size, skill and **mobility** (how easily workers change jobs and move to where the work is)
- **Labour productivity** — output per worker ($\text{GDP} \div \text{workforce}$), raised by education, training and technology; a more productive workforce is more competitive internationally
- **Investment** in capital, which increases productive capacity

| Benefits of growth | Drawbacks of growth |
|---|---|
| more goods and services to satisfy needs and wants | technology may replace workers, raising unemployment |
| more business opportunity, sales and profit | unsustainable depletion of resources |
| higher employment | negative externalities such as pollution |
| low, stable inflation if output keeps pace with demand | risk of inflation if demand outruns capacity |
| more government revenue | growing inequality if the gains are not shared |
| a higher standard of living overall | |

**Negative growth** means output is shrinking: fewer goods and services, falling incomes and living standards. It may be short (a recession) or prolonged (a slump).

## Nominal and real GDP

**Nominal GDP** is measured at **current prices**, so it rises whenever prices rise, even if output does not. People may not be better off — they may even be worse off. **Real GDP** removes the effect of inflation, so it shows whether output genuinely changed.

$$\text{real GDP} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{GDP deflator}} \qquad \text{where the deflator} = 1 + \text{inflation since the base year}$$

Equivalently, $\text{real GDP} = \text{nominal GDP} \div (\text{price index}/100)$.

*Worked example 1.* Prices have risen 1% since the base year, so the deflator is 1.01. Nominal GDP of \$1 000 000 gives real GDP of $1\,000\,000 \div 1.01 = \$990\,099$.

*Worked example 2.* Nominal GDP rises from \$100bn to \$110bn while prices rise 10%. Real GDP is $110 \div 1.10 = \$100$bn — output has **not grown at all**; the whole rise was inflation.

**Real GDP per head (per capita)** divides real GDP by the population, giving average income per person. It allows for population change: if GDP grows more slowly than the population, average income is **falling**. With real GDP of \$100bn and a population of 5 million, real GDP per head is $100\,000\,000\,000 \div 5\,000\,000 = \$20\,000$. Real figures are more reliable than nominal ones.

**Never claim people are better off because nominal GDP rose.** Check prices, then check population.

**Uses of real GDP data:** governments use it to make policy (if too many consumer goods and too few capital goods are being made, it might tax consumer goods more and machinery less), to compare living standards from year to year, and to compare with other countries and regions. In NZ the tertiary (service) sector produces about two-thirds of GDP, the secondary sector about a fifth, and the primary sector well under a tenth.

## Growth and welfare

**Economic welfare** is the level of prosperity and the quality of living standards in an economy. GDP measures growth in money terms and is linked to quality of life, but it is **not** the same thing:

- it ignores **non-market activity** — voluntary work, work at home, illegal trade — so it understates true output
- it says nothing about **sustainability** — whether the growth damages the environment or uses up resources
- it ignores **inequality** — GDP per head is an average, and the gains may go to a small elite
- it ignores **what** is produced (weapons count the same as hospitals) and what people buy with their income
- it ignores **negative externalities**, leisure, health and security

So measure welfare with **more than one indicator**: real GDP per head, the **Human Development Index**, the **Gini coefficient**, and measures such as literacy, doctors per head and pollution. These are explained in [Development, population and poverty](../notes/development.md).

**Economic development** is an improvement in quality of life and living standards — literacy, life expectancy, healthcare. Growth usually **enables** development, because higher real GDP can fund health and education, but the link is **not guaranteed**: the proceeds can be wasted or kept by a wealthy few.

## The economic cycle

The **economic cycle** (or business cycle) is the pattern of recurring ups and downs in **real GDP** over time around its long-term growth trend. It usually takes **5–10 years** to go round, and some stages are more severe than others. Governments want long-term, stable growth, but the annual growth rate fluctuates considerably.

| Stage | What is happening |
|---|---|
| **Growth** | economic activity grows rapidly; sales, revenue and profits rise; new businesses start; output, incomes and employment grow |
| **Boom / peak** | demand, sales and profits peak; demand exceeds supply, so **inflation** rises; the economy **overheats**, with shortages of materials and equipment raising costs; unemployment is low, so wages rise as firms compete for skilled workers; interest rates are likely raised to slow spending |
| **Recession / downturn** | activity slows and growth may turn **negative**; demand and profits fall, production slows, redundancies raise unemployment and cut incomes; firms cut investment; competition intensifies; **disinflation** as prices are cut to attract buyers; interest rates are likely cut |
| **Slump** | the trough — a deep, prolonged recession |
| **Recovery / upturn** | confidence returns and spending rises; output, sales, revenue and profit increase; firms hire again, and rising employment boosts spending further |

**Shapes of recession.** **V-shaped** — short and sharp with a quick recovery, often after an external shock such as an earthquake; the best case. **U-shaped** — recovery takes longer; businesses fail, unemployment rises and policy struggles to work. **K-shaped** — some parts recover quickly while others do not: during closed borders, construction recovered strongly while tourism kept suffering, and people owning property and shares saw their wealth rise while others did not.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a **general and sustained rise in the average price level** of goods and services. Not every price has to rise. It is always expressed as a **rate of change per period** — a percentage change per year.

**Hyperinflation** is rapid, excessive inflation — often defined as above **50% a month** (Germany in the 1920s, Zimbabwe in 2007). People hoard goods, causing shortages; money loses purchasing power, so people spend more to buy less; people stop depositing money, so banks fail; and tax revenue falls, so the government cannot fund essential services. NZ suffered high inflation in the 1970s and 1980s; in 1989 the Reserve Bank was given the job of using monetary policy to hold inflation within a target band (originally 0–3%).

### Measuring inflation — the consumer price index

It is impossible to track every price, so statisticians use the **consumer price index (CPI)**: the price of a **basket** of goods and services commonly bought by the average household. Each item is **weighted** by its share of household spending — if 20% of spending is on food, food has a 20% weighting. Very volatile prices may be excluded, and the basket is revised as spending patterns change. In NZ the three heaviest groups are **housing and household utilities**, **food** and **transport**. The **base year** has an index of **100**.

$$\text{weighted average price} = \sum (\text{weight} \times \text{average price})$$

$$\text{CPI} = \frac{\text{weighted average price this year}}{\text{weighted average price in the base year}}\times100$$

$$\text{inflation rate} = \frac{\text{index}_{\text{new}} - \text{index}_{\text{old}}}{\text{index}_{\text{old}}}\times100$$

*Worked example — building the basket.*

| Group | Base-year weight | Base-year price | Weighted | Year 1 weight | Year 1 price | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Travel and leisure | 15% | \$20 | \$3.00 | 25% | \$22 | \$5.50 |
| Household items | 25% | \$40 | \$10.00 | 25% | \$46 | \$11.50 |
| Clothing and footwear | 40% | \$30 | \$12.00 | 35% | \$38 | \$13.30 |
| Transport | 20% | \$25 | \$5.00 | 15% | \$20 | \$3.00 |
| **Basket** | | | **\$30.00** | | | **\$33.30** |

- Year 1: CPI $= 33.30/30\times100 = 111$ — prices are **11% above the base year**.
- Year 2 basket \$36: CPI $= 36/30\times100 = 120$ — 20% above the base year, and $(120-111)/111\times100 = 8.1\%$ inflation over that single year.
- Year 3 basket \$40.32 → CPI 134.4; year 4 basket \$45.20 → CPI 150.67.

*Chained example.* From a base of 100, prices rise 15% → index 115. A further 10% rise gives $115 + 115\times0.10 = 126.5$, not 125.

*Reading an index.* A CPI of 112 means average prices are **12% higher than in the base year**. If it then reaches 127, prices are 27% above the base year, and they rose $(127-112)/112\times100 = 13.4\%$ between the two years.

**An index of 120 means prices are 20% higher than in the base year — not 120% higher, and not 20% inflation this year.** That is the classic mistake.

**Uses of the CPI:** as an **economic indicator** — governments use it to steer policy and workers use it in pay claims; as a **price deflator** to turn money values into real values; and for **indexation** — linking pay rises, benefits and pensions to the CPI. **Problems:** tastes and fashions change, new products (new technology) become household staples, so the basket must be kept up to date; and no household matches the average.

Accurate measurement matters: if a government **overestimates** inflation it may raise taxes, cut spending or raise interest rates unnecessarily, increasing unemployment and slowing growth; the CPI also drives wage claims and indexed benefits, and reliable figures help firms plan investment.

### Causes of inflation

The general explanation is **"too much money chasing too few goods"**: spending grows faster than firms can produce because the money supply has grown. Governments may let the money supply expand to boost demand in a recession, to meet rising demand, or in response to wage demands. To keep inflation low, the money supply should grow no faster than real GDP.

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Demand-pull** | aggregate demand rises faster than firms can supply, so market prices are bid up |
| **Cost-push** | production costs rise (wages, materials, energy, indirect taxes) and firms pass them on to protect profit margins |
| **Imported** | a fall in the exchange rate (or higher world prices) makes imports dearer, and the rise feeds into domestic prices |

**Higher productivity can reduce inflation:** more output from the same inputs lowers average cost, so firms can cut prices; it also lowers wage cost per unit, reduces waste, and raises profit for investment in cost-cutting capital — all of which reduce cost-push pressure.

### Effects of inflation

| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Consumers** | real incomes fall — the purchasing power of wages falls |
| **Savers** | lose — their money buys less |
| **Borrowers** | gain — the real value of what they owe falls (\$50 000 borrowed for a whole car might later be worth only three-quarters of one) |
| **Fixed and low-income earners** (superannuitants, beneficiaries) | suffer most — they cannot negotiate rises and spend a large share of income |
| **Firms** | costs rise but competitive markets may not allow price rises, so profits fall; uncertainty deters investment; wage demands follow; exporters become less competitive |
| **The economy** | extra costs for firms, less competitive exports, uncertainty |

### Deflation and disinflation

**Deflation** is a **sustained fall** in the general price level. **Benign** deflation comes from an increase in aggregate supply — new technology, supply-side policy, higher productivity — and is good for the economy. **Malign** deflation comes from a fall in aggregate demand, as in a recession, and is damaging:

- consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices, so unsold stock loses value
- firms cut output and staff; household incomes fall and unemployment rises, cutting demand further
- the real value of debt rises; investment stops
- the government must borrow more as tax revenue falls
- a deep recession can follow as the spiral continues

Cutting interest rates helps less than it seems, because the **real** interest rate rises when prices fall: borrow \$1000 at 0% while prices fall 5% a year, and the debt you repay is worth \$1050 in real terms — an effective 5% real interest rate.

**Disinflation is not deflation.** Disinflation is a slowing of the rate of inflation — prices are still rising, just more slowly.

## Employment and unemployment

The **labour force** is people of working age who are **both willing and able** to work. Someone willing but unable, or able but unwilling, is not counted. Those outside it — stay-at-home parents, students, the retired, children, those unable or unwilling to work — are the **dependent population**. In NZ, **full-time** work is 30 hours a week or more.

$$\text{unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{number unemployed}}{\text{labour force}}\times100$$

*Worked example.* A country has a labour force of 35 million and 30 million employed, so 5 million are unemployed: $5/35\times100 \approx 14.3\%$. Another has a labour force of 4.5 million and 3.5 million employed: $1/4.5\times100 \approx 22.2\%$. Divide by the **labour force**, not the population.

Measurement is contested: the figure excludes those unable or unwilling to work, students and the armed forces, and it misses **under-utilisation** — people in part-time or short-hours work who want more hours. So **"unemployed" is not the same as "not in work"**, and politicians can exploit the difference.

| Full-time workers | For the firm | For the worker |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | motivated, skilled staff raise output and quality; loyalty; a reputation that attracts good workers | higher pay, job security, career progression |
| Drawbacks | higher wage costs; burnout; more regulation and paperwork | longer hours, less flexibility, higher expectations |

| Part-time workers | For the firm | For the worker |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | flexible cover for absences; lower costs; no career path to provide | work–life balance, experience, less stress |
| Drawbacks | less motivated and reliable; less loyal; more management time | lower pay, irregular work, fewer benefits, no security |

**Employment by sector** — primary (extracting raw materials), secondary (manufacturing and construction), tertiary (services) — shifts as countries develop. Regions specialise too (Waikato and Canterbury farming, Central Otago wine, Queenstown tourism), so a decline in one industry hits particular regions.

| Type of unemployment | Cause | Policy that fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical** | **low demand** for goods and services in a recession; firms need fewer workers, spending falls further and a downward spiral develops | boost demand — expansionary fiscal or monetary policy |
| **Structural** | the long-term **decline of an industry** (mining, shipbuilding) through changing tastes, behaviour or deindustrialisation leaves workers with the wrong skills or in the wrong place | retraining, relocation help, regional policy |
| **Frictional** | the time between leaving one job and finding the next | better information about vacancies |
| **Seasonal** | jobs exist only at certain times of year (summer fruit pickers, winter ski instructors) | little needed — like frictional unemployment, not a major concern |

There is always **some** unemployment, because people are always between jobs.

**Government policy on unemployment:** manage the economic cycle so downturns are short and shallow; keep inflation low; keep the exchange rate competitive; fund education and retraining for the unemployed; improve information about job opportunities; and reduce unemployment benefits to increase the incentive to work. Other influences: whether high taxes or generous welfare weaken the **incentive to work**; the strength of **trade unions**; **regulation** that raises the cost of employing people; and **labour immobility** — workers unwilling or unable to move to where jobs are.

**Costs of unemployment.** For the individual: lost income and living standards, possible dependence on welfare or charity, depression and damage to health, and skills that become obsolete the longer it lasts. For the government: welfare payments — a **fiscal cost** with an opportunity cost — and lower tax revenue. For the economy: wasted human resources, lower GDP and fewer goods and services, weaker demand, and social problems including crime. Long-term unemployment can cause **relative poverty** — incomes well below the national average (for example below 60% of it) — as savings run out and children's education suffers.

**Low unemployment** is a sign of growth and higher living standards, with more tax revenue for public services — but it can be **inflationary**: with vacancies hard to fill, firms raise pay to attract workers and pass the cost on in prices.

Causes of low unemployment include strong growth, high education and training, high investment and consumer spending, expansionary government policy and low interest rates.

## Fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** is varying **government spending** and **taxation** to influence aggregate demand.

**What the government spends on:** public goods (defence, policing, public roads); merit goods (education, healthcare, libraries); public services (transport, postal, immigration, ambulance services); and welfare — **transfer payments** such as jobseeker support and NZ Superannuation.

**Reasons for taxation:** to fund public spending (the main reason); to manage the macroeconomy (a tax cut raises spending power); to **reduce inequality** by taxing high incomes more; to discourage **imports** and protect domestic firms; and to discourage **harmful products** such as tobacco. The proportion of national income taken in tax is the **tax burden**. Tax is compulsory, though some is avoidable — a non-smoker pays no tobacco tax.

| Principle of a good tax | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Equity** | fair — based on ability to pay |
| **Non-distortionary** | should not distort behaviour, such as destroying the incentive to work |
| **Certainty** | taxpayers know when and how much to pay (PAYE is deducted every payday) |
| **Convenience** | easy and simple to pay, or people will not pay |

| Tax type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | taken directly from income or profit; the burden falls on the person or firm taxed | income tax (PAYE), corporation (company) tax, capital gains tax, inheritance tax (not in NZ) |
| **Indirect** | imposed on spending; the consumer pays the producer, who pays the tax to the government | **GST** (an ad valorem tax, 15% in NZ), petrol tax, **excise duties** (a fixed charge per unit on alcohol and tobacco), **import tariffs**, **user charges** such as road tolls |

| Tax system | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Progressive** | as income rises, the **proportion** taken in tax rises — NZ income tax; it reduces inequality |
| **Proportional (flat rate)** | the **same proportion** at every income — NZ company tax at 28% |
| **Regressive** | as income rises, the **proportion** taken **falls** — unfair to low earners, who lose a larger share of income |

The test is always the **proportion**, never the dollar amount — a rich person paying more dollars under a flat rate is not being taxed progressively.

**Indirect taxes like GST are regressive.** Low earners spend nearly all their income, so nearly all of it is taxed; a high earner who spends only half of their income is taxed on only half.

*Worked example — NZ income tax (the brackets used before the 2024 changes).*

| Annual income band | Rate |
|---|---|
| \$0 – \$14 000 | 10.5% |
| \$14 001 – \$48 000 | 17.5% |
| \$48 001 – \$70 000 | 30% |
| \$70 001 – \$180 000 | 33% |
| over \$180 000 | 39% |

The rates are unchanged in the current brackets, but the thresholds have risen (for 2026–27: \$15 600, \$53 500 and \$78 100, with 39% still above \$180 000). The method is identical — use whichever table a question gives you.

Tax each slice of income at its own rate:

- \$36 000: $14\,000\times0.105 + 22\,000\times0.175 = 1470 + 3850 = \$5320$; effective rate $5320/36\,000 \approx 14.8\%$
- \$75 000: $1470 + 34\,000\times0.175 + 22\,000\times0.30 + 5000\times0.33 = 1470 + 5950 + 6600 + 1650 = \$15\,670$; effective rate $\approx 20.9\%$
- \$500 000: $1470 + 5950 + 6600 + 110\,000\times0.33 + 320\,000\times0.39 = \$175\,120$; effective rate $\approx 35.0\%$

The **effective (average) rate** rises with income, which is what makes the system progressive — but it is always below the top **marginal** rate (39%), because the lower slices are taxed at lower rates. Read which rate a question wants.

| Indirect taxes — advantages | Indirect taxes — disadvantages |
|---|---|
| cheap and easy to collect | regressive — everyone pays the same rate regardless of income |
| everyone pays, including those not working (retired people) | inflationary — they raise prices, which can affect demand |
| can target goods to discourage (or encourage) | revenue is hard to predict |
| flexible — the rate is easy to change | can encourage evasion and black markets |

**Expansionary fiscal policy** — raising government spending or cutting taxes to boost aggregate demand (a new rail link creates jobs and demand during and after construction). Spending usually exceeds revenue, so the budget moves into **deficit**. **Contractionary fiscal policy** — cutting spending or raising taxes to slow the economy and reduce prices, or to cut the deficit and national debt. A **budget surplus** is revenue above spending. Fiscal policy can also **redistribute** income, by taxing high earners more to fund support for low earners.

**Problems with fiscal policy:** the right timing and size are hard to judge, and overshooting overheats the economy into a bust; government borrowing leaves less finance for private firms; higher income and profit taxes may weaken incentives to work; and people may expect inflation and push for higher wages.

## Monetary policy

**Monetary policy** is the central bank's manipulation of **interest rates** (in NZ, the **Official Cash Rate**), the **money supply** and, through them, the **exchange rate**.

| | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | **lowered** | **raised** |
| Borrowing | cheaper, so consumption and investment rise | dearer, so consumption and investment fall |
| Saving | less attractive | more attractive |
| Money supply | increased — including **quantitative easing**, where newly created money buys bonds from banks so they have more to lend | reduced — including selling bonds to banks at attractive rates, so they have less to lend |
| Exchange rate | tends to fall, as foreign savers are less attracted | tends to rise, as higher returns attract foreign savers |
| Used when | the economy is weak or in a slump | the economy is overheating and inflation is rising |

**Exchange-rate policy.** If the NZ dollar falls, imports cost more and the balance of payments can suffer; raising interest rates attracts overseas savers into NZ accounts, raising demand for the NZ dollar and lifting its value. Cutting rates has the opposite effect.

The transmission is not instant or certain. A rate cut only raises spending if households and firms are **confident** and credit is **available**; a heavily indebted household may use lower repayments to pay down debt instead.

## Supply-side policy

**Supply-side policy** aims to increase **aggregate supply** — the economy's **productive capacity** — and make markets work more efficiently, rather than to change demand.

| Measure | How it raises capacity |
|---|---|
| **Tax incentives** | tax relief for setting up in an area or for research and development |
| **Subsidies** | financial help to meet costs and encourage supply (farming) |
| **Deregulation** | easing the rules for starting and running a business, cutting costs and hassle and encouraging investment |
| **Education and training** | an upskilled workforce is more productive and competitive |
| **Competition policy** | prevents monopolies forming and overcharging |
| **Removing trade barriers** | encourages mutually beneficial trade and production |
| **Privatisation** | selling state-owned assets to the private sector, raising revenue and, if private firms are more efficient, better quality at lower cost |
| **Other measures** | cuts in income and company tax, cuts in unemployment benefit, laws reducing union power |

The gains are usually real but **slow** — training a workforce or building infrastructure takes years — and the spending has an opportunity cost.

**Will higher government spending increase growth?** It may: it raises aggregate demand and output; spending on education, training and health raises productivity and long-run capacity; infrastructure cuts firms' costs; and research spending raises potential. It may not: it may cause inflation and make exports uncompetitive; it may be offset by lower private spending or higher taxes; education spending may not raise quality; higher benefits may weaken work incentives; and the money may be spent wastefully.

## Conflicts between objectives

| Conflict | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Growth vs price stability** | if demand rises faster than resources, the result is higher prices rather than more output and jobs |
| **Full employment vs price stability** | lower unemployment means higher incomes and demand (demand-pull) and less spare labour, so wage demands rise — a wage–price spiral |
| **Growth vs the environment** | more output and consumption mean more pollution and habitat destruction |
| **Growth vs the balance of payments** | rising incomes raise spending on imports, worsening the current account |
| **Redistribution vs incentives** | higher taxes and benefits reduce inequality but may weaken the incentive to work and invest |
| **Fiscal expansion vs sound public finances** | boosting demand through spending enlarges the deficit and the national debt |

**The starting position decides the outcome.** A demand boost in a deep recession, with spare capacity and unemployed workers, mainly raises **output**. The same boost at full capacity mainly raises **prices**. Always say which case you are in, and add that policies work with a **time lag**, so a measure can arrive after the problem it was meant to solve has passed.

## Practice

1. Explain the difference between saving and borrowing. *Model points:* saving is income not spent, borrowing is money obtained from a lender; saving means spending less than income, borrowing more; saving builds wealth and earns interest, borrowing builds debt and costs interest; saving allows spending later, borrowing spending now.
2. The CPI was 127 in year Y. What does this show? *Model points:* average prices have risen 27% compared with the base year (and, if it was 112 in year X, by 13.4% between X and Y).
3. Discuss whether a government should raise indirect taxes and cut direct taxes. *Model points:* for — stronger incentives to work, lower poverty traps, taxes on demerit goods cut external costs; against — indirect taxes are regressive so inequality worsens, both changes may be inflationary, tax cuts may be saved or spent on imports, and indirect taxes can be avoided so revenue may fall; conclude on the government's priority.
4. Analyse two possible conflicts between government aims. *Model points:* use two rows of the conflicts table, each with its full causal chain.
