# Demand, supply and the market

## Demand

**Demand** is the quantity consumers are **willing and able** to buy at each price over a stated period. Both words matter — wanting a Ferrari is not demand.

**The law of demand:** as price rises, quantity demanded falls, so the demand curve slopes **downward**. Two reasons: the good becomes dearer relative to substitutes, and the consumer's real income buys less.

## Conditions of demand — what shifts the curve

A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the curve. Anything else causes a **shift**.

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Income** | rises → more demand for a **normal** good; **less** demand for an **inferior** good |
| **Tastes and fashion** | a good becoming fashionable shifts demand right |
| **Price of a substitute** | a substitute's price rises → demand for this good rises |
| **Price of a complement** | a complement's price rises → demand for this good falls |
| **Population** | a larger or differently structured population shifts demand |
| **Advertising** | successful campaigns shift demand right |
| **Expectations** | expecting a future price rise shifts demand right now |
| **Interest rates** | lower rates raise demand for credit-financed goods |

**Normal good** — demand rises with income (restaurant meals).
**Inferior good** — demand *falls* as income rises, because consumers switch to better alternatives (bus travel, supermarket own-brand).
**Substitutes** — one can replace the other (tea and coffee); their prices move demand in the **same** direction.
**Complements** — consumed together (printers and ink); their prices move demand in **opposite** directions.

## Supply

**Supply** is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a stated period.

**The law of supply:** as price rises, quantity supplied rises, so the supply curve slopes **upward** — a higher price makes production more profitable and covers the higher cost of extra output.

## Conditions of supply — what shifts the curve

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Costs of production** | wages, materials or energy rise → supply shifts **left** |
| **Technology** | improvement raises output per input → supply shifts right |
| **Indirect taxes** | a tax on the producer acts like a cost → supply shifts left |
| **Subsidies** | a payment to the producer → supply shifts right |
| **Number of producers** | more firms in the market → supply shifts right |
| **Weather / natural events** | a drought shifts agricultural supply left |
| **Price of a related good** | if another crop becomes more profitable, supply of this one falls |

## Movement vs shift

This distinction is the single most common source of lost marks.

- The good's **own price** changed → **movement along** the existing curve (an *extension* or *contraction*).
- **Anything else** changed → the whole curve **shifts** (an *increase* or *decrease* in demand or supply).

Never say "demand increased because the price fell". A price fall causes an **extension of demand**, not an increase in demand.

## Equilibrium

**Equilibrium** is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At that price the market clears — there is no shortage and no surplus, so there is no pressure on price to change.

| Price | Situation | Pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Below equilibrium | **excess demand** (shortage): buyers want more than producers supply | price pushed **up** |
| Above equilibrium | **excess supply** (surplus): unsold stock | price pushed **down** |

Equilibrium is a point of balance, not of fairness. It does **not** mean everyone can afford the product, or that the allocation is socially desirable.

## Effects of shifts

| Change | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | rises | rises |
| Demand decreases | falls | falls |
| Supply increases | falls | rises |
| Supply decreases | rises | falls |

**When both shift, one outcome becomes ambiguous.** If demand and supply both increase, quantity definitely rises but price depends on which shift is larger. Say so, and state what it depends on — that is the evaluation mark.

## Numerical equilibrium

Let $Q_d = 100 - 2P$ and $Q_s = 20 + 2P$.

Set them equal: $100 - 2P = 20 + 2P \Rightarrow 80 = 4P \Rightarrow P = 20$, and $Q = 60$.

If demand rises to $Q_d = 120 - 2P$: $120 - 2P = 20 + 2P \Rightarrow 100 = 4P \Rightarrow P = 25$, $Q = 70$.

Always substitute back into **both** equations to check.

## Price controls

**Maximum price (price ceiling)** — set *below* equilibrium to make a good affordable (rent controls, basic food).

- Consequence: **excess demand**, so shortages, queues, waiting lists, rationing and black markets.
- Producers supply less, so quality may fall and investment dries up.

**Minimum price (price floor)** — set *above* equilibrium to protect producers or discourage consumption (farm support prices, minimum alcohol pricing, minimum wage).

- Consequence: **excess supply**, so surpluses the government may have to buy and store; in a labour market, unemployment.

Both controls prevent the price mechanism from clearing the market. Evaluate them by who gains, who loses, and whether the intended group actually benefits.

## Pricing strategies

Firms do not simply accept a market price. Common strategies:

| Strategy | Method | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** | add a mark-up to unit cost | simple, but ignores demand |
| **Penetration** | low launch price to build market share | price-sensitive competitive markets |
| **Skimming** | high launch price, lowered later | new or technically advanced goods |
| **Competitive** | match or undercut rivals | similar products in close competition |
| **Price discrimination** | different prices to different groups for the same good | where groups can be separated and have different elasticities (off-peak fares, student tickets) |

Which strategy raises revenue depends on **price elasticity of demand** — see the next topic.
