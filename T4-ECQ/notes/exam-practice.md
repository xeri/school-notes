# Exam practice: data response and 20-mark questions

This page covers Paper 2: one worked data-response question (Section B) and two full structured questions (Section C), each with its mark allocation and a model answer. Paper 1, the 30 multiple-choice questions, has its own timed paper in [MCQ practice](../notes/mcq-practice.md). Try each part yourself first: cover the answer, write, then compare. At exam pace each 20-mark question takes about 32 minutes. The mark allocations follow the style of the Cambridge IGCSE 0455 Paper 2 mark schemes (2025 and 2026), which the school's exam is modelled on; the chains behind every answer are in [Policy chains](../notes/policy-chains.md).

**How the marks work** (from the 0455 mark schemes):

| Part | How it is marked |
|---|---|
| *identify / state / define* | 1 mark per correct point; only the first answers given are considered |
| *explain two…* [4] | 1 mark for each reason identified + 1 for each explanation |
| *draw a diagram* [4] | 1 labelled axes; 1 original curves labelled; 1 correct shift with label or arrow; 1 both equilibria ($P_1$/$Q_1$ and $P_2$/$Q_2$, or $E_1$ and $E_2$). No equilibrium mark without a correct shift. |
| *analyse* [5–6] | 1 mark per explained link; **a list of unexplained points is capped at 3** |
| *discuss* [6] (data question) | up to 4 marks for each side |
| *discuss* [8] (structured) | levels: **Level 3, 6–8** — both sides developed, with thoughtful evaluation; **Level 2, 3–5** — relevant but lacking depth, or one-sided; **Level 1, 1–2** — simple definitions or terms. The school's own scheme gives up to 5 a side plus 2 for a detailed evaluative conclusion. |

A point is credited **once**: reversing an argument you already made ("tax revenue may rise because incomes rise" after "tax revenue may fall because incomes fall") earns nothing. Give the other side a **different** reason.

## Data response: New Zealand's economy (20 marks)

**Source.** *The figures below are illustrative — they follow the broad pattern of New Zealand's economy in recent years but are not official statistics. Use real Stats NZ and Reserve Bank figures only if your teacher gives them.*

Between 2021 and 2023 New Zealand faced its highest inflation in three decades. The Reserve Bank raised the Official Cash Rate (OCR) sharply, mortgage rates rose and house prices fell. By 2024 inflation had returned to the Reserve Bank's 1–3% target band, but unemployment had risen and many firms reported falling sales. The Reserve Bank then cut the OCR. The New Zealand dollar weakened, which exporters of dairy, meat and fruit welcomed, while importers complained of rising costs.

**Table 1: New Zealand economic indicators, 2021–2025 (illustrative)**

| Indicator | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation (annual % change in CPI) | 5.9 | 7.2 | 4.7 | 2.2 | 3.0 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | 3.2 | 3.4 | 4.0 | 5.1 | 5.4 |
| OCR at the end of the year (%) | 0.75 | 4.25 | 5.50 | 4.25 | 2.25 |
| Current account balance (% of GDP) | −6.0 | −8.8 | −6.9 | −6.0 | −4.0 |
| Exchange rate (US\$ per NZ\$1, annual average) | 0.71 | 0.64 | 0.61 | 0.61 | 0.58 |

### (a) Identify the year in which inflation was highest. [1]

**2022** (7.2%) (1).

### (b) Calculate the percentage change in the value of the NZ dollar against the US dollar between 2021 and 2025. [2]

$\dfrac{0.58 - 0.71}{0.71}\times100$ (1) $= -18.3\%$ — a fall of about 18% (1).

*Divide by the original (2021) value. The answer alone, correct, earns both marks; a wrong answer with the right method earns 1.*

### (c) Explain one reason why the NZ dollar may have fallen between 2023 and 2025. [2]

The OCR was cut from 5.50% to 2.25% (1), so saving in New Zealand earned a lower return and foreign savers moved their money elsewhere, reducing demand for NZ dollars (1).

*Other creditable reasons: weaker demand for NZ exports; New Zealanders buying more imports (supplying more NZ\$); speculation that the NZ dollar would fall.*

### (d) Draw a demand and supply diagram to show the effect of the change in (c) on the foreign exchange market for NZ dollars. [4]

Draw: vertical axis "price of NZ\$ in US\$ (exchange rate)", horizontal axis "quantity of NZ\$" (1); original $D_1$ and $S$ labelled (1); demand shifted **left** to $D_2$ with an arrow (1); equilibria marked — $ER_1$/$Q_1$ and the lower $ER_2$/$Q_2$, or $E_1$ and $E_2$ (1). See [The foreign exchange market](../DIAGRAMS.md#the-foreign-exchange-market).

*No mark for the equilibria unless the shift is correct.*

### (e) Analyse the relationship between the OCR and the inflation rate shown in Table 1. [5]

- **Relationship** (up to 2): an **inverse** relationship (1), but **with a time lag** — a higher OCR is followed by lower inflation a year or two later (1).
- **Evidence** (up to 2): the OCR rose from 0.75% in 2021 to 5.50% in 2023 (1), and inflation fell from 7.2% in 2022 to 2.2% in 2024 (1).
- **Explanation** (up to 2): a higher OCR raises mortgage and loan rates, so households and firms borrow and spend less (1); aggregate demand falls, reducing demand-pull inflation (1).
- **Exception** (up to 2): in 2022 the OCR **and** inflation both rose (1) — because the OCR was raised in response to inflation and takes time to work (1).

*Model answer:* The data show an inverse relationship between the OCR and inflation, with a time lag. The OCR rose from 0.75% in 2021 to 5.50% in 2023, and inflation fell from its peak of 7.2% in 2022 to 2.2% in 2024. A higher OCR raises borrowing costs, so consumers and firms spend less, aggregate demand falls and demand-pull inflation eases. The exception is 2022, when both rose, because the Reserve Bank was raising the OCR in response to high inflation and the effect takes a year or more to arrive. When the OCR was cut in 2025, inflation rose slightly to 3.0%, which fits the same relationship.

### (f) Discuss whether the fall in the value of the NZ dollar will benefit New Zealand's economy. [6]

**Model answer.**

The fall may benefit the economy because New Zealand's exports become cheaper in foreign currency. Dairy, meat and fruit exporters can sell more abroad, raising export revenue, output and jobs in those industries; as their workers spend their incomes, aggregate demand and economic growth rise, which is useful when unemployment has risen to 5.4%. At the same time imports become dearer in NZ dollars, so New Zealanders switch towards domestic products, and the current account deficit should narrow — the data show it improving from −8.8% of GDP in 2022 to −4.0% in 2025.

However, the fall may not benefit the economy. New Zealand imports essential goods such as fuel and machinery, for which demand is price inelastic, so the import bill may rise rather than fall. Dearer imports raise firms' costs and push up prices — imported, cost-push inflation — and inflation did rise from 2.2% to 3.0% in 2025. Consumers' real incomes fall, and exporters who use imported inputs lose part of their price advantage. The benefit also takes time, because export contracts are fixed in the short run.

Overall, the fall is likely to benefit an export-oriented economy like New Zealand's while unemployment is high and there is spare capacity, but the benefit depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports and on whether the extra inflation pushes the Reserve Bank to raise the OCR again.

*Marking:* up to 4 for why it will (exports cheaper → more sold → jobs and growth; imports dearer → current account improves), up to 4 for why it may not (inelastic import demand; imported inflation; dearer inputs; time lags). Using Table 1 figures as evidence strengthens both sides.

## Structured question 1: externalities and tax (20 marks)

*Traffic in a large city causes congestion and air pollution. The government is considering raising the tax on petrol and using the revenue to subsidise buses and trains.*

### (a) Define a negative externality. [2]

A cost of production or consumption (1) that falls on a **third party** not involved in the transaction and is not paid for by the producer or consumer (1).

### (b) Explain two external costs of car use. [4]

- **Air pollution** (1): exhaust fumes damage the health of people living near busy roads, who did not choose to drive and are not compensated (1).
- **Congestion** (1): each extra car slows every other road user, wasting their time and raising firms' delivery costs (1).

*Also creditable: noise; accidents injuring pedestrians; greenhouse-gas emissions and climate change; road damage paid for by taxpayers.*

### (c) Analyse, using a demand and supply diagram, how a tax on petrol could reduce the external costs of car use. [6]

**Model answer.** A tax on petrol is an indirect tax paid by fuel sellers, so it raises their costs of supply (1). The supply curve of petrol shifts to the left from $S_1$ to $S_2$ (1), raising the equilibrium price from $P_1$ to $P_2$ and reducing the quantity demanded from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$ (1). Because petrol is a complement to car use, the higher price makes each journey more expensive, so some drivers make fewer trips, share cars or switch to public transport (1). Fewer car journeys mean less congestion and less air pollution, so the external costs fall (1). Ideally the tax equals the external cost per unit, raising the supply curve from the private cost (MPC) to the social cost (MSC) so that output falls to the socially optimum level (1).

**Diagram** (credited within the 6): price on the vertical axis, quantity of petrol per week on the horizontal; $D$ and $S_1$; $S_2$ shifted left/up with an arrow; $P_1$, $P_2$, $Q_1$, $Q_2$ marked. Or use [the tax-correction diagram](../notes/policy-chains.md#1-indirect-tax-on-a-demerit-good-or-a-good-with-external-costs).

*Note: listing effects without the links caps this part at 3 marks.*

### (d) Discuss whether subsidising public transport is better than taxing petrol to reduce congestion. [8]

**Model answer.**

Subsidising public transport may be the better policy. A subsidy lowers bus and train operators' costs, shifting supply to the right, so fares fall and more people use public transport; each passenger who leaves their car at home reduces congestion and pollution, an external benefit to every other road user. Unlike a petrol tax, a subsidy is not regressive — it helps low-income households, who rely most on buses — and it gives drivers a real alternative, which matters because without one they simply pay the higher petrol price and keep driving. The school's own guidance makes this point: promoting public transport can be a better answer than simply discouraging car use, especially in a city such as Auckland.

However, a petrol tax may be better. Demand for public transport may be price inelastic if services are slow, infrequent or do not go where people need to travel, so lower fares attract few drivers out of their cars, and much of the subsidy is spent on passengers who already used the bus. A subsidy has an opportunity cost — the money could fund hospitals or schools — and operators may become inefficient if they rely on it. A petrol tax, by contrast, raises revenue rather than costing it, and it acts on every driver, giving all of them a reason to cut unnecessary trips. Its weakness is that demand for petrol is price inelastic in the short run, so congestion may fall only a little, and the tax is regressive.

Overall, neither policy works well on its own. The tax is cheaper for the government but changes behaviour only where drivers have an alternative; the subsidy provides the alternative but costs money and fails if the service is poor. The most effective approach is the one in the stimulus — use the petrol-tax revenue to fund better and cheaper public transport — and its success depends on the price elasticity of demand for petrol and for public transport, and on how good the public-transport network is.

*Why this is Level 3:* both sides are developed with mechanisms (supply shifts, PED, regressive tax, opportunity cost); there is a real context (Auckland, the school's own point); and the conclusion makes a judgement and names what it depends on.

## Structured question 2: exchange rates and protection (20 marks)

*A New Zealand manufacturer of building materials says it cannot compete with cheap imports, especially when the NZ dollar is strong. It has asked the government for a tariff on imported building materials.*

### (a) Define a tariff. [2]

A **tax** (1) on **imported** goods (1).

### (b) Explain two reasons why a government might protect a domestic industry. [4]

- **To protect jobs** (1): if cheap imports force domestic firms to close, workers become unemployed, so protection keeps demand for domestic output and employment (1).
- **To support an infant industry** (1): a new industry has high average costs until it grows large enough to gain economies of scale, so protection gives it time to become competitive (1).

*Also creditable: declining (sunset) industries — to slow job losses; strategic industries (food, energy, defence); anti-dumping; to reduce a current account deficit; to raise revenue.*

### (c) Analyse how an appreciation of the NZ dollar could affect New Zealand's current account. [6]

**Model answer.** An appreciation means each NZ dollar buys more foreign currency (1). NZ exports therefore become more expensive in foreign currency, so foreign buyers demand fewer of them and export volumes fall (1) — and if demand for exports is price elastic, export revenue falls (1). At the same time imports become cheaper in NZ dollars, so New Zealanders buy more imports and fewer domestic substitutes (1), raising spending on imports (1). With export revenue falling and import spending rising, the balance on goods and services worsens, so the current account deficit widens (1). The effect is smaller if demand for exports and imports is inelastic, and it takes time because existing contracts are fixed.

### (d) Discuss whether a government should impose tariffs to protect domestic industries. [8]

**Model answer.**

A tariff may be justified. It raises the price of imported building materials from the world price to the world price plus the tariff, so domestic manufacturers can sell more at a higher price; their output, profits and employment rise, which is valuable if the industry is a large employer in a particular region. If the industry is new, protection can give it time to grow and gain economies of scale so that it can later compete without help. The government also collects tariff revenue, which it can spend on training or infrastructure, and fewer imports may reduce the current account deficit.

However, a tariff also has serious costs. Consumers — here, home builders — pay higher prices and have less choice, and because building materials are an input, construction costs rise, pushing up house prices and adding to cost-push inflation. Sheltered firms face less competition, so they have less incentive to cut costs and innovate, and protection meant to be temporary often becomes permanent. Most importantly, trading partners may retaliate with tariffs on New Zealand's exports; for a small economy that depends on exporting dairy, meat and fruit and on free trade agreements, the jobs lost in exporting industries could easily exceed the jobs saved in manufacturing.

Overall, a government should rarely impose tariffs. They can be justified for a short, fixed period to support a genuine infant industry or to soften the collapse of a declining one, but the case depends on how long the protection lasts, whether the industry can realistically become competitive, and how likely retaliation is. In this case the problem is partly the strong NZ dollar, which may not last; supply-side support such as training or help with investment would address the manufacturer's competitiveness without raising costs for builders or risking retaliation.

*Why this is Level 3:* both sides carry full chains (tariff → price → domestic output and jobs; tariff → input costs → inflation; retaliation → exports), it uses the stimulus (building materials, the strong dollar), and the conclusion names the conditions and a better alternative.

## How to choose 3 of the 6 structured questions

Use the first 5 minutes of Paper 2. Paper 1 is collected before you see Section C, so the choice happens here. For each question, look **past** the easy (a) and (b) parts.

- [ ] **Read the (c) and (d) parts first** — they carry 14 of the 20 marks.
- [ ] **Can I write the (c) chain?** Say it in your head: at least five linked steps, ending in the effect the question asks about.
- [ ] **Do I know the diagram**, if (c) needs one — every label, without guessing which curve shifts?
- [ ] **Can I give two different reasons on *each* side of (d)**, not one reason and its reverse?
- [ ] **Do I know what the answer depends on** (PED, time lags, spare capacity, cause, cost) for a real conclusion?
- [ ] **Is the context one I can use** — a country, firm or market I can refer to in every paragraph?
- [ ] **Avoid** a question just because its (a) definition is easy, or because the topic sounds familiar but the (d) is on an angle you have not practised.
- [ ] **Decide once, then commit.** After Section B, do your strongest Section C question first to bank marks, and keep to about 32 minutes each: (a) 3, (b) 6, (c) 10, (d) 13.
- [ ] **Number and label as you go.** Write the question number and each part letter in the answer booklet's margin.
