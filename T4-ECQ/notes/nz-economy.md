# The New Zealand economy

This page covers the course-outline area **The NZ Economy**: who makes the decisions in the NZ economy, how it is structured, which institutions run policy and publish the data, and the features that shape almost every NZ case study. It is background for every later topic — the details of each policy are in [Macroeconomics and policy](../notes/macroeconomics.md) and of trade in [International trade and exchange rates](../notes/trade.md). Figures quoted here are rounded and change over time; always use the figures a question gives you.

## Microeconomics and macroeconomics

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Studies | individual markets and decision makers | the whole economy |
| Decision makers | households (consumers and workers), individual firms, the government in one market | the government and central bank, with households, firms and the rest of the world in aggregate |
| Typical questions | why has the price of avocados risen? should this firm raise its price? what sets a builder's wage? | why is inflation rising? why has unemployment fallen? is the economy growing? |
| NZ example | the Auckland housing market; the market for dairy farm workers | the inflation rate, the unemployment rate, GDP growth, the current account balance |

## The four sectors of the economy

| Sector | Its role | NZ examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Households** | supply land, labour, capital and enterprise; receive incomes; spend, save and pay tax | about 5 million people in about 2 million households |
| **Firms** | combine the factors of production to produce goods and services; pay incomes; invest | Fonterra, Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, a local dairy (corner shop) |
| **Government** | taxes, spends, regulates, produces public and merit goods, and employs — over 400 000 people | central government, the Reserve Bank, local councils |
| **The overseas sector** | buys NZ exports and sells NZ imports; invests in NZ and receives investment from it | China, Australia and the USA as the largest trading partners |

The money flowing between them is the **circular flow of income**, explained in [Macroeconomics and policy](../notes/macroeconomics.md). Leakages from the flow are **saving, taxes and imports**; injections are **investment, government spending and exports**.

## A mixed economy

New Zealand is a **mixed economy**: most goods and services are produced by **private-sector** firms for profit and allocated by the price mechanism, while the **public sector** provides public and merit goods (defence, police, state schools, public hospitals), welfare and infrastructure, and regulates markets.

The balance has moved over time. Until the early 1980s NZ was one of the most regulated economies in the developed world — with import licensing, farm subsidies, price and wage controls and a fixed exchange rate. From 1984 governments **deregulated**: the NZ dollar was floated in March 1985, farm subsidies and most import controls were removed, many state trading activities were corporatised as **state-owned enterprises (SOEs)** and some were privatised. The Reserve Bank was made independent and given an inflation target in 1989. The economy is now one of the more open, market-based economies in the OECD, with a large welfare state alongside.

| Ownership | Meaning | NZ examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Private sector** | owned by individuals or shareholders; aims mainly at profit | supermarkets, banks, most farms, Fonterra (a farmer-owned co-operative) |
| **Public sector** — government departments | run directly by the state to provide a service | the Ministry of Education, Inland Revenue, the police |
| **Public sector** — state-owned enterprises and Crown entities | owned by the government but run on commercial lines | KiwiRail, NZ Post, Kiwibank (government-owned through a holding company) |
| **Mixed ownership** | partly listed on the stock exchange, with the government keeping a majority | Air New Zealand, and the large electricity generators partly sold in 2013–14 |

## The structure of the NZ economy

**Classifying firms by sector.** Every firm belongs to one of three sectors of production:

| Sector | Activity | NZ examples | Share of NZ output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | extracting or growing natural resources | dairy and sheep farming, forestry, fishing, horticulture, mining | well under a tenth of GDP — but most of the value of NZ's **goods** exports |
| **Secondary** | manufacturing and construction — turning raw materials into products | dairy processing, timber products, house building | about a fifth |
| **Tertiary** | services | tourism, retail, banking, education, health, IT | about two-thirds |

As economies develop, the share of output and jobs in the primary sector falls and the tertiary sector grows; NZ follows the developed-country pattern, except that its exports remain unusually dependent on primary products.

**NZ's place in the world economy.**

- **Small and open.** NZ produces a narrow range of goods and imports most manufactured goods, fuel and machinery, so trade (exports plus imports) is a large share of GDP.
- **Export-dependent on a few commodities.** Dairy, meat, forestry products, fruit and wine — and, in services, **tourism** and international education. A fall in the world dairy price or a slowdown in China hits farm incomes, the exchange rate and growth.
- **Main partners:** China, Australia, the USA, the EU and Japan.
- **A long-running current-account deficit**, financed by overseas borrowing and investment — see [International trade and exchange rates](../notes/trade.md).
- **A floating exchange rate** that moves with dairy prices, interest-rate differences and global risk sentiment.
- **Exposure to shocks**: earthquakes (Christchurch 2011), cyclones (Gabrielle 2023), biosecurity threats to farming, and border closures that hit tourism (2020–22).
- **Long-term issues** usually cited: relatively low labour productivity growth, housing affordability, an ageing population and the cost of NZ Superannuation, and the environmental cost of intensive farming.

## Who runs economic policy and who measures it

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **The government (Cabinet) and Parliament** | sets taxes and spending in the annual **Budget** (fiscal policy), passes laws, sets the minimum wage each year |
| **The Treasury** | the government's economic adviser; prepares the Budget and forecasts |
| **Inland Revenue (IRD)** | collects taxes — PAYE income tax, GST, company tax |
| **The Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ)** | the **central bank**: sets the **Official Cash Rate (OCR)** to keep CPI inflation within its **1–3% target** range over the medium term (aiming at 2%); regulates banks; issues notes and coins |
| **Stats NZ** | publishes the official data — the **CPI** (quarterly), the **Household Labour Force Survey** (quarterly unemployment), GDP (quarterly), the balance of payments and the census |
| **Ministry of Social Development (MSD)** | pays benefits such as Jobseeker Support and NZ Superannuation |
| **The Commerce Commission** | enforces competition and consumer law — mergers, cartels, misuse of market power |
| **Local councils** | collect **rates** (a property tax), provide water, local roads, parks and libraries, and make land-use rules |

## The role of government — locally, nationally and internationally

| Level | What the government does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Locally** | provides local public goods; regulates land use and building; charges rates | Auckland Council's planning rules affect how many houses can be built — see the housing case in [Elasticity, market failure and market structures](../notes/elasticity-failure.md) |
| **Nationally** | pursues the macroeconomic aims (growth, low unemployment, stable prices, a stable balance of payments, fairer income distribution); corrects market failure; provides public and merit goods; redistributes income through taxes and benefits | the Budget; the OCR; the minimum wage; KiwiSaver |
| **Internationally** | negotiates trade agreements; belongs to international organisations; gives aid; cooperates on shared problems such as climate change | the NZ–China free trade agreement (2008), CPTPP, the WTO; aid to Pacific countries |

## Key NZ settings worth knowing

| Setting | Detail |
|---|---|
| Currency | the NZ dollar (NZD), floating since 1985 |
| GST | 15% on most goods and services — an indirect, ad valorem tax |
| Income tax | progressive, from 10.5% up to a top rate of 39% |
| Company tax | 28%, a proportional (flat) rate |
| Inflation target | CPI inflation of 1–3% a year over the medium term, set in the Reserve Bank's remit |
| Unemployment measure | the Household Labour Force Survey — a labour force survey, not a claimant count |
| Retirement | NZ Superannuation, a universal state pension from age 65; KiwiSaver, a voluntary work-based savings scheme with employer contributions |
| Minimum wage | set by the government and reviewed every year — check the current rate |

## Practice

1. Explain the difference between microeconomics and macroeconomics, using an NZ example of each. *Model points:* micro studies individual markets and decision makers (the market for avocados, a firm's pricing); macro studies the whole economy (NZ's inflation or unemployment rate); each with its decision makers.
2. Explain why NZ is described as a mixed economy. *Model points:* most production by private firms through the price mechanism; government provides public and merit goods, welfare and regulation; examples of each; the post-1984 reforms moved it towards the market but did not remove the state.
3. Analyse why the NZ economy is vulnerable to a fall in world dairy prices. *Model points:* dairy is the largest goods export; lower prices cut export revenue and farm incomes; farmers spend and invest less, so demand and jobs in rural regions fall; lower demand for NZ dollars may depreciate the currency; the current account worsens; growth slows.
4. Identify the organisation responsible for each: setting the OCR; measuring the CPI; collecting GST. *Answer:* the Reserve Bank; Stats NZ; Inland Revenue.
