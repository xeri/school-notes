# International trade and exchange rates

## Specialisation and the gains from trade

Countries, like workers, can **specialise** — concentrate resources on what their land, climate, skills and capital suit them to. Specialisation raises total output, but it leaves a country short of everything it has stopped producing, so it must **trade**. That is the whole basis of international trade.

**Why countries trade:**

- to obtain goods they cannot produce at all (climate, minerals, land)
- to obtain goods others produce more cheaply or to a higher standard
- to sell a surplus and earn foreign currency
- to widen consumer choice and increase competition
- to reach markets larger than the domestic one, allowing economies of scale
- to build political and diplomatic ties — trading partners rarely fight

**Globalisation** is the process by which economies become increasingly interconnected through trade, investment, migration and the flow of information. It is driven by cheaper transport, faster communication and rising incomes.

| | Benefits | Risks and costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | more choice, lower prices | jobs lost if production moves abroad |
| **Producers** | larger markets, cheaper inputs, scale economies, shared technology | stronger foreign competition, thinner margins |
| **Governments** | higher GDP, higher tax revenue, better living standards | harder to tax and regulate multinationals; environmental and adjustment costs |

The large risk is **over-dependence**: a country specialised in one or two exports is exposed to a fall in world demand or price for them, and the **adjustment costs** of trade fall unevenly — on particular workers, industries and regions, not on the nation as a smooth average. Say who bears the cost; that is where evaluation marks are.

## Absolute and comparative advantage

| | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute advantage** | a country can produce more of a good from the **same** resources than another can | compare output directly |
| **Comparative advantage** | a country has a **lower opportunity cost** of producing a good | compare what must be given up |

*Worked example.* With half its resources in each industry:

| | Beef | Tobacco |
|---|---|---|
| Country A | 250 | 200 |
| Country B | 100 | 150 |

Country A has an **absolute advantage in both** goods — it produces more of each. But look at opportunity cost:

- For A, 200 tobacco costs 250 beef, so 1 tobacco costs $250/200 = 1.25$ beef.
- For B, 150 tobacco costs 100 beef, so 1 tobacco costs $100/150 = 0.67$ beef.

B sacrifices **less** beef per unit of tobacco, so B has the **comparative advantage in tobacco** and A in beef. If each specialises accordingly, total output of both goods rises, and at any exchange rate between 0.67 and 1.25 beef per tobacco **both** countries end with more of both goods than before.

**A country with an absolute disadvantage in everything can still gain from trade.** That is the whole point of comparative advantage — and the result holds only while the opportunity-cost ratios differ, transport costs are not prohibitive, and resources can actually be switched between industries.

## Multinational companies

An **MNC** has its headquarters in one country (the **home** country) and operations in others (**host** countries).

| | Host country gains | Host country loses |
|---|---|---|
| | employment, training and transferred skills | domestic firms may be unable to compete and close |
| | capital investment and a contribution to GDP | profits may be sent home rather than reinvested |
| | competition pressures local firms to improve | wages and conditions may be exploited where labour law is weak |
| | more consumer and business choice | transfer pricing and tax avoidance reduce tax revenue |
| | tax revenue on profits and sales | loss of local culture and local control |

The home country gains export markets, growth and profit inflows, but may lose investment and jobs to the host.

## Free trade and protectionism

**Free trade** is the removal of trade barriers so goods and services move freely. **Protectionism** is restricting trade in order to shield domestic producers.

| Instrument | How it works | Immediate effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Tariff** | an indirect tax on imports | import price rises, quantity of imports demanded falls, domestic sales rise, government earns revenue |
| **Quota** | a physical limit on the quantity imported | import supply restricted, so the price rises — but **no** tax revenue for the government |
| **Subsidy to domestic producers** | a payment that lowers their costs | domestic supply shifts right, domestic price falls, imports are undercut |
| **Administrative barriers** | paperwork, licensing, safety and labelling standards, slow customs | raises the cost and delay of importing without an explicit tax |
| **Embargo** | a total ban on importing a good | imports fall to zero |

| Argument **for** protection | The counter-argument |
|---|---|
| **Infant industry** — a new industry needs shelter until it reaches efficient scale | protection may become permanent and the industry never grows up |
| **Employment** — protection saves jobs in the protected industry | jobs are lost elsewhere as input costs rise and exports face retaliation |
| **Dumping** — foreign firms selling below cost to destroy local rivals | genuinely low foreign cost is not dumping; proving dumping is hard |
| **Strategic industries** — food, energy and defence should not depend on foreign supply | security has a price, paid by consumers in higher prices |
| **Declining (sunset) industries** need time to adjust | delaying adjustment keeps resources in low-productivity use |
| **Correcting a trade imbalance** | treats the symptom, not the cause of uncompetitiveness |
| **Retaliation** — others protect, so we must | this is how a trade war starts |

**Costs of protection:** consumers pay higher prices and have less choice; firms using imported inputs face higher costs and lower profits; inefficient domestic firms are kept alive, wasting resources; and **retaliation** raises the price of this country's exports, cutting export volumes and employment in the export sector.

A **free trade agreement** between countries removes tariffs, bans subsidies, simplifies customs procedures and eases the movement of businesspeople. A **customs union** goes further, applying a common external tariff.

The judgement on protection depends on **how long** it lasts, **which** industry it covers and whether that industry can realistically become competitive, **how likely retaliation** is, and whether a cheaper alternative (training, infrastructure, investment support) would achieve the same aim.

## Exchange rates

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. It is set in the **foreign exchange market** by the demand for and supply of the currency.

| Who **demands** this currency | Who **supplies** this currency |
|---|---|
| foreigners buying its exports | residents buying imports |
| foreigners investing in it or saving in its banks | residents investing abroad |
| tourists visiting the country | residents travelling abroad |
| speculators expecting it to rise | speculators expecting it to fall |

| Determinant | Effect on the currency |
|---|---|
| **Relative interest rates** rise | **appreciates** — foreign savers seek the higher return |
| **Demand for exports** rises | **appreciates** — more of the currency is needed to pay for them |
| **Demand for imports** rises | **depreciates** — more of the currency is supplied to buy foreign currency |
| **Relative inflation** rises | **depreciates** — exports become uncompetitive, so demand for the currency falls |
| **Inward foreign direct investment** rises | **appreciates** (and the investor's own currency depreciates) |
| **Speculation** that it will rise | **appreciates**, as buyers move in early |
| **Central bank** buys its own currency | **appreciates**; selling reserves of it causes a depreciation |

**Appreciation** is a rise in the currency's value — one unit buys **more** foreign currency. **Depreciation** is a fall — one unit buys **less**. Under a fixed system the deliberate equivalents are **revaluation** and **devaluation**.

*Worked conversion.* To convert into a foreign currency, **multiply** by the rate quoted as "1 domestic = $x$ foreign"; to convert back, **divide**.

At 1 domestic unit = 0.60 foreign, a foreign good priced at 120,000 costs $120\,000/0.60 = 200\,000$ domestic. If the rate rises to 0.62 — an **appreciation** — the same good costs $120\,000/0.62 = 193\,548$ domestic. The import has become cheaper. **Write the quotation direction down before you multiply or divide**; getting it upside down is the single commonest arithmetic error in this topic.

| System | How the rate is set | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Floating** | purely by demand and supply, with no intervention | adjusts automatically; no reserves needed; interest rates free for domestic aims | volatile and uncertain for traders and investors |
| **Fixed** | pegged to an anchor currency and defended by the central bank | certainty for trade and investment; disciplines inflation | needs large reserves; interest rates tied to defending the peg; a misaligned peg invites speculative attack |
| **Managed** | influenced by the central bank around a preferred range | combines some stability with some flexibility | still needs reserves; the target may not be credible |

## Effects of an exchange-rate change

Remember the mnemonic: **SPICED** — **S**trong **P**ound, **I**mports **C**heap, **E**xports **D**ear.

| | Appreciation | Depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| Export prices abroad | rise | fall |
| Import prices at home | fall | rise |
| Export volumes | tend to fall | tend to rise |
| Import volumes | tend to rise | tend to fall |
| Firms using imported inputs | gain — cheaper materials | lose — dearer materials, higher costs |
| Inflation | eases — imports are cheaper | rises — imported inflation |
| Employment in export industries | falls | rises |
| Current account | tends to worsen | tends to improve |

**None of this is automatic.** Four reasons:

1. **Elasticity.** A depreciation only raises export *revenue* if demand for exports is price **elastic**; if it is inelastic, the lower price earns less. Likewise imports only fall much if demand for them is elastic — and a country importing essentials it cannot produce has very inelastic import demand.
2. **Time lags.** Existing contracts are priced in advance, so volumes take months or years to respond.
3. **Capacity constraints.** If export factories are already at full capacity, higher foreign demand cannot be met.
4. **Imported inputs.** An exporter that imports its raw materials sees its costs rise with the depreciation, offsetting the competitiveness gain.

A firm facing an unfavourable appreciation can cut prices and accept a lower margin, find cheaper suppliers, raise efficiency, compete on quality and brand rather than price, or shift towards goods with inelastic demand.

## The balance of payments

The **balance of payments** records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. It has three parts: the **current account** (the examinable one), the **capital account** and the **financial account**.

| Current account component | Also called | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance on goods** | the visible trade balance | exports minus imports of physical goods — cars, oil, food, machinery |
| **Balance on services** | the invisible trade balance | tourism, banking, insurance, education, transport |
| **Net primary income** | investment and employment income | interest, dividends and profits from abroad, wages sent home |
| **Net secondary income** | current transfers | aid and grants, migrant remittances, contributions to international organisations |

$$\text{balance of trade} = \text{visible exports} - \text{visible imports}$$
$$\text{current account} = \text{balance on goods} + \text{balance on services} + \text{net primary income} + \text{net secondary income}$$

*Worked example*, in $bn:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Visible exports | 800 |
| Visible imports | 1250 |
| **Balance of trade** | **−450** |
| Invisible exports | 290 |
| Invisible imports | 220 |
| **Balance on services** | **+70** |
| Net primary income | +20 |
| Net secondary income | −60 |
| **Current account balance** | **−420** |

So $-450 + 70 + 20 - 60 = -420$: a current account **deficit** of $420 bn. Note that a **goods-trade deficit is not the same as a current-account deficit** — services and income flows can offset it, and often do.

**Causes of a deficit:** uncompetitive exports (high costs, high relative inflation, poor quality), an overvalued exchange rate, strong domestic income growth pulling in imports, loss of a major export market, a fall in the world price of a key export, or heavy imports of capital equipment during industrialisation.

| A deficit may not matter if… | A deficit is a problem when… |
|---|---|
| it is financed by **equity** investment, which need not be repaid | it is financed by **borrowing**, creating debt and interest obligations |
| the imports are capital goods that raise future productive capacity | the imports are consumption, so nothing is built for the future |
| it is small relative to GDP and temporary | it is large and persistent, so reserves and confidence drain |
| foreign investment brings jobs, technology and growth | foreigners take ownership of a large share of domestic assets |
| consumption of imports raises living standards now | the cause is underlying uncompetitiveness, which worsens over time |

A **surplus** is not automatically good either: it may mean weak domestic demand, it can bring inflationary pressure and currency appreciation, and it invites protectionist retaliation from deficit partners.

**Corrective policies for a deficit:**

| Policy | Mechanism | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Depreciation of the currency** | exports cheaper, imports dearer | depends on elasticities; raises imported inflation |
| **Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy** | lower incomes cut import spending | also cuts growth and raises unemployment |
| **Protectionism** | tariffs and quotas cut imports directly | retaliation; higher input costs; consumer loss |
| **Supply-side policy** | training, infrastructure and investment raise productivity and export quality | slow, and expensive up front |

Supply-side measures attack the cause rather than the symptom, but take years; demand-reducing measures work faster at the cost of growth and jobs. Which is right depends on whether the deficit is **cyclical** (temporary, from strong demand) or **structural** (from persistent uncompetitiveness).
