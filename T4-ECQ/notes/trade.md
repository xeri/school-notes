# International trade and exchange rates

## Globalisation

**Globalisation** is the process by which the world becomes increasingly **interconnected** through massively increased trade and cultural exchange — economic, social, technological, cultural and political changes that increase the interdependence of people, firms and economies.

Its key aspects:

- economies relying on each other for a growing variety of goods and services (NZ imports cars and electronics)
- opportunities to buy and sell in other countries, bringing in foreign currency and more choice
- labour and capital moving more freely around the world
- the growth of global financial markets
- the effect of all this on resource allocation, the balance of payments, growth and living standards

It has been driven by rising wealth, new technology, and faster, cheaper communication. It also shifts economic weight between countries: the USA's share of the world economy has fallen as China and India have grown.

| | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | more choice; lower prices from specialist producers | jobs lost if employers use foreign labour or move production overseas; exploitation of cheap labour in developing countries |
| **Producers** | larger markets and profits; can locate where labour and materials are cheaper; shared knowledge and technology; can hire globally | more overseas competition, so lost sales or lower prices |
| **Governments / countries** | higher production, GDP and living standards; cultural diversity; trade encourages peace — a country is unlikely to attack a valuable trading partner | multinationals are hard to regulate and tax; challenges of immigration; environmental damage from moving people and goods |

## Specialisation and why countries trade

Like workers and firms, **regions and countries specialise** — NZ in dairy, agriculture and tourism; South Korea and Japan in electronics, appliances and cars; Vietnam in clothing and footwear; Brazil and Colombia in coffee. Specialisation leaves a country short of the things it no longer produces, so it must **trade** — this exchange is the backbone of international trade. NZ dairy farmers' expertise exceeds China's, while China makes low-cost technology; each specialises and both gain.

Without trade, countries would have to be **self-sufficient**, spreading resources over a huge variety of goods instead of their most efficient use, so total output would be lower and costs higher. Trade lets countries:

- obtain goods they cannot produce at all (climate, minerals, land)
- obtain goods others make more cheaply or better
- sell a surplus and earn foreign currency
- widen consumer choice and increase competition
- reach bigger markets, giving economies of scale and access to the best workers and resources abroad
- build peaceful relations — and trade sanctions give large economies leverage over hostile governments

The big risk is **over-dependence**: a country specialised in one or two exports is exposed to a fall in world demand or price for them. And the **adjustment costs** of trade fall unevenly — on particular workers, industries and regions, not on the nation as a smooth average. Say who bears the cost; that is where evaluation marks are.

## Absolute and comparative advantage

| | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute advantage** | a country can produce **more** of a good using the **same** factors of production (or the same amount with fewer resources) | compare output directly |
| **Comparative advantage** | a country has a **lower opportunity cost** of producing a good | compare what must be given up |

*Worked example — Australia and Malawi*, each putting half its resources into each industry:

| | Beef | Tobacco |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | 250 | 200 |
| Malawi | 100 | 150 |
| **Total** | **350** | **350** |

Australia has an **absolute advantage in both** goods. But Malawi is 75% as productive as Australia in tobacco (150/200) and only 40% as productive in beef (100/250), so its relative strength is tobacco. Opportunity cost confirms it:

- Australia: 200 tobacco costs 250 beef, so **1 tobacco costs 1.25 beef**.
- Malawi: 150 tobacco costs 100 beef, so **1 tobacco costs 0.67 beef**.

Malawi gives up **less** beef per unit of tobacco, so it has the **comparative advantage in tobacco**; Australia has it in beef.

**After specialising.** Malawi moves all its resources into tobacco (0 beef, 300 tobacco). Australia moves most of its resources into beef (400 beef, 80 tobacco).

| | Beef | Tobacco |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | 400 (+150) | 80 (−120) |
| Malawi | 0 (−100) | 300 (+150) |
| **Total** | **400 (+50)** | **380 (+30)** |

**After trading** 130 beef for 130 tobacco (a rate of 1 for 1, which lies between the two opportunity costs of 0.67 and 1.25):

| | Beef | Tobacco |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | 270 (was 250) | 210 (was 200) |
| Malawi | 130 (was 100) | 170 (was 150) |

**Both countries end with more of both goods.** A country with an absolute disadvantage in everything can still gain from trade — that is the whole point of comparative advantage. The result holds only while the opportunity-cost ratios differ, the trading rate lies between them, transport costs are not prohibitive, and resources can actually be switched between industries. Specialisation must also be planned: had Australia specialised completely in beef (500 beef, 0 tobacco), world tobacco output would have **fallen** to 300.

## Multinational companies

A **multinational company (MNC)** has its headquarters in one country — the **home** country — and operations, factories or assembly plants in others — the **host** countries (Starbucks, IKEA, Toyota, Adidas).

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Host country** | employment and training; transfer of skills and expertise; spending with local suppliers and capital investment add to GDP; competition pushes domestic firms to improve; more consumer and business choice; efficient practices, technology and standards; tax revenue on profits and sales | domestic firms may be unable to compete and close; unethical behaviour where labour law is weak (low wages, poor conditions); imposing foreign culture on local culture; profits sent home rather than reinvested; **transfer pricing** and tax avoidance cut tax revenue |
| **Home country** | markets home-country products worldwide; jobs at home and abroad; growth and development; exports and profits support the balance of payments | capital flows out to host countries; jobs may go to foreign workers; home-country development may be neglected for more profitable investment abroad |

## Free trade and protectionism

**Free trade** is the elimination of trade barriers to create large open markets for goods and services. **Protectionism** is restricting trade with other nations in order to protect domestic firms.

**Why governments are wary of uncontrolled free trade:** job losses in developed countries as firms outsource; rapid depletion of natural resources; faster climate change from transporting goods; exploitation of workers in developing countries; and a widening gap between rich and poor countries.

| Instrument | How it works | Immediate effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Tariff** | an indirect tax (customs duty) on imports | import price rises, quantity of imports demanded falls, domestic sales rise, and the government earns revenue |
| **Import quota** | a limit on the quantity that can be imported | import supply is restricted, so the price rises — but **no** tax revenue |
| **Subsidy to domestic producers** | money paid to cut their costs and encourage supply | domestic supply shifts right and imports are undercut |
| **Embargo** | a total ban on importing a good | imports fall to zero |
| **Administrative barriers** | paperwork, licensing, safety and labelling standards, slow customs | raises the cost and delay of importing without an explicit tax |

| Argument **for** protection | The counter-argument |
|---|---|
| **Infant (emerging) industries** need shelter until they can compete | protection may become permanent and the industry never grows up |
| **Declining (sunset) industries** — a sudden collapse would cause large-scale unemployment | delaying adjustment keeps resources in low-productivity use |
| **Limiting over-specialisation** in a few industries | the gains from comparative advantage are given up |
| **Correcting a trade imbalance** between exports and imports | treats the symptom, not the cause of uncompetitiveness |
| **Others are doing it** — lower barriers only when others do | this is how a trade war starts |
| **Protecting jobs**, and defending against **dumping** or for **strategic** reasons (food, energy, defence) | jobs are lost elsewhere; genuinely low foreign cost is not dumping; security has a price paid by consumers |

**Arguments against protection:** it restricts **consumer choice** and raises prices; it restricts new revenue and employment opportunities; it keeps **inefficient firms** alive, wasting resources; it raises costs for firms using imported raw materials (and domestic substitutes may be of lower quality); it makes protected firms complacent; and other countries may **retaliate**, raising the price of this country's exports and cutting export sales and jobs.

A **free trade agreement (FTA)** sets rules for how countries treat each other in trade, to make trade more efficient and profitable: it removes tariffs, bans subsidies, simplifies customs procedures, and makes it easier for businesspeople to travel and live in partner countries; it may bring strategic and political benefits too. A **customs union** goes further, removing tariffs between members and applying a common external tariff (the EU, CARICOM, the Southern African Customs Union). As a small country with limited resources, NZ relies heavily on trade and FTAs to maintain its standard of living.

The judgement on protection depends on **how long** it lasts, **which** industry it covers and whether that industry can realistically become competitive, **how likely retaliation** is, and whether a cheaper alternative (training, infrastructure, investment support) would achieve the same aim.

## Exchange rates

When NZ buys Japanese cars, money flows **out** of NZ (import payments) and **into** Japan (export earnings), so NZ dollars must be exchanged for yen. An **exchange rate** is the **value of one currency compared with another**.

To convert, **multiply** by the rate quoted as "1 domestic = $x$ foreign"; to convert back, **divide**. At NZ\$1 = US\$0.70, NZ\$25 is $25 \times 0.70 = 17.50$ — US\$17.50.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Appreciation** | the currency's value **rises** — one unit buys **more** foreign currency | NZ\$1 = €0.60 → NZ\$1 = €0.67 |
| **Depreciation** | the value **falls** — one unit buys **less** foreign currency | NZ\$1 = A\$0.95 → NZ\$1 = A\$0.84 |
| **Revaluation / devaluation** | the deliberate equivalents under a fixed system | |

*Worked conversion.* An Italian car costs €120 000. At NZ\$1 = €0.60 it costs $120\,000 / 0.60 = 200\,000$ — **NZ\$200 000**. At NZ\$1 = €0.62 it costs $120\,000 / 0.62 = 193\,548$ — **NZ\$193 548**. The NZ dollar has **appreciated**, so the import is cheaper. **Write the quotation direction down before you multiply or divide**; getting it upside down is the commonest arithmetic error in this topic.

*Reading prices.* An American car that cost NZ\$50 000 last year costs NZ\$60 000 now with no change in its US price → the NZ dollar has **depreciated**. NZ apples that cost Japanese buyers ¥2000 now cost ¥1500 → the NZ dollar has **depreciated** against the yen. If the NZ dollar appreciates, imported soy sauce gets **cheaper** in NZ dollars.

### How exchange rates are determined

Exchange rates are set by **demand and supply on the foreign exchange market**. Foreigners buying NZ goods, services or assets, or wanting to hold NZ dollars, **demand** NZ dollars. New Zealanders buying from overseas, and other holders selling NZ dollars, **supply** them.

| Determinant | Effect on the currency |
|---|---|
| **Demand for exports** rises (better products, effective marketing, higher incomes in export markets) | **appreciates** — more of the currency is needed to pay for them |
| **Demand for imports** rises | **depreciates** — more of the currency is supplied to buy foreign currency |
| **Relative interest rates** rise | **appreciates** — savers seek the higher return |
| **Relative inflation** rises | **depreciates** — exports become dearer, so demand for them and the currency falls |
| **Foreign direct investment** into the country | **appreciates** the host currency (and depreciates the MNC's home currency) |
| **Speculation** that it will rise | **appreciates** as buyers move in early; expected falls cause selling and depreciation |
| **Government / central-bank intervention** | selling reserves of its own currency increases supply and lowers the rate; buying raises it |
| **Global trading patterns** and the **balance of payments** | a persistent deficit tends to depreciate the currency; a surplus to appreciate it |

| System | How the rate is set | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Floating** | purely by demand and supply, with no government intervention | adjusts automatically; no reserves needed; interest rates free for domestic aims | volatile and uncertain for traders and investors |
| **Fixed** | pegged to an anchor currency and not allowed to fluctuate | certainty for trade and investment; disciplines inflation | needs large reserves; interest rates tied to defending the peg; a misaligned peg invites speculative attack |
| **Managed** | influenced by the central bank around a preferred rate | some stability with some flexibility | still needs reserves; the target may not be credible |

## Effects of an exchange-rate change

The old mnemonic is **SPICED** — **S**trong **P**ound, **I**mports **C**heap, **E**xports **D**ear. It works for any currency: a strong NZ dollar makes imports cheap and exports dear.

| | Appreciation | Depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| Export prices abroad | rise | fall |
| Import prices at home | fall | rise |
| Export volumes | tend to fall | tend to rise |
| Import volumes | tend to rise | tend to fall |
| Firms using imported inputs | gain — cheaper materials | lose — dearer materials, higher costs |
| Inflation | eases — imports are cheaper and spending falls | rises — **imported** (cost-push) inflation |
| Employment in export industries | falls | rises |
| Economic growth | likely to fall — net exports $(X-M)$ fall and consumer spending drops as export jobs go | likely to rise as net exports and aggregate demand rise |
| Current account | tends to worsen | tends to improve |
| Foreign-currency debts | cheaper to repay | **dearer** to service and repay |

**None of this is automatic.** Four reasons:

1. **Elasticity.** A depreciation raises export *revenue* only if demand for exports is price **elastic**; if it is inelastic, the lower price earns less. Likewise imports fall much only if demand for them is elastic — a country importing essential food, oil and machinery has very inelastic import demand.
2. **Time lags.** Existing contracts are priced in advance, so volumes take months or years to respond.
3. **Capacity constraints.** If export factories are already at full capacity, higher foreign demand cannot be met.
4. **Imported inputs.** An exporter that imports its raw materials sees its costs rise with the depreciation, offsetting the competitiveness gain.

So a depreciation tends to help an **export-oriented** economy with strong domestic production, while an economy that relies on imported food, energy and materials mainly gets **higher inflation and a lower standard of living**.

**Coping with a high exchange rate.** Exporters facing an appreciation can cut prices and accept a lower margin, find cheaper suppliers, raise efficiency, compete on non-price factors (quality, brand, social responsibility), or focus on goods with inelastic demand.

## The balance of payments

**Exports** are goods and services produced in one country and sold to another; **imports** are those bought and brought in. NZ's main exports are dairy (concentrated milk, butter), beef and timber; its main imports are cars, crude and refined petroleum, and broadcasting equipment; China and Australia are its biggest partners both ways.

The **balance of payments (BoP)** is a financial record of a country's transactions with the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. In theory it must **balance** over time, because a country, like a person, can spend only what it earns or borrows. It has three accounts: the **current account** (the one to know in detail), the **capital account** and the **financial account**.

**Visible** trade is trade in **goods**; **invisible** trade is trade in **services** such as insurance, banking and tourism. A New Zealander spending on hotels and meals in Europe is an **invisible import**; a British tourist paying for a Queenstown backpackers is an **invisible export** for NZ.

$$\text{balance of trade} = \text{visible exports} - \text{visible imports}$$
$$\text{balance on services} = \text{invisible exports} - \text{invisible imports}$$

Spending more on imported goods than is earned from exports is a **trade deficit**; the reverse is a **trade surplus**.

| Current account component | Also called | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance on goods** | the visible trade balance | exports minus imports of physical goods — cars, oil, steel, clothing, food |
| **Balance on services** | the invisible trade balance | tourism, banking, education, insurance, transport |
| **Balance on primary income** | investment and employment income | interest on savings abroad, dividends on foreign shares, profits from overseas subsidiaries, wages sent home by residents working abroad, minus the same paid to foreigners |
| **Balance on secondary income** | current transfers | one-way transfers with nothing in return — foreign aid and grants, migrants' remittances, contributions to international organisations, pensions and gifts across borders |

$$\text{current account} = \text{goods} + \text{services} + \text{primary income} + \text{secondary income}$$

Each item is a **credit** (money in) or a **debit** (money out); the current account is in surplus or deficit depending on which is larger. Overseas aid received, donations from abroad and taxes paid by overseas visitors on goods bought here are credits; pensions paid to residents of other countries and membership fees to international organisations are debits.

*Worked example*, in \$bn:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Visible exports | 784.2 |
| Visible imports | 1230.4 |
| **Balance of trade** | **−446.2** |
| Invisible exports | 286.4 |
| Invisible imports | 219.9 |
| **Balance on services** | **+66.5** |
| Net primary income | +21.0 |
| Net secondary income | −58.6 |
| **Current account balance** | **−417.3** |

So $-446.2 + 66.5 + 21.0 - 58.6 = -417.3$: a current account **deficit** of \$417.3bn. A **goods-trade deficit is not the same as a current-account deficit** — services and income flows can offset part of it.

If a country spends more than it earns, the gap must be paid for by capital flowing in from overseas, recorded in the other two accounts, which are usually small next to the current account:

- **Capital account** — transfers of ownership of fixed assets (an immigrant bringing assets with them).
- **Financial account** — financial investment: net foreign direct investment, net purchases of overseas shares and bonds, and short-term **"hot money"** flows.

**Causes of a current-account deficit:** uncompetitive exports (high costs, high relative inflation, poor quality, inefficiency), an overvalued exchange rate, strong domestic income growth pulling in imports, the loss of a major export market, a fall in the world price of a key export, or heavy imports of capital equipment during industrialisation. NZ has run a current-account deficit for around fifty years.

| A deficit may not matter if… | A deficit is a problem when… |
|---|---|
| it is financed by **equity** investment (buying shares), which need not be repaid | it is financed by **borrowing**, creating debt repayments — a common problem in developing countries |
| foreign investment brings jobs, technology and growth, and signals a productive economy | foreigners take control of a large share of the country's assets |
| consumption of imports raises living standards now | the economy is unbalanced towards consumption of imports rather than investment |
| a resulting depreciation makes exports cheaper and helps close the gap | the cause is underlying inefficiency, so exports stay uncompetitive |
| it is small — under about **5% of GDP** is usually considered manageable | it is large and persistent, draining reserves and confidence |

A **surplus** is not automatically good either: it may mean weak domestic demand, it can bring inflationary pressure and currency appreciation, and it invites protectionist retaliation from deficit partners.

**Corrective policies for a deficit:**

| Policy | Mechanism | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Depreciation of the currency** | exports cheaper, imports dearer | depends on elasticities; raises imported inflation |
| **Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy** | lower incomes cut import spending | also cuts growth and raises unemployment |
| **Protectionism** | tariffs and quotas cut imports directly | retaliation; higher input costs; consumer loss |
| **Supply-side policy** | training, infrastructure and investment raise productivity and export quality | slow, and expensive up front |

Supply-side measures attack the cause rather than the symptom, but take years; demand-reducing measures work faster at the cost of growth and jobs. Which is right depends on whether the deficit is **cyclical** (temporary, from strong demand) or **structural** (from persistent uncompetitiveness).

## Practice

1. Explain two reasons why an economy may have a high exchange rate. *Model points:* high relative interest rates attract foreign savings, raising demand for the currency; strong demand for its exports; low relative inflation; inward investment; speculation.
2. Discuss whether a fall in the international value of its currency will always benefit an economy. *Model points:* for — exports cheaper, export demand and AD rise, jobs and GDP rise, imports dearer so the current account improves; against — imported inflation and cost-push pressure, lower living standards, dearer foreign debt; evaluation — elasticities, time lags, capacity, imported inputs, and whether the economy is export-oriented or import-dependent; "always" invites a conclusion that it depends.
3. Discuss whether trade protection supports the growth of domestic firms. *Model points:* for — dearer imports shift demand to domestic goods, raising revenue, profit and jobs; infant industries can grow to economies of scale; against — retaliation cuts export sales, dearer imported materials raise costs, lower-quality domestic inputs, complacency and inefficiency.
