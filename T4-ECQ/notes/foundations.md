# Scarcity, choice and economic systems

## What economics studies

**Economics** is a social science: the study of the **production, distribution and consumption** of goods and services, and of how consumers, producers (firms) and the government interact. An **economy** is any area where people and firms produce, exchange and consume — a suburb, a city, a country or the whole world — and smaller economies sit inside larger ones.

A **resource** is anything that can be used for a specific purpose. In every country resources are scarce, so governments, firms and individuals must decide how to allocate them to satisfy as many needs and wants as possible.

## The basic economic problem

**Wants are unlimited; the means to satisfy them are limited.** That is **scarcity**. Once one want is satisfied another replaces it — a 75% exam mark makes you want 80% next time; a market leader wants still more market share.

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Need** | something people **must** have to survive | food, water, air, shelter, clothing |
| **Want** | something people would **like** to have but can survive without | phones, cars, watches, games consoles |

People usually satisfy needs first and spend what is left on wants. The **limited means** are:

- **time** — 24 hours a day, rich or poor
- **money or income** — even the rich cannot buy everything they want
- **skill and knowledge** — nobody is good at everything

Because means are limited, every economy (and every firm) must answer three questions: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **for whom**.

## Opportunity cost

Choosing one thing means not choosing another. **Opportunity cost** is **the next best alternative forgone** — one alternative, not the sum of everything rejected. It applies to every economic agent: households, firms and government.

*Worked example.* A government has \$1 billion and three uses for it. It ranks healthcare first, police second and education third, and chooses healthcare. The opportunity cost is the **police spending** — the next best alternative — not police plus education.

| Who chooses | The choice | The opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Student | study Economics | the other subject that could have filled that slot |
| Firm | buy a new car for the business | the extra worker who could have been hired |
| Government | fund healthcare | the police spending not made |

## Goods, services and resources

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Producer** | any person or organisation that uses resources to make goods and services |
| **Consumer** | an individual or household that uses goods and services |
| **Goods** | items with a physical presence |
| **Services** | something done for you — a mechanic, a hotel, a haircut |
| **Free goods** | plentiful and costless, such as wind and sunshine |
| **Economic goods** | scarce, so they have a cost — almost everything |
| **Consumer goods** | for private use by households; **durable** ones last (cars, washing machines), **non-durable** ones are used up (food, petrol) |
| **Capital (producer) goods** | man-made goods used to make other goods and services — a coffee machine, a drill, a truck, a road |

Resources fall into three groups: **natural** (cotton, soil, water, oil, coal, wood), **human** (pickers, drivers, accountants, shop assistants) and **man-made** (machines, lorries, power stations, roads, factory buildings).

## Factors of production

The scarce resources used to produce goods and services are the **factors of production** — the inputs that a production process turns into outputs.

| Factor | Meaning | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| **Land** | the place where business activity happens, and all natural resources — soil, minerals, water, forests | rent |
| **Labour** | human physical and mental effort; its size, skill and availability decide how much and how well an economy produces | wages |
| **Capital** | man-made resources that help produce other goods and services — machinery, factories, tools | interest |
| **Enterprise** | the ability to employ and organise the other factors, taking the risk of the venture | profit |

"Capital" in economics means productive equipment, not money. Money is only a means of acquiring capital.

## Types of good that cause trouble for markets

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Public good** | **non-excludable** (a non-payer cannot be stopped from benefiting) and **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce what is left for others); no private firm will supply it because nobody has to pay | street lighting, lighthouses, police, defence |
| **Merit good** | beneficial, but consumers **undervalue** it, so it is **under-consumed** in a free market | education, healthcare |
| **Demerit good** | harmful, but consumers **overvalue** it, so it is **over-consumed**; governments often tax it | tobacco, alcohol |

**Merit goods are not public goods.** A merit good is excludable and rival — a school place can be charged for and a seat used by one pupil is not available to another. A public good is neither. Confusing the two loses marks.

## Specialisation and the division of labour

A task is usually done better by an expert. **Specialisation** is concentrating on one product or task; firms and workers who specialise become the "go-to" supplier and can earn a higher return. Within large-scale production this becomes the **division of labour**: the work is split into separate tasks and each worker specialises in one.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| higher productivity and efficiency | boredom from repeating one task, so quality may suffer |
| less wasted time and resources; uses each person's abilities | workers feel like "a cog in the wheel" and lose motivation |
| makes it easier to bring in specialised machinery | heavy reliance on others — if one specialist is absent, the line may stop |
| | machinery may replace workers rather than help them |

Division of labour works best in **mass production of standardised products**, such as a factory making footballs.

## Derived demand and diminishing returns

**Derived demand** — the demand for a good or factor exists because of the demand for something else. Rising demand for mobile phones raised demand for **lithium** for their batteries; demand for labour is derived from demand for what workers produce.

**The law of diminishing (marginal) returns** — if one factor is increased while the others stay fixed, the extra output from each additional unit will **eventually fall**. A café that hires more staff at first serves more customers, but with the same floor space and coffee machines, extra workers start getting in each other's way, so each adds less output than the one before.

## Production possibility curve

A **PPC** (or production possibility frontier, PPF) shows the **maximum combinations of two goods** that can be produced when **all resources and technology are used in the most efficient way**. It assumes only two goods. Full drawing rules are in [the diagrams page](../DIAGRAMS.md).

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **On** an axis | all resources in one good, none of the other |
| **On** the curve | efficient — resources fully used; more of one good means less of the other, so opportunity cost appears |
| **Inside** the curve | **production inefficiency** — resources unused or badly used; **both** goods can increase with **no** opportunity cost |
| **Outside** the curve | **unattainable** with current resources and technology |

*Worked example — a soft-drink firm's PPC.*

| Point | Coke (cans) | Fanta (cans) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 90 | 0 |
| B | 80 | 50 |
| C | 60 | 80 |
| D | 0 | 90 |

- Moving from A to C gains **80 Fanta** at the cost of **30 Coke** (90 → 60). The opportunity cost of those 80 Fanta is 30 Coke.
- Producing 60 Coke and 60 Fanta is a point **inside** the curve: Fanta could rise by 20 to point C with **no** loss of Coke — that is production inefficiency, stated in figures.
- From B to C, 20 Coke buys 30 extra Fanta; from C to D, 60 Coke buys only 10 more. Each extra unit of Fanta costs more Coke — **increasing opportunity cost**.

**Shape.** A curve **bowed outwards** (concave to the origin) means resources are better suited to one good than the other, so opportunity cost rises as you specialise further. A **straight line** means resources are equally efficient at both (a standard car and a luxury car from the same plant), so opportunity cost is **constant**. A **steeper** section means a **higher** opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis.

**Movement along the curve** — reallocating resources, for example moving labour from manufacturing to services: more services at the expense of manufacturing. This is a movement, not a shift.

**Shifts of the curve.**

| Outward (right) — production possibilities rise: economic growth | Inward (left) — production possibilities fall |
|---|---|
| higher productivity or efficiency of inputs | natural disasters — floods, tsunami, drought |
| better management of inputs | large outward migration, so fewer skilled workers |
| more capital and a larger labour supply | loss of inputs through war or conflict |
| innovation — new products, processes and resources | economic recession (see the caution below) |
| discovery or extraction of new natural resources | |

*Caution on recession.* The teaching material lists recession as a cause of an inward shift. Be precise in an answer: a recession first leaves resources **unemployed**, which is a point **inside** the existing curve. The curve itself moves in only if capacity is actually lost — firms close for good, machinery is scrapped, skills decay.

**Only one end moving.** If only one industry improves — a breakthrough in farming technology, or more labour for agriculture — the curve pivots outwards on that axis only. Production of both goods does **not** always rise when factors improve.

**Capital goods against consumer goods.** Capital goods expand future productive capacity; consumer goods satisfy wants now but do not directly add to growth. An economy that puts more resources into capital goods today (new manufacturing machinery rather than luxury cars) can expect its PPC to shift out faster later — the opportunity cost is consumption now.

## Economic systems

An **economic system** is the way an economy answers what, how and for whom. Systems grow out of how people think and behave, and they change over time. There are **four**: free market, command (planned), mixed and traditional.

| | Free market | Command / planned | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who answers the three questions | the private sector, through demand and supply | the government | both; the government decides how far it intervenes |
| How resources are allocated | by **price**: a high price encourages supply, a low price encourages buying | by state plans, often **five-year plans** | prices for most goods; the state provides essential services and merit goods |
| Who gets the output | those **willing and able to pay**; unprofitable goods are not produced | roughly equal shares; wages nearly equal regardless of role | market incomes, with redistribution through welfare and pensions |
| Advantages | **efficiency** (competition makes firms listen to consumers), **freedom of choice**, **incentive** to work hard | **prevents waste** of scarce resources and limits pollution, **social equality** (basic needs met), **economies of scale** from large national suppliers | combines incentives with provision for those the market leaves out |
| Disadvantages | **environmental damage** (no consequence for pollution or depletion), **inequality**, **social problems** (no public goods; the poor cannot afford education or healthcare) | **lack of freedom** of purchase and career, **no incentive** to work harder, little international trade | the right boundary is contested and politically fought over |
| Examples | none pure; Hong Kong before 1997 came closest | North Korea, Cuba, the former USSR | NZ, Australia, the UK, Germany — nearly every economy |

A **traditional economy** answers the three questions by **custom and tradition** — people produce what their community has always produced, often for their own use, with goods shared or bartered rather than sold for money.

Almost every real economy is **mixed**, because every economy needs some government. Place countries on a **continuum** from command to free market rather than in boxes.

**Private sector** — businesses owned by individuals or companies, mainly to make a **profit** (shops, restaurants, tech firms). **Public sector** — organisations owned and run by the government, mainly to **provide a service** (state schools, police, the armed forces).

## The price mechanism

In a market, prices allocate resources through three functions:

| Function | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Signalling** | a rising price tells producers that buyers want more, and tells consumers to look for substitutes |
| **Incentive** | a higher price makes supplying more profitable, drawing resources into that market |
| **Rationing** | when a good is scarce its price rises, so it goes to those willing and able to pay the most |

No central instruction is needed: a rise in demand raises the price, which draws resources in until the extra demand is met.

The limits are the examinable point: the price mechanism **ignores** external costs, provides no public goods, under-provides merit goods, over-provides demerit goods, and distributes according to **ability to pay** rather than need.
