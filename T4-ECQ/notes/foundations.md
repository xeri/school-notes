# Scarcity, choice and economic systems

## The basic economic problem

**Wants are unlimited; resources are scarce.** That is the whole of economics in one sentence. Scarcity forces choice, and every choice has a cost.

A **need** is essential for survival (food, water, shelter). A **want** is desired but not essential.

**Opportunity cost** is the **next best alternative forgone** — not the sum of everything rejected. If you can spend an hour on economics, maths or sleep, and you choose economics, the opportunity cost is whichever *one* of maths or sleep you valued most.

| Who chooses | The choice | The opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer | spend $50 on a jacket | the concert ticket not bought |
| Producer | use the factory for product A | the output of product B forgone |
| Government | build a hospital | the school not built |

## Factors of production

| Factor | Meaning | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| **Land** | all natural resources — soil, minerals, water, forests | rent |
| **Labour** | human physical and mental effort | wages |
| **Capital** | man-made goods used to produce other goods — machinery, factories, tools | interest |
| **Enterprise** | organising the other three factors and bearing the risk | profit |

Note that "capital" in economics means productive equipment, not money. Money is a means of acquiring capital.

## Production possibility curve

A **PPC** shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its given resources and technology, **used fully and efficiently**.

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **On** the curve | efficient — all resources employed |
| **Inside** the curve | inefficient, or resources unemployed |
| **Outside** the curve | unattainable with current resources and technology |

**Moving along** the curve reallocates resources from one good to the other — this is where opportunity cost is visible.

**Shifting outward** means productive capacity has grown: more or better resources, new technology, more training, investment in capital. **Shifting inward** follows a war, a natural disaster or a fall in the workforce.

A **bowed (concave) curve** shows **increasing opportunity cost**: resources are not equally suited to both goods, so as you push further into one, each extra unit costs more of the other. A straight-line PPC assumes constant opportunity cost.

*Worked example.* A workshop can make 20 tables and 0 chairs, or 16 tables and 12 chairs. Moving to the second combination sacrifices 4 tables to gain 12 chairs, so the opportunity cost of those 12 chairs is **4 tables**. It does **not** follow that the next 12 chairs cost the same — on a bowed curve they cost more.

## Economic systems

Every system must answer three questions: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **for whom**.

| | Market economy | Planned economy | Mixed economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides | consumers and firms, through prices | the government, centrally | both |
| Ownership | private | state | both |
| Motive | profit and self-interest | social and political objectives | both |
| Strength | strong incentives, efficiency, choice, innovation | can provide for all, reduce inequality, supply public goods | combines incentives with provision for unmet needs |
| Weakness | inequality, market failure, public goods undersupplied, unemployment | weak incentives, poor information, shortages and surpluses, little choice | boundaries are contested and politically fought over |

Compare systems on **incentives, information, equity** and **the ability to respond to unmet needs**. Neither extreme is flawless — almost all real economies are mixed.

## The price mechanism

In a market, prices allocate resources through three functions:

| Function | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Signalling** | a rising price tells producers that buyers want more, and tells consumers to look for substitutes |
| **Incentive** | a higher price rewards producers for supplying more, and rewards switching resources into that market |
| **Rationing** | when a good is scarce the price rises, so only those willing and able to pay the most obtain it |

This operates with no central instruction — a rise in demand for a good raises its price, which draws resources in until the extra demand is met.

The limits are the examinable point: the price mechanism **ignores** external costs, undersupplies public and merit goods, overprovides demerit goods, and distributes according to ability to pay rather than need.
