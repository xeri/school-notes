# Business Studies — Pre-Q Syllabus

**Start here:** [Study guide](notes/study-guide.md) · [Term 3 decisions](notes/t3-decisions.md) · [CCC extension](notes/ccc-extension.md) · [Business vs Economics](notes/econ-overlap.md)

**Final exam (Term 4):** 3 hours, **120 marks** total.

- **3 compulsory short-answer questions**, 20 marks each — 50% of the paper. Each is made up of 2-, 4- and 6-mark parts.
- **3 compulsory long-answer questions**, 20 marks each — 50% of the paper, requiring you to interpret and analyse **previously unseen data** in a business context. Each is made up of 8- and 12-mark parts.

There is no choice of question. How each type of question is marked is set out in [How marks are awarded](#how-marks-are-awarded) below. The teacher's guidance is roughly 5 marks for **application** in each short-answer question and about 6 in each long-answer question — at least 30 marks across the paper — so every answer must use the details of the case. Earlier terms: a 1-hour exam at the end of Term 1 and a 2-hour exam at the end of Term 2. Class and common tests each term. (The 2018 sample paper used an older 160-mark layout; the current format is the one above.)

**Course aims.** To develop understanding of the environments in which businesses operate and how they operate; how businesses are structured and their role in sectors of the economy; business objectives and the effect of major groups on them; how businesses are financed and how their relations with other organisations, consumers, employees, owners and society are regulated; and how inputs are transformed into outputs.

## How marks are awarded

Every mark in Business Studies rewards one of four skills. The examiner annotates each one in the margin.

| Skill | Marked | What it looks like | IGCSE weighting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** (AO1) | [k] | a correct term, definition, advantage or point | 40% |
| **Application** (AO2) | [app] | the point tied to **this** business, using a fact from the case | 20% |
| **Analysis** (AO3) | [an] | a developed chain of reasoning: *so… which means… leading to…* | 25% |
| **Evaluation** (AO4) | [ev] | a justified judgement, decision or recommendation | 15% |

In this course roughly **5 marks of every 20-mark short-answer question** and **about 6 of every 20-mark long-answer question** are application — at least 30 of the 120 marks. An answer with perfect theory and no reference to the case loses those marks.

### Command words

| Command | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Identify / State / Name** | give the point; no explanation needed |
| **Define / What is meant by** | the precise meaning; an example alone is not a definition |
| **Outline** | the main points briefly |
| **Explain** | say why or how, with a developed reason |
| **Identify and explain** | the point **plus** its development — one mark each |
| **Calculate** | work it out, showing the formula and the working |
| **Consider** | review the information given and respond to it |
| **Do you think… / Discuss / Evaluate** | both sides, then a judgement |
| **Recommend… / Justify your answer** | choose one option and support the choice with evidence and argument |

### How each type of question is built

**"Identify and explain" = point + development.** The first mark is for the point (knowledge). The second is for developing it: *why* it matters or *what it leads to*. If the question is about the case, one of the marks is only available when the development is applied to the business.

| Marks | Typical command | How the marks are made up | Model structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Identify two… | 1 [k] per correct point; only the first ones written are marked | short list |
| 2 | What is meant by…? | 2 for a full definition; 1 for a partial one | one precise sentence |
| 2 | Identify and explain one… | 1 [k] + 1 development | point → *which means* → effect |
| 3 | Identify and explain one… (in context) | 1 [k] + 1 [app] + 1 [an] | point → applied to the case → consequence for the business |
| 4 | Identify and explain two… / one advantage and one disadvantage | 2 × (1 [k] + 1 [app] or development) | two short paragraphs, one per point |
| 6 | Explain two… (in context) | 2 [k] + 2 [app] + 2 [an] | two paragraphs: point, case fact, consequence |
| 6 | Do you think…? Justify your answer. | 2 [k] + 2 [an] + 2 [ev]; or levels: L1 1–2, L2 3–4, L3 5–6 | one side, the other side, a justified decision |
| 8 | Identify and explain two… (long answer) | 2 [k] + 2 [an] + **4 [app]** (two per point) | two developed paragraphs, each using **two** case facts |
| 12 | Consider… Recommend which… Justify your answer. | levels of response (below) plus up to 2 [app] | discuss **every** option, then recommend one and say why the others lose |
| any | Calculate… | method mark(s) + answer mark | formula, substitution, answer with units |

**Calculations.** Write the formula, substitute, then give the answer with its unit (\$, units, %, or a ratio). A wrong figure carried forward is still credited if the later method is right (the **own figure rule**), so always show the working.

### What application looks like

Application means answering in the context of the business in the question. It is **not** just writing the business's name, and it is **not** dropping in a famous company from outside the case. It **is**:

- what the business sells — a perishable good, a luxury, a service — and who to
- its legal structure, size and sector ("a sole trader with two part-time staff")
- its market: niche or mass, saturated, competitive, exporting
- **its numbers**: "the \$50 000 loan", "a 10% fall in sales", "a gross margin of 70%"
- named people and groups: the owner by name, "the 50 fruit pickers", not "the workers"
- its stated objectives and problems

A useful test: could the sentence be pasted, unchanged, into an answer about a different business? If yes, it carries no application mark.

*Not applied (point and development only):* "A sole trader keeps all the profit, which motivates the owner." *Applied:* "As a sole trader, Mere keeps all the profit from every loaf her bakery sells, which rewards the 5 a.m. starts she works."

### Levels of response for evaluation questions

The long 12-mark "recommend and justify" questions are marked by **level**, not by counting points. The IGCSE Business Studies 0450 case-study paper uses three levels:

| Level | IGCSE 0450 marks (out of 12) | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 9–12 | sound applied knowledge; **detailed discussion of every option**; a **well-justified recommendation** that says why the alternatives were rejected |
| 2 | 5–8 | sound applied knowledge; detailed discussion of **at least one** option; a judgement with some justification |
| 1 | 1–4 | limited knowledge; options outlined with little explanation; a simple judgement with little justification |

The school's own mark schemes use the same idea in a 1–3 / 4–6 / 7–10 form plus up to 2 application marks: each extra developed (Level 2) explanation earns another mark; Level 3 needs **at least two** Level 2 explanations **and then** a recommendation; 7–8 marks for limited judgement, 9–10 for a well-justified one. The 6-mark "do you think" questions follow the same pattern in miniature: L1 (1–2) relevant points, L2 (3–4) developed arguments, L3 (5–6) both sides **and** a justified judgement.

**A judgement only scores if it rests on analysis written earlier.** A recommendation that appears from nowhere stays in Level 2.

**Writing the evaluation:**

1. Answer the question directly: "Overall, the business should choose X."
2. Give the deciding reason, using a case fact: "because it is new and small, with only \$500 of cash…"
3. Say why the alternative is worse **here** — "rather than Y, which would cost too much and take too long for a firm this size."
4. Add what it **depends on**, or what would change your mind: "…unless sales fall, in which case…"
5. Where sensible, separate the short run from the long run: "now X; once it has grown, Y."

A one-sided answer, or "it depends" without a decision, cannot reach the top level.

## 1. Understanding business activity

- The purpose of business: needs, wants, scarcity, opportunity cost, factors of production, specialisation, adding value and how to increase it.
- Enterprise: the characteristics of successful entrepreneurs; why and how governments support business start-ups.
- Classification of business: primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; the chain of production; why the importance of each sector changes as an economy develops; the public and private sectors; free-market, command and mixed economies; privatisation.
- Business plans and the causes of business failure.
- Measuring business size: employees, sales revenue, output, capital employed, market share and market capitalisation — why the measures can rank firms differently, and why profit is **not** a size measure.
- Business growth: why owners want to expand; internal and external growth; the problems of growth and how to overcome them; mergers and takeovers; horizontal, vertical (forward and backward) and conglomerate integration; why some firms stay small.
- Economies of scale: purchasing, marketing, financial, risk-bearing and technical. Managerial economies. Diseconomies of scale: labour, communication, management and agglomeration.
- Sole traders and partnerships: formation, control, liability, finance; the deed of partnership.
- Limited companies: private and public; the differences between unincorporated businesses and limited companies; recommending a suitable form of ownership; limited liability; separate legal identity; shares, shareholders and directors; dividends.
- Franchises: the franchisor–franchisee relationship, fees, benefits and restrictions.
- Joint ventures and co-operatives.
- Social enterprise and its objectives; the public sector, including public corporations; how public- and private-sector objectives differ.
- Business aims and objectives: aims vs objectives, SMART objectives, mission statements; survival, profit maximisation and satisficing, growth, market share, sales, CSR and service quality; how objectives change over time; Māori business concepts.
- Corporate social responsibility (the triple bottom line) and business ethics.
- Internal and external stakeholder groups: owners, shareholders, employees, customers, banks, government, competitors, the community — their aims and how those aims conflict.
- Technology and business: its effect on processes, products, competition and stakeholders.

## 2. People in business

- Why people work; the meaning and importance of motivation.
- Motivation theory, with diagrams: Taylor's scientific management, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Herzberg's two-factor theory, McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y.
- Financial rewards: wages (time rate, overtime and piece rate), salaries, commission, bonus, profit sharing, performance-related pay, share ownership; fringe benefits.
- Non-financial methods of raising job satisfaction: job rotation, job enlargement, job enrichment, teamworking.
- Organisational structure: drawing and reading organisational charts; levels of hierarchy, de-layering; the roles of directors, managers, supervisors and other employees; trust versus control in delegation; chain of command, span of control, delegation, tall and flat structures, centralised and decentralised organisations.
- Management functions and leadership styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire — and when each suits.
- Communication: the process, internal and external, formal and informal, one-way and two-way; verbal, written and visual methods; barriers to effective communication and how to remove them.
- Trade unions: their role, collective bargaining, industrial action, the effects on workers and on the business.
- Human resource management: workforce planning, the stages and methods of recruitment and selection, recommending who to employ, full-time and part-time employees, job description and person specification, internal vs external recruitment, the importance of training; induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training; why downsizing may be needed and choosing who to make redundant; dismissal and redundancy, employment contracts and employment law, labour turnover.

## 3. Marketing

- The role of marketing: identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs; maintaining loyalty and building customer relationships; why spending patterns change, why markets have become more competitive, and how businesses respond; market orientation vs product orientation.
- Markets: mass and niche markets, market segmentation, target markets, price and non-price competition, market share and market growth.
- Market research: market orientation; primary and secondary; qualitative and quantitative; the methods of each (postal and online surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation; government, published and commercial reports) with their benefits and limitations; questionnaires and the need for sampling; bias; presenting and interpreting data.
- The marketing mix — **Product**: core, tangible and augmented features; fit for purpose; invention and innovation; the benefits and limitations of new products; USP, branding and brand image; packaging; drawing the product life cycle, extension strategies, how the stage affects price and promotion, and the product portfolio.
- The marketing mix — **Price**: cost-plus, competitor, penetration, skimming, premium, psychological, promotional/loss-leader, contribution pricing and price discrimination; price elasticity of demand.
- The marketing mix — **Promotion**: the aims of promotion, above- and below-the-line promotion, informative and persuasive advertising, sales promotion, public relations and sponsorship, social media, the marketing budget.
- The marketing mix — **Place**: distribution channels (direct, retailer, wholesaler, agent), intermediaries; e-commerce and its opportunities and threats for businesses and consumers; the internet and social media in promotion.
- Legal controls on marketing: misleading promotion, faulty and dangerous goods, consumer protection.
- Marketing strategy: how the four elements must support each other and the target market; common strategies; SWOT analysis.
- Entering new markets, including other countries: the opportunities, the risks, and how to overcome the problems.

## 4. Operations management

- Production and productivity; labour productivity; labour-intensive and capital-intensive production.
- Methods of production: job, batch and flow — and the circumstances suiting each. How technology changes production: CAD, CAM, robots and automation.
- Inventory: raw materials, work in progress and finished goods; why firms hold inventory and its costs; the inventory control chart (maximum, reorder level, buffer stock, lead time).
- Lean production, just-in-time and kaizen: the benefits and the risks.
- Costs: fixed, variable, total and average; using cost data in simple decisions such as whether to continue production.
- Break-even analysis: total revenue, contribution, break-even output; constructing, completing, amending and interpreting break-even charts; margin of safety; using break-even for decisions such as a price change; the limitations of the model.
- Quality: what quality means; quality control, quality assurance and total quality management; quality circles; the costs of poor quality and of improving it.
- Location decisions: the factors affecting location for manufacturing and service businesses, legal controls on location, optimal location, infrastructure, government influence, multi-site locations, offshoring and locating in another country.

## 5. Financial information and decisions

- The need for business finance: start-up, survival and expansion capital; capital and revenue expenditure.
- Short-term and long-term finance needs and sources; debt and equity. Sources of finance — internal: retained profit, owner's savings, sale of assets, sale and leaseback. External: overdraft, credit card, bank loan, trade credit, hire purchase and leasing, venture capital, share issue, debentures, government support; microfinance and crowdfunding.
- Choosing a source of finance: amount, purpose, time, cost, risk (gearing), size and legal form of the business.
- Why cash matters. Cash flow forecasting: constructing, completing, amending and interpreting a forecast; receipts, payments, net cash flow, opening and closing balance; why cash is not profit; the causes of and solutions to cash-flow problems.
- Working capital and liquidity.
- Profit: how it is made and why it matters (reward for risk, source of finance). Income statements: trading account, profit and loss account, appropriation account; revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses, operating profit, profit for the year; the use of profit.
- Statements of financial position: non-current and current assets, current and non-current liabilities, equity; interpreting how the business is financed and what it owns.
- Ratio analysis — profitability: gross profit margin, operating profit margin, return on capital employed.
- Ratio analysis — liquidity: current ratio, acid-test ratio.
- Profitability and liquidity as concepts; who uses accounts and ratios to make which decisions; the limitations of ratio analysis.

## 6. External influences on business activity

- Government economic objectives: low and stable inflation, high and stable employment, economic growth, a favourable balance of international trade and payments.
- Government policies: fiscal policy (spending and taxation, including tariffs and quotas), monetary policy (interest rates) and supply-side policies; how each affects a business's costs, demand and decisions.
- The business cycle: growth, boom, recession, slump, recovery; drawing the cycle; the impact of changes in employment, inflation and GDP; how firms are affected differently.
- Economic influences: inflation, interest rates, unemployment, economic growth, exchange rates.
- External influences and constraints: political, environmental, social, technological, ethical and legal; legal controls on employment, consumer protection, health and safety, and the environment.
- Environmental and ethical influences: the environmental impact of business, including global warming; sustainable development; legal controls on pollution; ethical issues such as child labour and fair prices to suppliers; pressure groups, external costs and benefits, private and social costs and benefits, cost-benefit analysis.
- Business and the international economy: globalisation and its opportunities and threats; why governments introduce tariffs and quotas; why businesses become multinational and the impact of multinationals on home and host countries; appreciation and depreciation; importing and exporting, exchange-rate changes, free trade and protectionism (tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes).
