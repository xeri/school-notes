#deck:Business::2 People in business
Definition of motivation	The driving force behind behaviour — at work, the desire to work hard and do a good job
Five benefits of a satisfied, motivated workforce	Higher productivity, higher morale, better co-operation, lower absenteeism, lower labour turnover
Taylor's view of motivation	Money is the main motivator — make each task as efficient as possible and pay piece rate
Criticism of Taylor	Assumes everyone is motivated only by money; repetitive tasks demotivate
Maslow's five levels, bottom to top	Physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation
How does a business meet safety needs?	Job security, safe conditions, a contract
How does a business meet esteem needs?	Recognition, status, job titles, praise
Criticism of Maslow	Assumes everyone is the same — not all employees are motivated by the same needs
Herzberg's hygiene factors	Pay, conditions, job security, status, relationships with managers — they do not motivate, but must be satisfied or satisfaction falls
Herzberg's motivators	Achievement, recognition, responsibility, interesting work, promotion, personal development
Herzberg's practical conclusion	Improving pay removes a grievance; enriching the job creates motivation
How does piece rate work, and what is the risk?	Paid per unit produced — encourages speed but quality may fall, so costly quality control is needed
How does commission work?	A percentage of the sales the employee makes — common for sales staff
Risk of profit sharing	Less profit is left to reinvest in the business
Four examples of fringe benefits	Company car, staff discounts, health insurance, gym membership
Definition of job rotation	Employees swap tasks for limited periods — more variety and easier cover, but the tasks are no more enjoyable
Definition of job enlargement	Adding more tasks at the same level
Definition of job enrichment	Adding more challenging tasks and genuine responsibility, with training
Which non-financial motivator does Herzberg recommend?	Job enrichment
Two costs of training	Time away from production and the expense; the trained worker may also leave
Definition of chain of command	The route down which instructions pass
Definition of span of control	The number of subordinates a manager directly supervises
Definition of delegation	Entrusting tasks and responsibilities from senior managers to junior managers or employees
Who keeps accountability after delegation?	The manager — accountability cannot be delegated
Definition of de-layering	Removing a level of management
Advantages of a tall structure	Easier supervision, managers can specialise, delegation prepares staff for promotion
Disadvantages of a tall structure	High management cost and slow decision-making
Advantages of a flat structure	Lower management cost and more freedom to make decisions, which can raise motivation
Disadvantages of a flat structure	Harder to supervise and communicate; staff may lack skills; objectives may be unclear
When does a wide span of control work?	With experienced capable staff doing similar low-risk work
Five functions of management	Planning, organising, coordinating, commanding, controlling
When does autocratic leadership suit?	Emergencies, inexperienced staff, safety-critical work
When does democratic leadership suit?	Skilled experienced teams, and decisions needing buy-in
When does laissez-faire leadership suit?	Creative, highly expert professionals
Five elements of the communication process	Sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback
Main weakness of one-way communication	No feedback, so no confirmation of understanding
What is the grapevine?	Informal communication — fast but unreliable and prone to rumour
Remedy for no feedback	Ask the receiver to confirm or repeat back the instruction
Which channel suits detailed technical information?	Written, so it can be referred back to
Which channel suits sensitive news?	Face-to-face, allowing questions and reassurance
Definition of a trade union	An organisation that aims to improve its members' conditions and provide support and legal services
Definition of collective bargaining	Negotiation between a union and an employer on behalf of all members
Four forms of industrial action, most to least severe	Strike, sit-in, go-slow, work to rule (also an overtime ban)
Who loses from a strike?	Both — the business loses output and revenue, the employees lose pay
Difference between a job description and a person specification	The description states the duties; the specification states the skills and qualities needed
Two advantages of internal recruitment	Cheaper and quicker, and the candidate is already known
Two disadvantages of internal recruitment	A narrower pool, no new ideas, and it leaves another vacancy
Two advantages of external recruitment	A wider pool and new skills and fresh ideas
Purpose of induction training	To introduce a new employee to the premises, procedures, safety rules and colleagues
On-the-job training: advantage and drawback	Cheap and directly relevant; bad habits can pass on and the trainer's output falls
Off-the-job training: advantage and drawback	Broader higher-quality skills; expensive and time away from work
Difference between dismissal and redundancy	Dismissal is for the employee's conduct or performance; redundancy is because the job no longer exists
What makes a dismissal unfair?	No adequate reason, or the correct procedure was not followed
Five protections employment law gives	A written statement of terms, protection from unfair dismissal, protection from discrimination, a safe workplace, wage protection
Theory X manager	Believes workers dislike work and responsibility, so supervises closely — motivated only by money or threat
Theory Y manager	Believes workers are self-motivated and want responsibility, so supports and develops them
Definition of performance-related pay	Pay linked to an appraisal of how effectively the employee works — used where output cannot be counted
Centralised vs decentralised organisation	Centralised keeps decisions at the top (tall structures); decentralised delegates them down (flat structures)
Labour turnover formula	(employees leaving in a year ÷ number employed) × 100
Voluntary vs compulsory redundancy	Voluntary: the employee chooses to leave for compensation; compulsory: the employee is forced to leave, with compensation
Five ways to overcome communication barriers	Keep records to re-send, choose the right method for the audience, keep it short and jargon-free, train staff, use several methods
What is a sit-in?	Union members occupy the premises but do not do their normal work
