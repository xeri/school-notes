Labour productivity formula	Total output in a period ÷ number of employees
Why does higher productivity lower unit labour cost?	The same wage bill is spread over more units — provided pay and quality are unchanged
Five ways to raise productivity	Training, better equipment, improved motivation, better layout, automation
Capital-intensive: two advantages	Low unit cost at volume and consistent quality
Capital-intensive: two drawbacks	High fixed cost and inflexibility
Labour-intensive: two advantages	Lower set-up cost and the flexibility to customise
Definition of job production	One distinct product made at a time to order
Job production: advantage and drawback	Fully customised at a premium price; high unit cost and slow
Definition of batch production	Groups of identical items made before changing task
Batch production: main drawback	Downtime between batches and stocks of part-finished goods
Definition of flow production	Continuous mass production of a standardised product along a line
Flow production: two advantages	Very low unit cost and consistent quality
Flow production: two drawbacks	Huge capital cost and inflexibility; a breakdown halts everything
Which method suits high customisation?	Job production
Definition of lean production	Reducing every form of waste in the process
Definition of kaizen	Continuous small improvements
Definition of just-in-time	Holding almost no stock; materials arrive as they are needed
Three benefits of JIT	Less capital tied up in stock, lower storage cost, less waste from obsolete stock
Three risks of JIT	A late delivery halts production, no buffer for a demand surge, loses bulk discounts
Definition of fixed cost	A cost that does not change with output in the short run
Three examples of fixed cost	Rent, insurance, salaries
Definition of variable cost	A cost that changes directly with output
Three examples of variable cost	Materials, piece-rate wages, power used in production
Total cost formula	Fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output)
Contribution per unit formula	Selling price − variable cost per unit
Break-even output formula	Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Profit formula using contribution	(output × contribution per unit) − fixed costs
Margin of safety formula	Actual output − break-even output
Output needed for a target profit	(fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit
F = $4800, p = $20, v = $12 — break-even?	Contribution $8, so 4800 ÷ 8 = 600 units
At 750 units with those figures — profit and margin of safety?	Profit $1200; margin of safety 150 units
On a break-even chart, where is break-even?	Where the total revenue line crosses the total cost line
Three assumptions of break-even analysis	Constant unit price and variable cost, fixed costs unchanged across the range, everything produced is sold
What does break-even analysis ignore?	Capacity limits, demand uncertainty, and bulk discounts that make the lines non-linear
Definition of quality control	Inspecting finished output and rejecting defects
Definition of quality assurance	Designing the process so defects are prevented at each stage
Main weakness of quality control	The waste has already been created, and workers take no responsibility for it
Definition of total quality management	An organisation-wide commitment to zero defects through continuous improvement
Four costs of poor quality	Scrap and rework, warranty claims, lost customers, damaged reputation
Three costs of improving quality	Training, inspection and monitoring time, better materials
Which location factor matters most for a retailer?	Proximity to the market — passing trade and customer access
Which location factor matters most for a bulky-input factory?	Proximity to raw materials, to cut transport cost
Does the lowest rent give the lowest total cost?	No — cheap premises far from customers add transport cost and lose sales
Three benefits of locating abroad	Lower labour costs, access to a new market, avoiding tariffs
Three risks of locating abroad	Quality control at a distance, longer supply chains, exchange-rate movements
