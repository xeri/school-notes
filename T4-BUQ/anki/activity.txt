#deck:Business::1 Understanding business activity
Definition of opportunity cost	The next best alternative given up when a choice is made
Definition of added value	Selling price minus the cost of bought-in materials
Is added value the same as profit?	No — wages, rent and overheads still come out of it
Four ways to add value	Building a brand, excellent service, extra product features, convenience
Four factors of production	Land, labour, capital, enterprise
Primary sector	Extracting natural resources — farming, fishing, mining
Secondary sector	Manufacturing and construction
Tertiary sector	Providing services — retail, banking, transport
What is de-industrialisation?	The decline in the importance of the secondary (manufacturing) sector as an economy develops
Difference between private and public sector	Private is owned and run by private individuals and firms, mainly for profit; public is owned and controlled by government, mainly to provide services
Two purposes of a business plan	To force the owner to think the venture through, and to persuade a lender or investor to provide finance
Five causes of business failure	No USP, insufficient cash flow and poor financial management, poor planning and management, changes in the environment (laws, competitors, economy), overexpansion
Five ways to measure business size	Number of employees, sales revenue, output, capital employed, market share (or market capitalisation)
Why can size measures disagree?	A capital-intensive firm can have huge revenue with very few employees
Definition of economies of scale	The lower average costs of production a firm gains as it grows
Purchasing economy of scale	Buying materials in bulk earns cheaper or discounted rates
Technical economy of scale	Large firms can afford the latest specialised equipment, raising efficiency and productivity
Financial economy of scale	Banks see larger firms as less risky, so lend at cheaper interest rates
Marketing economy of scale	Larger advertising campaigns reach a wider audience, spreading the cost over more output
Four diseconomies of scale	Labour (boredom), communication (sites, languages, time zones), management (conflicting managers), agglomeration (too many different businesses to coordinate)
A $6000 campaign over 2000 vs 6000 units	$3 per unit vs $1 per unit
Liability of a sole trader	Unlimited — personally responsible for all business debts
Two advantages of being a sole trader	Total control and keeps all the profit (also easy to set up)
Two drawbacks of being a sole trader	Hard to raise capital, unlimited liability (also no continuity)
Two advantages of a partnership	Combines finance and expertise, shares the workload
Two drawbacks of a partnership	Unlimited liability and shared profits (also disagreements and no continuity)
Definition of limited liability	A shareholder can lose no more than the amount invested
What makes limited liability possible?	The company has separate legal identity from its owners
Is a public limited company in the public sector?	No — "public" means shares are offered to the public; it is a private-sector business
Difference between Ltd and plc	Ltd shares are sold privately with every shareholder's agreement; plc shares are bought and sold openly on the stock market
Two drawbacks of becoming a plc	Very expensive to set up, and easy to lose control (also financial information is public)
What does a franchisee pay the franchisor?	A start-up fee plus a percentage of sales revenue
Two benefits to the franchisee	A lower-risk start with a tried and trusted product; ongoing training and support
Two drawbacks to the franchisee	The fee and share of revenue reduce profit; must do things the franchisor's way
Two benefits to the franchisor	Quick, cheap expansion without borrowing; franchisees pay to grow the brand
Definition of a joint venture	Two or more parties pooling their resources to accomplish a specific task, sharing its profits, losses and costs
Definition of a co-operative	A business owned and democratically controlled by its members, run for their mutual benefit rather than for investors' profit
Definition of a social enterprise	A private-sector business with mainly social objectives whose surpluses are reinvested rather than used to maximise owners' profit
What does SMART stand for?	Specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time specific
Why do objectives change over time?	A start-up aims to survive, then to grow, then perhaps to maximise profit
Give an example of conflicting objectives	Growth may require cutting prices, reducing short-run profit
Definition of corporate social responsibility	Operating ethically in three areas — people, planet and profit (the triple bottom line)
Three arguments for CSR	Better brand image and sales, a possible USP, easier recruitment (and avoids fines and boycotts)
Three arguments against CSR	Higher costs cut profit, can slow expansion, shareholders may prefer dividends
Difference between ethics and legal compliance	Ethics considers society's interests beyond the law — what is right, not just what is legal
Definition of a stakeholder	Anyone who has a direct interest in the performance of a business
Is a shareholder the same as a stakeholder?	No — a shareholder is one kind of stakeholder
Main aim of employees as stakeholders	Fair pay, job security and good working conditions
Main aim of suppliers as stakeholders	Regular orders and prompt payment
Give an example of stakeholder conflict	Shareholders want dividends; employees want higher wages and job security — both come from the same profit
Effect of technology on operations	Automation cuts unit costs and raises consistency, but needs capital and may cut jobs
Effect of technology on employment	Demand shifts from unskilled to skilled work, creating retraining and redundancy costs
Is profit a measure of business size?	No — profit measures performance and efficiency, not size
Risk-bearing economy of scale	A wider product range, more customers and more locations spread the risk of failure
Difference between a merger and a takeover	A merger is a mutual agreement to join into one business; in a takeover one business buys another, which comes under its control
Horizontal integration	Joining a firm in the same industry at the same stage of production — less competition, more market share
Backward vs forward vertical integration	Backward: taking over a firm closer to the raw materials; forward: a firm closer to the end consumer
Conglomerate integration	Joining a firm in an unrelated industry — spreads risk
Three reasons some firms stay small	The type of industry (personal service), a small market, the owners' wish to keep control
Who owns and who runs a limited company?	Shareholders own it; directors, elected at the AGM, run it
What does a deed of partnership set out?	Voting rights, each partner's responsibilities, and how profit and loss are split — it is not a legal requirement
Free-market economy	Resources privately owned, no government control, prices set by supply and demand
Command (planned) economy	Government owns and plans all resources; no private businesses
Definition of privatisation	Selling government-owned businesses to the private sector
Definition of a public corporation	A business wholly owned by the government, run by a board of directors it appoints
Difference between an aim and an objective	An aim is where the business wants to go; an objective is a measurable target for achieving it
Definition of profit satisficing	Making enough profit, without profit being the only focus
Kaitiakitanga	Guardianship of natural resources — e.g. sustainability initiatives
Manaakitanga	The ethic of caring and hospitality — a welcoming workplace, looking after guests
Tino rangatiratanga	Self-determination, ownership and active control — taking responsibility for your own area
