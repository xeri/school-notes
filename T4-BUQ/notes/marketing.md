# Marketing

## The role of marketing

Marketing identifies customer needs and builds an offer that meets them **profitably**. Its objectives: to identify and satisfy customer needs, to maintain or increase market share, to build customer loyalty and brand image, and to target a chosen segment effectively.

**Market segmentation** divides a market into groups with shared characteristics — by age, income, gender, lifestyle, location or usage. It allows a focused mix and avoids wasted promotion, but narrows potential sales and costs more if several versions are needed.

| | Mass market | Niche market |
|---|---|---|
| Size | large, broad appeal | small, specific needs |
| Competition | intense | often fewer direct rivals |
| Unit cost | low — economies of scale | higher |
| Price | competitive | often premium |
| Risk | heavy investment | vulnerable to a single change in taste |

**Market share** compares one firm's sales with the total market's, using the **same period and the same measurement basis**:

$$\text{market share} = \frac{\text{firm's sales}}{\text{total market sales}}\times100$$

**Market growth** is the percentage increase in the size of the whole market.

## Market research

**Primary (field) research** collects new data for the present purpose: questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing. Specific and up to date, but slow and expensive.

**Secondary (desk) research** uses data that already exists: internal sales records, government statistics, trade journals, competitors' published accounts, internet sources. Cheap and quick, but may be out of date, too general, or collected for a different purpose.

**Qualitative** data explains *why* customers behave as they do (opinions, motives); **quantitative** data gives numbers that can be measured and compared.

**Sampling.** A sample stands in for the whole population because asking everyone is impossible. A **random** sample gives each person an equal chance; a **quota** sample fills set numbers from each segment.

**Judging reliability** — check:

- **how the sample was selected** — surveying only current customers misses everyone who rejected the product
- **sample size** — too small and the result is chance
- **question wording** — leading questions produce the answer you wanted
- **date** — tastes and competitors change
- **who paid for it** and why

An expensive survey can still be biased. Say *what is wrong with the method*, not just "the sample was small".

## Product

Product covers features, quality, design, branding, packaging and the service around it.

**Differentiation** makes the product distinguishable from rivals' — by design, quality, brand image or service — which supports a higher price and customer loyalty.

**Branding** gives recognition, allows a premium price, and builds repeat purchase, but takes sustained investment and means one scandal damages the whole range.

**Packaging** protects the product, makes it easy to transport and store, carries legal information, and promotes at the point of sale.

**Product life cycle:**

| Stage | Sales | Typical actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Development** | none; costs only | research, testing, no revenue |
| **Introduction** | low, growing | informative promotion, limited distribution, penetration or skimming price |
| **Growth** | rising fast | widen distribution, build brand, first profits |
| **Maturity** | peak, flattening | competitive pricing, persuasive promotion, extension strategies |
| **Decline** | falling | cut price, reduce promotion, withdraw or replace |

**Extension strategies** prolong maturity: a new version or variant, new packaging, a new target market or country, more promotion, a new use for the product.

A **product portfolio** spreads risk, so that a product in decline is balanced by one in growth.

## Price

| Strategy | Method | When it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** | add a fixed mark-up to unit cost | simple; ignores demand and competitors |
| **Penetration** | low price on launch to build volume and share | competitive market, price-sensitive buyers |
| **Skimming** | high launch price, lowered later | genuinely new or technically advanced product |
| **Competitive** | match or undercut rivals | similar products, strong competition |
| **Promotional** | temporary reduction | clearing stock, launching, boosting a quiet period |
| **Psychological** | $9.99 rather than $10 | retail, to appear cheaper |

**None of these guarantees profit.** A discount can raise volume while cutting contribution per unit.

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how much quantity demanded responds to a price change.

- **Elastic** demand (many substitutes, non-essential): a price *rise* reduces total revenue.
- **Inelastic** demand (few substitutes, essential, strong brand): a price *rise* increases total revenue.

*Numerical example.* A seller shifts 400 bottles at $20 — revenue $8000. Cutting the price to $18 requires $8000/18 = 444.4$, so **445 whole bottles**, merely to match the old revenue. And matching revenue is not matching profit: the extra units carry extra variable cost and the promotion itself costs money. Compare **contribution** after variable costs, less the extra fixed cost of the campaign.

## Promotion

| Method | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Advertising** (TV, radio, print, online, outdoor) | inform or persuade a wide audience |
| **Sales promotion** (BOGOF, coupons, free gifts, competitions, loyalty cards) | short-term boost to volume, encourage trial |
| **Personal selling** | explain a complex or expensive product |
| **Public relations / sponsorship** | build image and goodwill |
| **Direct mail and email** | target known customers cheaply |
| **Social media** | cheap reach, interaction, shareable content — but complaints are public |

Promotion needs an **objective** and a **measure of response** — not simply a large audience. Judge cost-effectiveness: extra contribution generated against the cost of the campaign. A campaign reaching millions is poor value if almost none of them are in the target segment.

## Place

**Place** is how the product reaches the buyer.

| Channel | Route | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | producer → consumer | highest margin, full control; needs own sales effort |
| Retailer | producer → retailer → consumer | reach, convenience; retailer takes a margin |
| Wholesaler | producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer | handles small orders and storage; longest, thinnest margin |
| E-commerce | producer → consumer online | national or global reach, low overheads, 24-hour selling; delivery costs, returns, no personal contact |

Choose the channel by the product (perishable, bulky, technical), the market (local or international), the cost, and the degree of control wanted.

## Marketing strategy

The four elements must **support each other** and the target segment. A premium handmade product needs a high price, quality packaging, selective promotion in the right media, and an exclusive channel that reaches customers willing to pay. Selling it cheaply in a discount store destroys the brand that justified the price.

When a question asks you to evaluate a mix, check each element for **consistency with the others and with the segment** — that is where the marks are.

## Entering new markets

Reasons to expand into another country: a saturated home market, a larger customer base, spreading risk across economies, lower production costs, extending a product's life cycle.

Risks and requirements:

- research into customer tastes, incomes and buying habits
- language, culture and brand-name translation
- legal differences: labelling, safety standards, advertising rules
- distribution and delivery infrastructure
- exchange-rate movements affecting revenue and costs
- tariffs and quotas raising cost or limiting volume

A successful domestic mix usually needs **adapting**, not copying. Methods of entry include exporting, a local agent, a joint venture with a local firm, a franchise, or direct investment — each trading control against risk and cost.
