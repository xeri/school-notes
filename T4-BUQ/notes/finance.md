# Financial information and decisions

## Why businesses need finance

| Need | Typical time horizon |
|---|---|
| Start-up capital (premises and equipment, materials, fees and licences) | long term |
| Survival capital (covering losses; paying wages, power and phone bills) | short term |
| Working capital (day-to-day running costs) | short term |
| Expansion capital (R&D, new technology, operations overseas, takeovers) | long term |

**Capital expenditure** is spending on non-current assets (premises, vehicles, equipment); **revenue expenditure** is spending on running costs (wages, rent, phone bills). **Fixed capital** is invested in non-current assets; **working capital** finances day-to-day operations; **venture capital** is finance for start-ups and small, high-risk, high-growth businesses.

**Match the term of the finance to the purpose.** Long-term finance pays for long-term assets; short-term finance for short-term costs. Buying a building with an overdraft is a classic error — the overdraft may be recalled long before the building pays for itself.

## Sources of finance

**Internal:**

| Source | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profit | not repaid, no interest, no loss of control | new or small firms may have little; means lower dividends |
| Owner's savings | quick to use, no interest | small owners often have little; personal risk |
| Sale of unwanted assets or stock | easy and cheap, nothing to repay; less storage | may take time to find a buyer; small firms have few spare assets |
| Sale and leaseback | sells a non-current asset for cash but keeps using it by renting it back | lease payments can be expensive; need a leasing company willing to buy |

**External:** lenders want to know the business has good prospects, is well managed and profitable, can repay with interest, and has assets to offer as **collateral**.

| Source | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Overdraft | quick, easy cover for short-term bills, interest only on what is used | higher interest than a loan; **repayable on demand** |
| Credit card | interest-free period; buy now, pay later | very high interest if not paid off; limited amount |
| Bank (commercial) loan or mortgage | fixed sum for capital spending, fixed interest rate, chosen repayment period | monthly repayments with interest; security required |
| Trade credit | buy now, pay later (e.g. within 30 days), no interest | discounts lost and future supplies refused if payment is late |
| Hire purchase | spreads the cost of vehicles and equipment; the asset is owned once the full price plus interest is paid | a deposit is usually required; total cost higher |
| Leasing | use an asset without buying it; the leasing company often maintains it; can buy at a discount or return it at the end | leasing charges can be high |
| Venture capital | from a venture capital organisation or business angel; not repaid, no interest | the investor takes a share of ownership and management |
| Share issue | large, **permanent** capital — shares are never repaid | complex and expensive; dilutes control; can lower the share price |
| Debentures | long-term loan certificates sold by limited companies, for major assets or expansion | must be repaid in full with interest at the end of the term |
| Government support (grants, tax concessions, low-cost loans) | a grant is not repaid; available when banks refuse | conditions attached, such as keeping a number of jobs |
| Microfinance | reaches very small businesses without collateral | small sums, often high rates |
| Crowdfunding | raises money and tests demand at once | may fail publicly; reveals the idea to competitors |

**Short-term and long-term sources.**

| Short-term (repaid within a year) | Long-term (more than a year) |
|---|---|
| overdraft, trade credit, credit card, short-term loan | bank loan, mortgage, hire purchase, leasing, debentures |
| for working capital: wages, stock, bills | for non-current assets and expansion |
| | equity: share issue, venture capital, owner's capital, retained profit |

**Debt** (loans, overdrafts, debentures) must be repaid with interest but keeps control with the owners. **Equity** (shares, venture capital) is never repaid and has no interest, but gives away part of the ownership, control and future profit.

**Alternative sources.** **Microfinance** is very small loans to people who cannot borrow from banks, often in developing countries, to start tiny businesses. **Crowdfunding** raises small amounts from many people online, often in return for a reward or a share. Both matter because banks refuse many new, small or poor applicants who have no collateral or track record.

**Choosing a source — the criteria:** what sources are available (the size and legal form of the business — a sole trader cannot sell shares — its track record and existing loans), what the money is for (running costs or non-current assets), how much and how quickly it is needed, the cost (retained profit and grants are cheapest), the effect on **control**, and the risk. Risk is measured by **gearing**, the proportion of finance that is debt rather than equity; the school slides describe a firm as highly geared when more than about 60% of its finance is debt. Too much debt raises the risk of failure.

*Decision example.* A two-owner repair workshop wants expensive diagnostic equipment but values personal service. **Retained profit** avoids interest and loss of control but may take too long to accumulate. A **loan** gives immediate access but adds repayments even in quiet months. **Incorporating** could attract equity, at the cost of administration and diluted control. Judge against the expected cash inflows and the owners' willingness to share decisions.

## Why cash matters

**Cash** is the money a business holds in notes, coins and its bank account. A business needs it to pay wages, suppliers, rent, loan repayments and tax **on time**. A firm that cannot pay its debts when they fall due can be forced to close even if it is profitable — so in the short run cash matters more than profit for survival.

## Cash is not profit

**Profit** is revenue less expenses for a period. **Cash flow** is money actually entering and leaving.

- A **credit sale** creates revenue and profit now but cash later.
- A **loan** brings in cash but is neither revenue nor profit.
- **Buying a machine** is a huge cash outflow but only a small expense each year (depreciation).

A profitable, growing business can run out of cash if it pays suppliers before its customers pay it. This is **overtrading**, and it is why cash-flow forecasting exists.

## Cash-flow forecast

A **cash-flow forecast** estimates the cash coming in and going out of a business over future months. A full forecast lists each item:

| Item | January | February | March |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Receipts (cash inflows)** | | | |
| Cash sales | 6,000 | 5,000 | 7,500 |
| Payments from credit customers | 2,000 | 2,000 | 2,500 |
| **Total receipts** | **8,000** | **7,000** | **10,000** |
| **Payments (cash outflows)** | | | |
| Materials | 3,000 | 3,500 | 4,000 |
| Wages | 2,500 | 2,500 | 2,500 |
| Rent and power | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Loan repayment | 0 | 500 | 500 |
| **Total payments** | **7,000** | **8,000** | **8,500** |
| **Net cash flow** | **1,000** | **−1,000** | **1,500** |
| Opening balance | 2,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| **Closing balance** | **3,000** | **2,000** | **3,500** |

- Net cash flow = receipts − payments
- Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow
- **This month's closing balance is next month's opening balance**

Note that February has a **negative net cash flow but a positive closing balance** — the business is still solvent. The distinction decides whether an overdraft is actually needed.

**Completing a forecast with gaps:** work down each column (total receipts − total payments = net cash flow; opening + net = closing), then carry the closing balance across to the next month's opening balance. A missing payment can be found backwards: payment = total payments − the other payments.

**Interpreting a forecast — what to comment on:** whether net cash flow is positive every month; the trend in the closing balance (rising or falling); months where the closing balance is negative (an overdraft will be needed — how much and for how long); the cause (seasonal sales, a large one-off payment, rising costs); and how reliable the estimates are.

**Liquidity** is how easily an asset turns into cash (cash, then bank balance, debtors, inventory, equipment, property). If cash outflows exceed inflows and the business cannot raise cash quickly, it has a liquidity problem; if it cannot pay its debts on time it becomes **insolvent** and may be forced into **liquidation**.

A cash-flow forecast shows when a surplus or deficit is expected, how much cash to keep in reserve, and how much must be borrowed to avoid insolvency — and it supports a loan application. Too much cash in reserve is idle; too little risks running out.

**Causes of cash-flow problems:** holding excess inventory; expanding too quickly (overtrading); investing too much in non-current assets (leasing may be wiser); borrowing too much, so interest payments rise; poor credit control (customers allowed too long to pay, or bad debts); inflation raising costs and wages; seasonal sales.

**Ways to improve cash flow** — either bring cash in sooner or pay it out later. **Solutions and their costs:** more debt finance or an overdraft (interest); delay payments to suppliers for as long as legally possible (strained relations, lost discounts); call in debtors early or offer prompt-payment discounts (lower revenue); improve credit control by checking customers' credit history before selling on credit; reduce stock (risk of stock-outs); lease rather than buy; sell assets; inject owner's capital.

A forecast depends on **estimated** timings and amounts, so test it against a pessimistic case.

## Working capital

$$\text{working capital} = \text{current assets} - \text{current liabilities}$$

It is the finance available for day-to-day operations. **It is not the bank balance** — it includes inventory and trade receivables and nets off what is owed in the short term. The school slides also express working capital as current assets **compared with** current liabilities, as a ratio: above 1:1 means positive working capital; below 1:1 (say 0.8:1, only 80 cents of current assets per \$1 owed) means negative working capital, so suppliers and banks may stop giving credit. (That ratio is the same calculation as the current ratio.)

**To improve working capital:** increase current assets — improve cash inflows (discounts for early payment), cut costs (cheaper suppliers), sell unwanted assets, reduce bad debts; or reduce current liabilities — pay off short-term debts, or refinance them with a long-term loan.

## Profit

**Profit** is made when revenue is greater than total costs. It matters to a private-sector business because it is the **reward for taking risks** (enterprise), a **source of finance** for growth (retained profit), a signal to lenders and investors that the business is succeeding, and the money from which dividends are paid. Public-sector bodies and social enterprises may aim to break even or reinvest any surplus instead.

## Income statement

An **income statement** summarises a business's revenue and costs over a period, usually a year, to show the profit or loss. It is a legal requirement, reports performance to stakeholders, allows comparison with last year, is wanted by investors and lenders, and helps forecasting. It has three sections: the **trading account** (gross profit), the **profit and loss account** (expenses, operating profit, interest, tax, profit for the year) and the **appropriation account** (how that profit is split between dividends and retained earnings).

| | \$ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 200,000 |
| Less cost of sales | (120,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **80,000** |
| Less expenses (rent, wages, marketing) | (50,000) |
| **Operating profit** | **30,000** |
| Less interest | (5,000) |
| **Profit before tax** | **25,000** |
| Less corporate tax at 28% | (7,000) |
| **Profit for the year** | **18,000** |
| Less dividends to shareholders | (8,000) |
| **Retained profit** | **10,000** |

- **Cost of sales** (cost of goods sold) = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory.
- **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales. It shows the margin on trading before overheads. Raise it by raising the price or cutting the cost of sales.
- **Operating profit** = gross profit − operating expenses. Raise it by raising gross profit or cutting expenses.

Profit for the year is used to pay **dividends** to owners or is kept as **retained profit** to finance future growth.

**Using an income statement to decide** (the IGCSE syllabus tests reading and using one, not building one from scratch): compare this year with last year (is revenue growing? is gross profit growing as fast as revenue?), compare with a similar firm, find which costs are rising, judge whether there is enough profit to fund an expansion, and decide whether to raise prices or cut costs.

**Profit is not cash.** A firm sells 25,000 items on credit at 3 dollars each, having paid suppliers 2 dollars each in cash. Its profit is 25,000 dollars, but its net cash flow is minus 50,000 dollars, because no customer has paid yet.

## Statement of financial position

A snapshot of what the business owns and owes **at a single date**.

| | \$ | \$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Non-current assets** | | |
| Premises | 110,000 | |
| Equipment and vehicles | 40,000 | |
| | | **150,000** |
| **Current assets** | | |
| Inventory | 30,000 | |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | 20,000 | |
| Cash | 10,000 | |
| | | **60,000** |
| **Total assets** | | **210,000** |
| **Current liabilities** | | |
| Trade payables (creditors) | 30,000 | |
| Overdraft | 10,000 | |
| | | **(40,000)** |
| **Non-current liabilities** | | |
| Long-term bank loan | | **(70,000)** |
| **Net assets** | | **100,000** |
| **Equity** (financed by) | | |
| Share capital | 60,000 | |
| Retained profit | 40,000 | |
| **Total equity** | | **100,000** |

Assets always equal the capital and liabilities financing them: **assets − liabilities = equity**, so **assets = liabilities + equity**. Here: 150,000 + 60,000 − 40,000 − 70,000 = 100,000.

**Interpreting the statement** (the syllabus tests reading one, not building one):

- **How is the business financed?** Here 100,000 from equity and 110,000 from liabilities (70,000 long-term loan plus 40,000 current). Heavy borrowing means interest costs and risk.
- **What does it own?** Mainly premises and equipment — useful as **collateral** for a loan, or to sell and lease back.
- **Can it pay its short-term debts?** Current assets 60,000 against current liabilities 40,000.
- **Could it raise cash?** It could sell some of its 30,000 of inventory, chase its 20,000 of receivables, or sell unused non-current assets.

Current items last less than 12 months; non-current items more than 12 months. **Intangible** non-current assets — goodwill, patents, trademarks, copyright — cannot be seen or easily valued. **Equity** is share capital (money raised by selling shares) plus retained earnings built up over the years.

## Ratio analysis

**Profitability:**

$$\text{gross profit margin} = \frac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100$$
$$\text{operating profit margin} = \frac{\text{operating profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100$$
$$\text{ROCE} = \frac{\text{operating profit}}{\text{capital employed}}\times100$$

This course uses **operating profit** as the ROCE numerator — do not silently substitute another figure. **Capital employed** is the long-term finance in the business: equity + non-current liabilities.

Using the statements above: gross margin $= 80/200 \times 100 = 40\%$, operating margin $= 30/200 \times 100 = 15\%$. Capital employed $= 100{,}000 + 70{,}000 = 170{,}000$, so ROCE $= 30/170 \times 100 = 17.6\%$.

A GPM of 40% means 40 cents of gross profit per \$1 of sales. ROCE should be higher than a bank's interest rate, or the owners would do better to leave their money in the bank. Margins and ROCE rise if costs fall, prices rise without losing many customers, or capital is used more efficiently.

**Liquidity:**

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets}}{\text{current liabilities}}$$
$$\text{acid-test ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets} - \text{inventory}}{\text{current liabilities}}$$

Current ratio $= 60/40 = 1.5$, written 1.5:1 — 1.50 dollars of current assets for every dollar of current liabilities. Below 1:1 the business could become insolvent; too high suggests cash or stock sitting idle. The school slides give an "ideal" range of **1:1 to 1.5:1** for both liquidity ratios.

The **acid-test** removes inventory because stock may not sell quickly — the stricter test of whether short-term debts can actually be met. *Example:* current assets 60,000 including inventory of 30,000, current liabilities 40,000 → acid test $= (60{,}000 - 30{,}000)/40{,}000 = 0.75{:}1$. Much lower than the current ratio, so the firm relies heavily on inventory; it could hold less stock (JIT) or, if urgent, sell stock off.

| Ratio | Tells you | Higher is… | Ways to improve it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | profit made on trading, before overheads | better | raise prices; find cheaper suppliers |
| Operating profit margin (the syllabus calls it "profit margin") | profit after all operating expenses | better | the above, plus cut overheads |
| ROCE | profit earned on the long-term capital invested | better | raise profit or use capital more efficiently |
| Current ratio | ability to pay short-term debts | better up to about 1.5–2; too high means idle assets | raise current assets or cut current liabilities |
| Acid-test ratio | the same, without relying on selling stock | about 1 is safe | cut inventory, chase receivables |

**Profitability** is how well a business turns sales and capital into profit; **liquidity** is its ability to pay its short-term debts. A profitable firm can still be illiquid — it may have all its money tied up in inventory and equipment.

**Who uses accounts, and why:** owners and shareholders (return on their investment), managers (performance and decisions), lenders (can the loan be repaid), suppliers (will we be paid), employees (job security and pay claims), government (tax), potential investors and competitors.

| User | Decision the ratios help with |
|---|---|
| Bank or lender | lend or not? Looks at liquidity (can interest and repayments be met?) and profitability |
| Shareholder or investor | invest, hold or sell shares? Looks at ROCE and profit margins compared with other firms |
| Managers | which costs to cut, whether to expand, which product to drop |
| Supplier | give trade credit or not? Looks at the current and acid-test ratios |

**Limitations of ratio analysis.** A ratio means nothing alone — it needs comparison **over time** or with **similar firms in the same industry**. It requires consistent accounting definitions. It tells you nothing about staff morale, brand strength, the quality of the assets, or the state of the market. And a high current ratio does not guarantee immediate access to cash.
