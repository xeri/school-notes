# Financial information and decisions

## Why businesses need finance

| Need | Typical time horizon |
|---|---|
| Start-up capital (premises, equipment, initial stock) | long term |
| Working capital (day-to-day: wages, stock, bills) | short term |
| Expansion (new site, new machinery, a takeover) | long term |
| Replacing worn-out assets | long term |
| Covering a temporary cash shortfall | short term |

**Match the term of the finance to the purpose.** Buying a building with an overdraft is a classic error — the overdraft may be recalled long before the building pays for itself.

## Sources of finance

**Internal:**

| Source | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profit | no interest, no loss of control | limited by past profits; cannot be used twice |
| Sale of unwanted assets | raises cash from idle resources | one-off; may be needed later |
| Owner's savings | quick, no interest | limited; personal risk |

**External:**

| Source | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Bank loan | fixed sum, planned repayments over years | interest; security usually required |
| Overdraft | flexible short-term cover, interest only on what is used | expensive; **repayable on demand** |
| Share capital | no repayment date, no interest | dilutes ownership and control; dividends expected |
| Debentures | long-term borrowing without diluting ownership | interest must be paid whatever the profit |
| Trade credit | delays supplier payment, effectively free | lost discounts; damages supply relationships if abused |
| Hire purchase / leasing | spreads the cost of equipment | total cost higher; leasing never gives ownership |
| Government grant | may not need repaying | conditions attached; not always available |
| Microfinance | reaches very small businesses without collateral | small sums, often high rates |
| Crowdfunding | raises money and tests demand at once | may fail publicly; reveals the idea to competitors |

**Choosing a source — the criteria:** how much is needed, what for, for how long, the cost (interest), the effect on **control**, the risk (gearing), the legal form of the business (a sole trader cannot sell shares), and whether existing borrowing or assets allow more.

*Decision example.* A two-owner repair workshop wants expensive diagnostic equipment but values personal service. **Retained profit** avoids interest and loss of control but may take too long to accumulate. A **loan** gives immediate access but adds repayments even in quiet months. **Incorporating** could attract equity, at the cost of administration and diluted control. Judge against the expected cash inflows and the owners' willingness to share decisions.

## Cash is not profit

**Profit** is revenue less expenses for a period. **Cash flow** is money actually entering and leaving.

- A **credit sale** creates revenue and profit now but cash later.
- A **loan** brings in cash but is neither revenue nor profit.
- **Buying a machine** is a huge cash outflow but only a small expense each year (depreciation).

A profitable, growing business can run out of cash if it pays suppliers before its customers pay it. This is **overtrading**, and it is why cash-flow forecasting exists.

## Cash-flow forecast

| Item | January | February | March |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Opening balance | 2,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| Receipts | 8,000 | 7,000 | 10,000 |
| Payments | 7,000 | 8,000 | 8,500 |
| **Net cash flow** | **1,000** | **−1,000** | **1,500** |
| **Closing balance** | **3,000** | **2,000** | **3,500** |

- Net cash flow = receipts − payments
- Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow
- **This month's closing balance is next month's opening balance**

Note that February has a **negative net cash flow but a positive closing balance** — the business is still solvent. The distinction decides whether an overdraft is actually needed.

**Causes of cash-flow problems:** overtrading, too much stock, customers paying late, seasonal demand, unexpected costs, over-investment in fixed assets, low profit.

**Solutions and their costs:** arrange an overdraft (interest); chase debtors or offer prompt-payment discounts (lower revenue); negotiate longer credit from suppliers (lost discounts, strained relations); reduce stock (risk of stock-outs); delay or lease equipment rather than buy; sell assets; inject owner's capital.

A forecast depends on **estimated** timings and amounts, so test it against a pessimistic case.

## Working capital

$$\text{working capital} = \text{current assets} - \text{current liabilities}$$

It is the finance available for day-to-day operations. **It is not the bank balance** — it includes inventory and trade receivables and nets off what is owed in the short term.

## Income statement

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 200,000 |
| Less cost of sales | (120,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **80,000** |
| Less expenses (rent, wages, marketing) | (50,000) |
| **Operating profit** | **30,000** |
| Less interest | (5,000) |
| **Profit for the year** | **25,000** |

- **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales. It shows the margin on trading before overheads.
- **Operating profit** = gross profit − operating expenses.

Profit is used to pay **dividends** to owners or is kept as **retained profit** to finance future growth.

## Statement of financial position

A snapshot of what the business owns and owes **at a single date**.

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Non-current assets (premises, equipment, vehicles) | 150,000 |
| Current assets (inventory, receivables, cash) | 60,000 |
| Current liabilities (payables, overdraft) | (40,000) |
| Non-current liabilities (long-term loan) | (70,000) |
| **Net assets** | **100,000** |
| Financed by: share capital + retained profit | 100,000 |

Assets always equal the capital and liabilities financing them.

## Ratio analysis

**Profitability:**

$$\text{gross profit margin} = \frac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100$$
$$\text{operating profit margin} = \frac{\text{operating profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100$$
$$\text{ROCE} = \frac{\text{operating profit}}{\text{capital employed}}\times100$$

This course uses **operating profit** as the ROCE numerator — do not silently substitute another figure.

Using the statements above: gross margin $= 40\%$, operating margin $= 15\%$, ROCE $= 30/100 \times 100 = 30\%$.

**Liquidity:**

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets}}{\text{current liabilities}}$$
$$\text{acid-test ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets} - \text{inventory}}{\text{current liabilities}}$$

Current ratio $= 60/40 = 1.5$. A ratio around 1.5–2 is often considered comfortable: too low risks insolvency, too high suggests cash or stock sitting idle.

The **acid-test** removes inventory because stock may not sell quickly — the stricter test of whether short-term debts can actually be met.

**Who uses accounts, and why:** owners and shareholders (return on their investment), managers (performance and decisions), lenders (can the loan be repaid), suppliers (will we be paid), employees (job security and pay claims), government (tax), potential investors and competitors.

**Limitations of ratio analysis.** A ratio means nothing alone — it needs comparison **over time** or with **similar firms in the same industry**. It requires consistent accounting definitions. It tells you nothing about staff morale, brand strength, the quality of the assets, or the state of the market. And a high current ratio does not guarantee immediate access to cash.
