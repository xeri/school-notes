# Understanding business activity

## Purpose of business

Businesses exist because human **wants** are unlimited while resources are **scarce**. Every choice therefore carries an **opportunity cost** — the next best alternative given up.

A business combines inputs (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and **adds value**: the difference between the selling price and the cost of the bought-in materials. A café buying coffee beans for $0.40 and selling a cup at $4.50 adds $4.10 of value through grinding, brewing, service and atmosphere. Added value is **not** profit — wages, rent and power still have to be paid from it.

Ways to add value: branding, better quality, convenience, speed, after-sales service, attractive packaging.

## Classification of business

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | extracting natural resources | farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| **Secondary** | manufacturing and construction | car assembly, baking, housebuilding |
| **Tertiary** | providing services | retail, banking, transport, education |

As an economy develops, employment usually shifts from primary to secondary and then to tertiary — **de-industrialisation** describes the fall in secondary-sector share.

**Private sector** businesses are owned by individuals and usually aim at profit. **Public sector** organisations are owned by government and aim to provide a service, often one that would be unprofitable or unfair to leave to the market.

## Business plans and failure

A **business plan** states the business idea, the owners, the market and competitors, the marketing plan, the operations plan, and the financial forecasts (cash flow and break-even). Its purposes: to force the owner to think the venture through, and to persuade a lender or investor to provide finance.

Common causes of **business failure**:

- poor cash-flow management — running out of cash even while profitable
- lack of market research, so the product does not meet a real need
- poor management, especially of growth that outruns the firm's systems
- over-borrowing, so repayments cannot be met in a quiet period
- external shocks: recession, a new competitor, a sudden cost rise

New businesses are most vulnerable because they lack a reputation, reserves and established customers.

## Measuring business size

| Measure | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Number of employees | easy to find | a capital-intensive firm may be huge with few staff |
| Revenue (sales turnover) | reflects trading scale | high revenue can hide low margins |
| Capital employed | reflects investment | ignores how productively it is used |
| Market share | shows competitive position | needs a clear definition of "the market" |

**The measures do not always agree.** An online retailer may be large by revenue but small by employees. Always say which measure you are using, and compare like with like.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** reduce the **average cost per unit** as output expands.

| Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Purchasing | bulk buying earns discounts per unit |
| Technical | specialist machinery spreads its cost over more units |
| Managerial | specialist managers' salaries spread over more output |
| Marketing | one advertising campaign supports a bigger volume |
| Financial | larger firms borrow more cheaply, being seen as lower risk |

*Numerical illustration.* A $6000 advertising campaign costs $3 per unit over 2000 sales but $1 per unit over 6000 sales — assuming the same campaign supports both volumes. **Total cost and cost per unit are different things.**

**Diseconomies of scale** raise average cost when a firm grows too large:

- **poor communication** — messages distort through more layers
- **poor coordination** — decisions take longer, departments duplicate work
- **low morale** — employees feel like a number, so effort and attendance fall

A bigger firm is therefore not automatically safer, more profitable or more efficient.

## Forms of ownership

Choose the form by comparing **control, finance, risk** and the owner's **objectives**.

| Form | Owners | Liability | Finance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | one | **unlimited** | own savings, loans, retained profit | total control, keeps all profit, easy to set up; hard to raise capital, no continuity |
| **Partnership** | usually 2–20 | **unlimited** (unless limited partnership) | partners' capital | combines finance and expertise, shares workload; disagreements, shared profit, each partner bound by the others' decisions |
| **Private limited (Ltd)** | shareholders | **limited** | shares sold privately | separate legal identity, continuity; shares cannot be sold publicly, accounts must be filed |
| **Public limited (plc)** | shareholders | **limited** | shares offered to the public | can raise very large sums; expensive to set up, must publish accounts, risk of takeover, loss of control |

**Limited liability** means a shareholder can lose no more than the amount invested. It exists because the company is a **separate legal person** from its owners. Note that a bank may still demand a personal guarantee, which reintroduces personal exposure.

**A public limited company is a private-sector business.** "Public" refers to the sale of shares to the public, not to government ownership — a very common confusion.

**Franchise.** The **franchisee** pays the **franchisor** an initial fee plus ongoing royalties, and trades using the franchisor's brand, products and systems.

| For the franchisee | For the franchisor |
|---|---|
| ✓ established brand, lower failure risk, training and support, national advertising | ✓ rapid expansion with someone else's capital, royalty income |
| ✗ fees reduce profit, little freedom over product or pricing, reputation depends on other franchisees | ✗ less control over day-to-day standards, reputation at risk from a poor franchisee |

**Joint venture.** Two businesses create a separate shared enterprise, pooling expertise, costs and local knowledge, and sharing both risk and profit. Disputes over strategy and differing management cultures are the main risks.

**Co-operative.** Owned and democratically run by its members (workers or consumers), who share the profits. Motivation can be high, but raising capital and making quick decisions are harder.

**Social enterprise.** Trades in order to pursue a social or environmental purpose; any surplus is reinvested to support that purpose rather than distributed to owners.

## Aims and objectives

Objectives should be **SMART** — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.

| Objective | Typical reason |
|---|---|
| Survival | a new business, or a recession |
| Profit | to reward owners and fund investment |
| Growth | economies of scale, market power, owner ambition |
| Market share | to strengthen position against rivals |
| Customer satisfaction | repeat purchase and loyalty |
| Social objectives | a social enterprise or a public-sector body |

Objectives **change over time**: a start-up aims to survive, then to grow, then perhaps to maximise profit. They also differ by sector — a public-sector hospital pursues service standards, not profit.

Objectives can **conflict**: growth may require cutting prices and therefore short-run profit.

## CSR and ethics

**Corporate social responsibility** is a business accepting responsibility for its effects on employees, customers, the community and the environment beyond what the law requires.

| Arguments for | Arguments against |
|---|---|
| better reputation and sales | higher costs, so higher prices or lower profit |
| easier recruitment and lower staff turnover | shareholders may prefer dividends |
| avoids fines, boycotts and bad publicity | competitors who do not comply undercut you |
| reduces waste, which can cut costs | benefits are long-term and hard to measure |

**Ethics** concerns what is *right*, which is a different boundary from what is *legal*. Paying the legal minimum wage can be legal but criticised as unethical. Do not assume every ethical change increases profit — judge the specific case.

## Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Main aim |
|---|---|
| Owners / shareholders | profit, dividends, rising share value |
| Employees | fair pay, job security, good conditions |
| Managers | authority, bonuses, career growth |
| Customers | quality, low prices, reliability, service |
| Suppliers | regular orders, prompt payment |
| Lenders / banks | interest paid, loan repaid |
| Government | tax revenue, employment, legal compliance |
| Local community | jobs, little pollution and congestion |

**Conflicts** are the examinable point. Raising wages pleases employees but reduces shareholders' profit. Cutting costs by relocating pleases shareholders but harms employees and the old community. Lowering prices pleases customers but may squeeze suppliers. Always name **both** sides and say which stakeholder has the most influence and why.

## Technology and business

| Area | Effect |
|---|---|
| Operations | automation and robotics cut unit costs and raise consistency, but need capital and may cut jobs |
| Marketing | e-commerce, social media and targeted advertising reach a wider market at lower cost |
| Communication | email, video calls and shared systems speed up decisions and support remote working |
| Stock control | computerised reordering and barcoding reduce holding costs and stock-outs |
| Employment | demand shifts from unskilled to skilled work; retraining and redundancy costs arise |

Technology brings continuing costs: purchase, maintenance, training, and the risk of obsolescence or system failure.
