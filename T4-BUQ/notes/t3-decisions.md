# Term 3 decisions: answer chains and 12-mark models

The long-answer questions ask you to **make a decision** for an unseen business. Most of the decisions come from Term 3: finance, quality, location and external influences. For each decision below:

- **Knowledge** — the points and terms to use [k]
- **Use the case** — the facts to hunt for and quote [app]
- **Chain** — how to develop a point [an]: *so… which means… leading to…*
- **Evaluation** — what the decision depends on, and a default recommendation [ev]

Five full 12-mark answers are marked up with [k] [app] [an] [ev]. The businesses in them are invented for practice. How the levels work: [How marks are awarded](../SYLLABUS.md). Formulas: [CALCULATIONS.md](../CALCULATIONS.md).

**The 12-mark frame.** Discuss **every** option with an advantage and a drawback, each tied to a case fact and developed. Then: "Overall, X should choose…", the deciding case fact, why each other option loses **for this business**, and what the choice depends on.

## Finance

### 1. Choosing a source of finance

**Knowledge.** The criteria: **purpose** (a non-current asset needs long-term finance; running costs need short-term), **amount**, **how quickly** it is needed, **cost** (interest, fees, a share of profit), **control** (equity gives some away), **risk** (debt must be repaid; high **gearing** — more than about 60% debt — is risky), and the **legal form and size** (a sole trader cannot sell shares; a new firm has no retained profit and no track record). Sources: [finance notes](finance.md).

| Option | Best argument | Worst argument |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profit / owner's savings | no interest, no loss of control | may be too small; personal risk for a sole trader |
| Bank loan | full sum now, fixed repayments, keeps control | interest and repayments drain cash; collateral needed |
| Overdraft | quick, flexible, interest only on what is used | high interest; repayable on demand — never for a long-term asset |
| Taking a partner / selling shares | never repaid, no interest, brings skills | shares profit and control; disagreements |
| Leasing / hire purchase | spreads the cost of equipment | costs more in total |
| Selling assets | no interest, nothing to repay | a growing firm usually needs its assets |

**Use the case.** The legal form; the amount and what it is for; the profit (could retained profit cover it?); the closing cash balance; any existing loans; interest-rate news; whether the owner wants to keep control.

**Chain.** "A bank loan provides the full \$40 000 at once [k], so she can buy the e-bikes before summer [app], which means hire revenue starts immediately [an] — but the \$3200 a year interest and the monthly repayments come out of a bank balance of only \$6000 [app], so a quiet month could leave her short of cash [an]."

**Evaluation.** Match the **term** to the purpose. Compare the **cost**: interest against the profit given away to a partner or shareholders. Check the **amount**: can internal sources cover any of it? A combination is often best — some savings plus a smaller loan. It depends on the interest rate and its trend, how certain the extra revenue is, and how much the owner values control.

#### 12-mark model: Aroha's Bike Hire

*Case.* Aroha has run a bike-hire business on the Auckland waterfront as a sole trader for six years, with three part-time staff. She wants \$40 000 to buy 30 e-bikes before summer. Last year's profit was \$25 000, which she draws as her income. The bank balance is \$6000 and she has \$15 000 of personal savings. Interest rates are forecast to rise. Her options: a five-year bank loan at 8%; her friend Sam investing \$40 000 as a partner for half the profits; or her own savings.

*Question.* Consider the three sources of finance. Recommend which Aroha should use. Justify your answer. [12]

**Bank loan.** A bank loan raises the full amount at once [k], so Aroha can buy all 30 e-bikes before the summer season [app], which means they start earning hire revenue in the busiest months [an]. She keeps full control and all of the profit [k]. However, interest is an extra cost: at 8% a year's interest on \$40 000 is \$3200 [app], and if rates rise as forecast a variable-rate loan will cost more [an]. The monthly repayments also leave the business, and with only \$6000 in the bank [app] a quiet winter month could cause a cash-flow problem [an]. As a sole trader she has unlimited liability, so the bank may want her house as security [k].

**A partner.** Sam's \$40 000 is never repaid and carries no interest [k], so there are no monthly repayments to strain cash flow [an], and Sam could share the work of managing the three part-time staff [app]. But Aroha would give away half the profit: half of \$25 000 is \$12 500 a year [app], almost four times the loan's interest, every year [an]. She would also lose sole control, and partners can disagree about how the business is run [k].

**Owner's savings.** Her \$15 000 costs no interest and keeps full control [k], but it covers only 37.5% of the \$40 000 [app] — about 11 of the 30 e-bikes — so she would miss much of the summer demand [an]. It also puts her personal money at risk [k].

**Recommendation.** Overall, Aroha should take the bank loan [ev]. Interest of about \$3200 a year is far cheaper than handing \$12 500 a year to a partner, and unlike her savings it raises the whole sum in time for summer [ev]. A loan is long-term finance for long-term assets that will earn revenue for several years [an]. To reduce the risk she should put in some of her savings and borrow less — borrowing \$25 000 cuts the first year's interest to \$2000 [ev]. This depends on the bank agreeing to lend (six profitable years and a business plan will help) and on the rate rise being small; if summer demand were very uncertain, the partner's money would be safer because nothing has to be repaid [ev].

*Why it reaches the top level:* all three options are discussed in context with an advantage and a drawback; the decision uses case numbers, says why the other two lose, and states what it depends on.

### 2. Fixing a cash-flow problem

**Knowledge.** Find the **cause** first, then match the solution. Causes: seasonal sales, a large one-off payment, poor credit control (customers slow to pay), too much inventory, overtrading (growing faster than cash allows), rising costs. Solutions either **bring cash in sooner** or **pay it out later** — each has a cost ([finance notes](finance.md)).

| Solution | Cost of the solution |
|---|---|
| Overdraft | interest; repayable on demand |
| Delay paying suppliers (use trade credit) | lost discounts, strained supplier relations |
| Chase debtors, shorten credit terms, offer prompt-payment discounts | lower revenue; customers may go elsewhere |
| Reduce inventory | risk of stock-outs and lost sales |
| Lease instead of buying | higher total cost |
| Sell unused assets, or sale and leaseback | lose the asset or pay rent on it |
| Owner puts in more capital | personal risk |
| Cut costs or raise prices | quality or sales may fall |

**Use the case.** Which months have a negative closing balance and by how much; whether net cash flow is negative every month or only some; the cause visible in the forecast (receipts falling in winter, a one-off equipment payment); how long the gap lasts.

**Chain.** "The closing balance is negative only in July and August [app], because receipts fall in winter [an], so a short-term overdraft of about \$4000 would cover the gap and be repaid in September when receipts rise [an]. A long-term loan would mean paying interest for years to fix a two-month problem [ev]."

**Evaluation.** A **temporary** gap → short-term finance (overdraft, trade credit). A **persistent** negative net cash flow → a structural fix (cut costs, raise price, reduce stock, long-term finance). Remember that a forecast is an estimate, so test it against a worse case.

### 3. Judging performance from ratios

**Knowledge.** The school's ratio set ([formulas](../CALCULATIONS.md)):

| Ratio | Formula | What "good" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | higher than last year or than rivals; 40% = 40c of gross profit per \$1 of sales |
| Operating profit margin | operating profit ÷ revenue × 100 | higher is better; a big gap below GPM means high expenses |
| ROCE | operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | above the bank's interest rate; otherwise the money would earn more in the bank |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | 1:1 to 1.5:1 (school "ideal"); below 1:1 risks insolvency; too high means idle cash or stock |
| Acid-test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | 1:1 to 1.5:1; far below the current ratio means the firm relies on selling stock |

**Limitations.** A ratio means little alone — compare it **over time** or with **similar firms**. One year can be untypical (a seasonal business's balance sheet date matters). Ratios say nothing about staff, brand, product quality or the market.

**Use the case.** Calculate the ratios — the level descriptors reward calculating them, not just quoting the figures. Then quote each ratio, the change (from 40% to 35%), the interest rate on any loan, and what the business sells (inventory that spoils, or sells slowly).

**Chain.** "GPM fell from 40% to 35% [app], so cost of sales grew faster than revenue [an] — perhaps suppliers raised prices or the business discounted to win sales — which means less gross profit from each \$1 of sales to pay the expenses [an]."

**Evaluation.** Say which ratio matters most **for the decision asked**: a bank lending short-term looks at liquidity; an owner deciding to expand looks at ROCE against the interest rate and at whether margins are rising. Separate profitability (making profit) from liquidity (paying debts now) — a profitable firm can still run out of cash.

#### 12-mark model: Pōhutukawa Garden Centre Ltd

*Case.* A private limited company running one garden centre. The directors want to borrow \$200 000 at 9% a year to open a second centre.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 400 000 | 500 000 |
| Gross profit | 160 000 | 175 000 |
| Operating profit | 60 000 | 50 000 |
| Capital employed | 300 000 | 400 000 |
| Current assets | 90 000 | 100 000 |
| — of which inventory | 60 000 | 80 000 |
| Current liabilities | 60 000 | 80 000 |

*Question.* Using suitable ratios, recommend whether the directors should open the second garden centre now. Justify your answer. [12]

| Ratio | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| GPM | 160/400 × 100 = 40% | 175/500 × 100 = 35% |
| OPM | 60/400 × 100 = 15% | 50/500 × 100 = 10% |
| ROCE | 60/300 × 100 = 20% | 50/400 × 100 = 12.5% |
| Current ratio | 90/60 = 1.5:1 | 100/80 = 1.25:1 |
| Acid test | (90 − 60)/60 = 0.5:1 | (100 − 80)/80 = 0.25:1 |

**For expanding.** Revenue grew from \$400 000 to \$500 000, a 25% rise [app], which shows demand for its plants is growing [an], so a second centre could reach customers the first cannot. ROCE of 12.5% is still above the 9% interest on the loan [k][app], so in theory each borrowed dollar earns more than it costs [an].

**Against expanding — profitability.** GPM fell from 40% to 35% [app], so cost of sales rose faster than revenue [an], perhaps because suppliers raised prices or plants were discounted to sell. Expenses stayed at 25% of revenue in both years (\$100 000 of \$400 000, then \$125 000 of \$500 000) [app], so the whole fall in OPM from 15% to 10% came from the gross margin [an] — the problem is buying and pricing, not overheads. ROCE fell from 20% to 12.5% [app]: the extra \$100 000 of capital produced **less** operating profit, not more [an]. ROCE is now only 3.5 points above the loan rate, and if it falls again it will be below it [an].

**Against expanding — liquidity.** The current ratio fell from 1.5:1 to 1.25:1 [app], still inside the 1:1–1.5:1 range [k], but the acid test fell to 0.25:1 [app]: only 25 cents of liquid assets for every \$1 of short-term debt [an]. The business depends on selling its \$80 000 of plants, which can die or sell slowly [app], to pay its debts, and loan repayments on \$200 000 would put more pressure on its cash [an].

**Recommendation.** Overall, the directors should not open the second centre now [ev]. Sales growth is encouraging, but falling margins and a falling ROCE show the existing centre is becoming less profitable, and an acid test of 0.25 means it could struggle to meet the extra repayments [ev]. They should first restore the gross margin (cheaper suppliers, less discounting) and cut inventory, then expand once ROCE is clearly above the interest rate again [ev]. This judgement rests on only two years of figures and no comparison with other garden centres; the inventory may also be high because the balance sheet was drawn up before the spring rush, in which case the liquidity is less worrying than it looks [ev].

## Operations

### 4. Choosing a quality method

**Knowledge.** ([operations notes](operations.md))

| | Quality control | Quality assurance | TQM |
|---|---|---|---|
| When | at the end | at every stage | always, by everyone |
| Who | inspectors | workers check their own stage | every employee |
| Strength | little training; faulty goods stopped before the customer | faults found at source, less waste, lower cost | zero defects; motivates through responsibility; fits lean production |
| Weakness | waste already made; hard to find where the fault began | training cost; relies on workers following the rules | expensive; needs every employee's commitment |

**Use the case.** What is made (food or medicine needs guaranteed standards; a simple product less so); the cost of one fault (a whole batch wasted, a safety risk, a bad review); the staff (skilled and permanent, or casual and part-time); the brand (premium quality is its USP); the size and budget.

**Chain.** "Under quality control a fault is found only at the end [k], so a whole batch of 200 loaves could be thrown away [app], which raises the cost per loaf and the waste [an]. Quality assurance checks the dough at each stage [k], so a mistake in the mix is caught before baking [an]."

**Evaluation.** A small firm with a simple product → quality control is enough. Food, safety-critical goods or a premium brand → quality assurance. TQM suits a firm committed to lean production with permanent, trained staff — hard with high labour turnover or casual staff. Many firms combine assurance during production with a final check. Always weigh the **cost of improving quality** (training, time) against the **cost of poor quality** (waste, refunds, lost customers).

### 5. Choosing a location

**Knowledge.** ([operations notes](operations.md))

| Type of business | Factors that matter most |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing | cost and nearness of materials (bulky inputs), transport links and ports, a large affordable site, labour supply and skills, power, government grants, room to expand, planning controls |
| Retail | customers and foot traffic, parking, visibility, rent, nearness to complementary businesses and rivals, security |
| Service | nearness to customers (a café, hairdresser) **or**, if online, skilled staff and good internet; rent |

**Quantitative** factors: rent, wages, transport costs, set-up costs, likely sales. **Qualitative** factors: room to expand, safety, owner's preference, staff willing to move, environmental and ethical concerns. **Lowest rent is not lowest total cost.**

**Use the case.** Each site's rent, distance to suppliers and customers, unemployment rate (recruitment and wages), grants, room to expand; what the business sells and where its customers are; whether it imports or exports (distance to the port).

**Chain.** "Site B's rent is \$70 000 a year lower [app], which cuts fixed costs and so lowers the break-even output [an], but it is 200 km from the port [app], so every delivery of imported granules costs more and takes longer, which raises variable costs [an]."

**Evaluation.** Compare the **total** cost (rent + transport + wages) and the effect on **revenue** (being near customers). Say which factor matters most for **this** business and why. Mention planning permission and whether existing skilled staff will move.

#### 12-mark model: Kiwi Kayaks Ltd

*Case.* Kiwi Kayaks Ltd (KK), a private limited company, makes plastic kayaks in batches with 25 staff. It imports plastic granules through the Auckland port, sells 60% of its kayaks to retailers in Auckland and exports 40% to Australia by sea. Its factory is full.

| | Site A | Site B |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Auckland industrial estate, 5 km from the port | regional town 200 km from Auckland |
| Rent | \$120 000 a year | \$50 000 a year |
| Government grant | none | \$30 000 if 10 new jobs are created |
| Local unemployment | 4% | 8% |
| Transport | motorway; next to main customers | rail link to the port; extra transport costs about \$40 000 a year |
| Room to expand | no | yes |

*Question.* Consider the two sites. Recommend which KK should choose. Justify your answer. [12]

**Site A.** Being 5 km from the port [app] means imported granules arrive quickly and cheaply [k], so lead times are short and less buffer stock is needed [an]. It is also next to the Auckland retailers who buy 60% of its kayaks [app], so deliveries are fast, which helps keep those customers [an]. However, the rent is \$70 000 a year higher than Site B [app], which raises fixed costs and the break-even output [an]. With unemployment at only 4% [app], recruiting the extra workers will be harder and KK may have to pay higher wages [an]. And there is no room to expand [app], so if demand keeps growing KK faces another costly move [an].

**Site B.** The rent is \$70 000 a year lower and there is a one-off grant of \$30 000 [app], so fixed costs fall sharply [an]. Unemployment of 8% [app] means more people are looking for work, so recruiting the 10 new staff should be easier and wage pressure lower [an]. There is room to expand [app], which suits a business whose factory is already full [an]. However, it is 200 km from the port [app], so both the imported granules and the Australian exports travel further, adding about \$40 000 a year in transport [app] and lengthening lead times [an]. Some of the 25 skilled staff may not move [k], so KK could lose experience and have to pay for training [an]. The grant also has a condition — 10 new jobs — which ties KK's hands if sales fall [an].

**Recommendation.** Overall, KK should choose Site B [ev]. Even after the extra \$40 000 of transport, it saves about \$30 000 a year (\$70 000 − \$40 000) plus the \$30 000 grant, and it is the only site with room to grow [ev]. Site A's closeness to the port and customers is valuable, but it is worth less than the rent difference, and its 4% unemployment and lack of space would limit KK's growth [ev]. The decision depends on the transport estimate being accurate and on KK keeping its key skilled staff, for example by offering relocation help; if exports to Australia grew much faster than home sales, distance from the port would matter more and Site A would become more attractive [ev].

## External influences

### 6. Responding to an interest-rate rise

**Knowledge.** ([external notes](external.md))

- The **cost of borrowing** rises: repayments on overdrafts and variable-rate loans increase, so profit falls.
- **Investment** looks less attractive, so expansion may be postponed.
- **Consumer demand** falls, especially for goods bought on credit (cars, furniture, houses) and luxuries, because mortgage payments rise and saving is more attractive.
- The **currency appreciates** as foreign savers move money in: exports become dearer, imports cheaper.

$$\text{interest for a year} = \text{amount borrowed}\times\text{interest rate}$$

**Hit hardest:** high gearing, a large overdraft, products bought on credit or postponable, exporters. **Hardly affected:** no borrowing, essential products, importers (cheaper inputs).

**Use the case.** The size of loans and overdraft (calculate the extra interest); whether the product is a luxury, an essential or bought on credit; planned investment; whether it exports or imports.

**Chain.** "The rate rising from 6% to 8% adds \$4000 a year to the interest on the \$200 000 loan [app], which reduces profit [an]. Customers who buy \$3000 sofas on finance [app] face dearer credit, so they postpone purchases, which means sales fall at the same time as costs rise [an]."

**Responses:** repay or avoid borrowing, use retained profit, delay investment, lease instead of buying, cut costs, offer interest-free credit (which the firm pays for), promote cheaper ranges.

**Evaluation.** Depends on how much the firm borrows, whether its loans are fixed-rate, how sensitive its demand is to credit, and how long rates stay high.

#### 12-mark model: Hōmai Furniture Ltd

*Case.* Hōmai Furniture Ltd (HF) makes sofas and beds priced \$2000–\$5000 and sells them in its two showrooms; many customers pay with finance. HF has a \$50 000 overdraft at a variable rate and plans to borrow \$200 000 for a computer-controlled cutting machine. The Reserve Bank is expected to raise interest rates from 6% to 8%.

*Question.* Consider these responses to the rise in interest rates: delay buying the machine; lease the machine instead; offer customers interest-free credit. Recommend which HF should choose. Justify your answer. [12]

**The effect first.** At 6% the \$200 000 loan would cost \$12 000 a year in interest; at 8% it costs \$16 000 [app] — \$4000 more, cutting profit [an]. The overdraft costs another \$1000 a year (2% of \$50 000) [app]. Demand will also fall: sofas costing up to \$5000, often bought on finance [app], are easy to postpone when mortgage and loan payments rise [an].

**Delay the machine.** HF avoids \$16 000 a year of interest and keeps its cash [k][app], which matters while sales are likely to fall [an]. With fewer customers, extra capacity is not needed right now [an]. But HF misses the machine's lower unit costs and consistent quality [k], and if rivals invest it may fall behind when demand recovers [an].

**Lease the machine.** HF gets the machine without borrowing \$200 000 [app], and the leasing company often maintains it [k], so productivity rises without a large loan [an]. But lease charges can be high, and the leasing company also faces higher interest rates, so charges may rise [an]; HF never owns the machine [k].

**Interest-free credit.** This protects sales, because customers avoid dearer finance [an] on a \$3000 sofa [app]. But HF pays for the credit: it waits longer to be paid [k], so it must lean on its \$50 000 overdraft [app], now at a higher rate, worsening its cash flow just when costs are rising [an].

**Recommendation.** Overall, HF should delay buying the machine [ev]. Sales are likely to fall, so the extra capacity is not urgent, and avoiding a \$200 000 loan at 8% protects both profit and cash [ev]. Interest-free credit is the worst option here because it increases HF's own borrowing at the moment borrowing has become dearer [ev]. Leasing is a reasonable middle course if the machine is needed to cut costs urgently, but it still adds a fixed charge while demand falls [ev]. This depends on how long rates stay high and how far demand falls; if rates are expected to fall again within a year, HF can buy the machine then, ready for the recovery [ev].

### 7. Responding to an exchange-rate change

**Knowledge.** **SPICED** — a **S**trong currency: **I**mports **C**heap, **E**xports **D**ear ([external notes](external.md)).

| | Exporter | Importer (buys materials or goods abroad) |
|---|---|---|
| NZ dollar **appreciates** | dearer abroad, or a lower margin if it holds the foreign price — **hurt** | inputs cheaper in NZ dollars — **helped**; but foreign rivals are cheaper in NZ too |
| NZ dollar **depreciates** | cheaper abroad, or more NZ dollars per sale — **helped** | inputs dearer, costs rise — **hurt** |

The net effect depends on the **share of imported inputs** (an exporter using imported materials gains little from a weaker dollar), the **PED** for the product, and whether it can switch suppliers or markets.

$$\text{price abroad} = \text{NZ price}\times\text{rate} \qquad \text{NZ dollars received} = \frac{\text{foreign price}}{\text{rate}}$$

**Use the case.** Whether it exports, imports, or both; the share of sales abroad; the old and new rates (calculate the new price or cost); how many substitutes customers have; its unit cost.

**Chain.** "The NZ dollar rising from US\$0.60 to US\$0.75 lifts the US price of a \$100 blanket from US\$60 to US\$75 [app], a 25% rise; blankets have many substitutes, so demand is elastic [an], which means US sales could fall by more than 25% and export revenue would fall [an]."

**Responses:** change the price (only if demand is inelastic), absorb the change in the margin, switch suppliers (local for an importer), find other markets, cut costs, build a brand so customers pay more.

**Evaluation.** Depends on PED abroad, how long the change is expected to last, what rivals do, and how big the margin is.

#### 12-mark model: Southern Wool Ltd

*Case.* Southern Wool Ltd (SW) makes wool blankets in Dunedin from New Zealand wool; all its costs are in NZ dollars, about \$70 a blanket. It sells 70% of its blankets in New Zealand at \$100 and exports 30% to the United States. When NZ\$1 = US\$0.60 its US price was US\$60. The NZ dollar has risen to US\$0.75.

*Question.* Consider these responses: keep the NZ price, so the US price rises; keep the US price at US\$60; sell more in New Zealand instead. Recommend which SW should choose. Justify your answer. [12]

**Keep the NZ price.** The US price becomes 100 × 0.75 = US\$75 [app], so SW keeps its \$30 profit on every blanket [an]. But this is a 25% rise for US customers [app]; blankets have many substitutes, so demand is likely to be price-elastic [k], which means US sales could fall by more than 25% [an] and SW might lose a market that is 30% of its sales [app].

**Keep the US price at US\$60.** SW keeps its US customers and its market share [an]. But each blanket now earns only 60 ÷ 0.75 = NZ\$80 [app], so the profit per blanket falls from \$30 to \$10 (\$80 − \$70) [app], two-thirds lower [an]. It is still profitable, but there is little left to cover any further rise in the dollar or in costs [an].

**Sell more in New Zealand.** The NZ price of \$100 and margin of \$30 are unaffected [app]. But SW already sells 70% of its output at home [app], and a strong NZ dollar makes **imported** blankets cheaper here too [k], so competition at home is rising, not falling [an]. Winning extra NZ sales may need more marketing or a price cut [an].

**Recommendation.** Overall, SW should keep the US price at US\$60 for now [ev]. A \$10 profit is far better than losing much of a market that takes 30% of its sales, and once US customers switch to other brands they are hard to win back [ev]. Raising the US price by 25% is too risky with elastic demand, and the home market offers little extra because cheap imports are increasing competition [ev]. If the strong dollar lasts, SW could raise the US price a little — at US\$66 it would receive 66 ÷ 0.75 = NZ\$88 — while promoting the New Zealand wool brand to make demand less elastic [ev]. The choice depends on how long the NZ dollar stays high and how US rivals price [ev].

### 8. Responding to a recession and to government policy

**Knowledge.** In a **recession** GDP falls (usually two quarters in a row), so demand, sales and profits fall, especially for luxuries and postponable purchases; firms cut costs, cut stock and delay investment; unemployment rises. But recruiting is easier, wage pressure falls, premises may be cheaper, and discount sellers can gain. Draw the cycle with axes **real GDP** and **time** and label growth, boom, recession, slump, recovery ([external notes](external.md)).

Government policy, Business-style — **tool → the firm's costs or demand → its response**:

| Policy | Tool | Effect on a firm |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal** (government spending and taxation, including tariffs and quotas) | income tax, corporate tax, GST, spending, tariffs | income tax up → less disposable income → lower sales of non-essentials; corporate tax up → less profit to reinvest; GST up → higher prices; spending up → more demand and contracts; tariffs → protect firms competing with imports but raise costs for firms importing materials |
| **Monetary** | interest rates | see decision 6 |
| **Supply-side** | privatisation, training and education, promoting competition | more skilled workers; more competition |

In a recession the government is likely to cut interest rates and taxes and raise spending; in a boom it does the reverse.

**Use the case.** Is the product a luxury or an essential? Does the firm have cash reserves? Fixed costs it cannot cut quickly? Planned expansion? The specific policy in the news item.

**Chain.** "In a recession incomes fall [k], so customers cut back on non-essentials like a \$4 takeaway coffee [app], which means sales and revenue fall while the stall's fixed costs stay the same [an], squeezing profit."

**Responses:** cut costs, reduce stock, offer value ranges or promotions, delay expansion, focus on cash and survival; or, if cash-rich, invest while premises and staff are cheaper.

**Evaluation.** How badly a firm is hit depends on its product (luxury or essential), its costs and debts, and its cash; say whether the recession is expected to be short or long. Do not claim a policy helps every firm.

### 9. Entering a new market or going international

**Knowledge.** Reasons: a saturated home market, a larger customer base, spreading risk, lower costs, extending the product life cycle. Problems: language and culture, different tastes, different laws and taxes, exchange-rate risk, risk of non-payment, tariffs and quotas, distribution. Methods, from low to high risk: export through an agent → license or franchise → joint venture with a local firm → set up operations abroad (becoming a **multinational**). Simply exporting does not make a firm multinational ([marketing notes](marketing.md), [external notes](external.md)).

**Use the case.** The firm's size and finance; how different the new market is; whether market research has been done; existing contacts abroad; the exchange rate and any tariffs.

**Chain.** "A joint venture with a local partner [k] gives the firm the partner's knowledge of Australian retailers [app], so it avoids costly mistakes in distribution and promotion [an], but it must share the profit and control [k]."

**Evaluation.** A small firm should start with the lowest-risk method and expand once it has proved demand. A successful home marketing mix usually needs adapting, not copying.

### 10. Responding to inflation

**Knowledge.** Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Effects on a firm: material and energy costs rise; workers demand pay rises; customers' real incomes fall, so demand for non-essentials falls; exports become less competitive; pricing and planning become harder; the real value of existing debt falls (helping borrowers). The government may respond by raising interest rates — see decision 6.

**Use the case.** Which costs are rising and by how much; whether the product is essential or a luxury (PED); whether the firm can pass on price rises; its margin; wage rates of its staff.

**Chain.** "Coffee beans rising from \$80 to \$100 a month is a 25% rise in a variable cost [app], so the profit on each coffee falls [an]; raising prices protects the margin, but with many cafés nearby demand is price-elastic, so customers may switch [an]."

**Responses:** raise prices (if demand is inelastic or the brand is strong), absorb the rise in the margin, find cheaper suppliers or buy in bulk, cut waste and raise productivity, change the product or size.

**Evaluation.** Depends on PED, how strong the brand is, what rivals do (they face the same costs), and whether the inflation is expected to last.

## Using these in the exam

- The case will not name the decision type. Ask: *is this about money (1–3), how it is produced and where (4–5), or something outside its control (6–10)?*
- A news appendix (interest rates, the currency, inflation, a multinational, a recession) is there to be **used** — quote it in the 12-mark answer.
- Calculate whenever the data allows: extra interest, the new import cost, a ratio, a percentage change. A number is the strongest application.
