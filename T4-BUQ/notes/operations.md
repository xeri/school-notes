# Operations management

## Production and productivity

**Production** turns inputs into outputs. **Productivity** measures output per unit of input:

$$\text{labour productivity} = \frac{\text{total output in a period}}{\text{number of employees}}$$

Higher productivity lowers **unit labour cost** — provided pay and quality are unchanged. Ways to raise it: training, better equipment, improved motivation, better factory layout, and automation.

**Capital-intensive** production uses a high proportion of machinery: high fixed cost, low unit cost at volume, consistent quality, but inflexible and expensive to set up. **Labour-intensive** production uses a high proportion of people: lower set-up cost, flexible and able to customise, but higher unit cost and more variable quality.

## Methods of production

| Method | What it is | Suits | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Job** | one distinct product at a time to order | bespoke tailoring, wedding cakes, construction | fully customised, premium price, motivating skilled work | high unit cost, slow, skilled labour needed |
| **Batch** | groups of identical items, then a change of task | bakery, paint, clothing sizes | some variety, some economies of scale | downtime between batches, stocks of part-finished goods |
| **Flow** | continuous mass production along a line | cars, bottled drinks, newspapers | very low unit cost, consistent, automated | huge capital cost, inflexible, repetitive and demotivating work, a breakdown halts everything |

Customisation favours job production; stable high demand supports specialised flow equipment.

## Lean production and JIT

**Lean production** aims to cut every kind of waste — overproduction, waiting, transport, excess stock, defects, unnecessary motion and over-processing — using methods such as **kaizen** (continuous small improvements), **cell production** and better layout.

**Just-in-time (JIT)** stock control holds almost no inventory; materials arrive as they are needed.

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| less capital tied up in stock | a single late delivery halts production |
| lower storage and insurance cost | no buffer against a demand surge |
| less waste from obsolete or damaged stock | loses bulk-buying discounts |
| problems surface immediately | needs utterly reliable suppliers and good IT |

## Break-even analysis

Let $F$ be total fixed costs, $p$ the selling price per unit and $v$ the variable cost per unit.

$$\text{contribution per unit} = p - v$$
$$\text{break-even output} = \frac{F}{p-v}$$
$$\text{profit} = q(p-v) - F$$
$$\text{margin of safety} = \text{actual output} - \text{break-even output}$$

**Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance, salaries). **Variable costs** change directly with output (materials, piece-rate wages, power used in production). **Total cost** $= F + vq$.

**Worked example.** $F = \$4800$, $p = \$20$, $v = \$12$.

- Contribution $= 20 - 12 = \$8$ per unit
- Break-even $= 4800/8 = 600$ units
- At 750 units: margin of safety $= 150$ units
- Profit $= 750(8) - 4800 = \$1200$
- Check: revenue $750\times20 = \$15\,000$; total cost $4800 + 750(12) = \$13\,800$; profit $\$1200$ ✓

**Break-even chart.** Output on the horizontal axis, costs and revenue in dollars on the vertical. Plot fixed cost as a horizontal line, total cost rising from the fixed-cost intercept, and total revenue rising from the origin. Break-even is where total revenue crosses total cost; the vertical gap between them to the right is profit, to the left is loss.

**Limitations.** The model assumes constant unit price and variable cost, fixed costs unchanged across the whole range, and **everything produced is sold**. It ignores capacity limits and demand uncertainty, and bulk discounts make the lines non-linear in reality. If contribution is zero or negative, there is no viable positive break-even volume at all.

## Quality

**Quality control** inspects output against a standard and rejects defects at the end of the process. Simple, but the waste has already been created and workers take no responsibility for it.

**Quality assurance** designs the process so defects are prevented, with checks at each stage and each worker responsible for their own output. Requires training and a culture change, but cuts waste.

**Total quality management (TQM)** extends this to the whole organisation, treating every department as having internal customers, and aiming at zero defects through continuous improvement.

| Costs of poor quality | Costs of improving quality |
|---|---|
| scrap and rework | training |
| warranty claims and refunds | inspection and monitoring time |
| lost customers and bad reviews | better materials and equipment |
| damaged brand reputation | slower output during the change |

Reducing defects cuts rework and complaints — but say in your answer that the training and monitoring have a cost, then judge whether the saving exceeds it.

## Location

Choose premises from the firm's **actual needs**, not a general list.

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Proximity to the market | essential for retail and perishable goods |
| Proximity to raw materials | matters when inputs are bulky or heavy to transport |
| Availability and cost of labour | skilled staff may only be available in certain areas |
| Transport links | roads, port, airport for deliveries and distribution |
| Rent and local taxes | a major fixed cost |
| Infrastructure | power, water, reliable internet |
| Government grants | may subsidise locating in a development area |
| Room to expand | avoids a costly second move |
| Competitors nearby | can draw customers to the area, or split them |

**Lowest rent need not mean lowest total cost** — cheap premises far from customers add transport cost and lose sales.

*Contrast.* A bakery delivering fresh products needs quick access to its customers and so pays for a central location. An online service values skilled staff and reliable communications far above passing foot traffic, and can locate cheaply.

**Locating in another country** can give lower labour costs, access to a new market, and avoidance of tariffs — against the risks of language and cultural differences, quality control at a distance, longer supply chains, exchange-rate movements and reputational concerns about working conditions.
