# Geography — Pre-Q Syllabus

**Final exam (Term 4):** one **three-hour examination, 100 marks**, which is 100% of the Pre-Q mark. This is the 2025 specification; the school's 2026 course pages direct students to the 2025 specification file, so it is the working reference.

- **75% core questions** — one question from each of the three themes; short answer, some resource-based.
- **25% paragraph/essay** — one question from each of the three themes; some essays may offer options to choose between.
- **Geographical skills are included in the core**, not set as a separate section. A topographic map is **likely to be included**, not guaranteed.
- Questions may be subdivided into parts.

Earlier school exams follow the same pattern on a smaller scale: Term 1 is a one-hour paper of about 40 marks with one 10-mark essay, and Term 2 a two-hour paper of about 80 marks with two 10-mark essays. Term 1, Term 2 and Term 4 results together are used for Form 6 placement.

**Question families:** definitions; resource description and comparison; process explanation; calculations and data interpretation; labelled and annotated diagrams; named examples; evaluated paragraphs and essays.

Resource-based questions may use photographs, map extracts, satellite images, drawings, diagrams, graphs, text extracts, statistics and tables of data. Resources may come from anywhere in the world and no regional knowledge is required unless the syllabus names it. Exam questions are very unlikely to be about a single case study, but you will be asked to refer to named examples, and essays expect examples to be used to evaluate.

**Equipment (2025 specification):** pencil, rubber, ruler, protractor and calculator, plus access to a sheet of plain paper for measuring distance and for cross-sections. Large-scale maps are 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 and always carry a full key; answers to the map question must use map evidence only.

**Scope notes.**
- *Weather instruments.* The school's weather and climate topic page marks two objectives as not required — *describe how weather data is collected* and *make calculations using information from weather instruments* — while the 2025 specification still lists the instruments and the Stevenson screen, and a 2024 Term 1 paper asked how humidity data is collected. The instruments, the Stevenson screen and reading a maximum–minimum thermometer are therefore kept, briefly.
- *Wind erosion.* Headed "not to be assessed" in the 2025 specification, but still listed on the school's weathering and erosion page (which names deflation, mesas, dunes and loess). It is covered in a short paragraph only.
- *Coral reefs.* The school's coasts topic lists coral reefs as "not examined". They are not covered.
- *Form 4 material.* The specification calls Pre-Q a two-year course begun in Form 4, and the rivers work was partly taught then. It is examinable and is kept in full.
- *Extra school requirements.* The school's topic pages add a hot-desert case study, a food-shortage case study and the Rostow, Frank and Clark–Fisher models, which the 2025 specification does not list. They are covered.

## 1. Population and settlement (specification Theme 2)

**2.1 Global population change**
- Describe and explain global population growth over the last 100 000 years, especially the growth of the last 250 years.

**2.2 Demographic processes**
- Define birth rate and death rate as the number per 1000 people per year, and show how each, and net migration, is calculated.
- Calculate natural increase, and show how birth rate, death rate and migration together change a country's population.
- Explain how social, economic and other factors — including government policy and disease such as HIV/AIDS — affect birth and death rates.
- Label, interpret and apply the Demographic Transition Model, and name countries at different stages.
- Describe and explain contrasting global patterns of natural population change.

**2.3 Age/sex pyramids**
- Construct and interpret an age/sex pyramid.
- Link pyramid shape to the stages of the Demographic Transition Model.
- Identify types of population structure, give reasons for them, and explain the implications of youthful and ageing populations.

**2.4 Population issues and policies**
- Describe and evaluate population policies aimed at high rates of natural increase and at ageing populations.
- *Named examples:* a country with a high rate of natural increase (youthful population) and its attempts to deal with the issues; a country with low or negative natural increase (ageing population) and its attempts to deal with the issues.

**2.5 Reasons for migration**
- Explain and give reasons for international migration, distinguishing voluntary, forced and economic movement.
- Apply Lee's push–pull model, including push factors, pull factors and intervening obstacles.

**2.6 Impacts of migration**
- Describe and evaluate the impacts of migration on both source and destination areas.
- *Named examples:* an international migration flow to or from New Zealand; an international refugee flow, with its impacts on source and destination.

**2.7 Population density and distribution**
- Using named regions, describe and explain the factors influencing population density and distribution.
- Name and locate on a map an area of high density and an area of low density, and give reasons for each.

**2.8 Patterns of settlement**
- Describe and explain the general patterns of settlement: dispersed, clustered, nucleated, linear, rural and urban.
- Explain the factors influencing the site, growth and function of settlements, and why some settlements grow more than others.

**2.9 Urbanisation**
- Describe the pattern of, and reasons for, global urbanisation since 1700, both over time and across space.
- Identify the nature of, and reasons for, urbanisation in HICs, including suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation and the rural–urban fringe as taught in class.

**2.10 Service provision**
- Describe and explain the role of urban areas in providing services: high-, middle- and low-order services, sphere of influence, range and threshold population.
- Give reasons for the hierarchy of settlements and services.
- *Named example:* New Zealand's urban hierarchy and the services at each level.

**2.11 Urban land use (HIC)**
- Describe the land uses found in urban areas (commercial, industrial, residential, transport) and the patterns shown by the Burgess and Hoyt models.
- Explain why each land use is found where it is, including bid-rent theory and the core–frame model of the CBD.
- Give reasons for changes in urban land use, including inner-city decay, redevelopment, gentrification, suburban growth and the rural–urban fringe.
- Explain the problems of urban areas — urban sprawl, infrastructure provision, traffic congestion, pollution, housing — and evaluate possible solutions.
- Understand the methods used to carry out urban fieldwork.
- *Named examples:* an inner-city problem area in an HIC and the attempts to deal with it; the Auckland urban area, its infrastructure problems and the attempts to deal with them.

**2.12 Urban growth (LIC/MIC)**
- Describe and explain rural-to-urban migration in LICs, including the spatial pattern and the push and pull factors of a specific named flow.
- Describe and evaluate the impacts of that migration on both the urban area and the rural communities left behind.
- Describe land-use patterns in LIC and MIC cities using the Latin American city model, including the relative location of squatter settlements and high-income housing.
- Describe the characteristics and issues of squatter settlements.
- Examine and evaluate strategies to reduce the impacts of rapid urban growth, including rural strategies that slow the migration flow.
- *Named example:* an urban area in an LIC or MIC — reasons for its rapid growth, the problems and successes of that growth, and the strategies used.

## 2. The natural environment (specification Theme 1)

**1.1 The geomorphic cycle**
- Show an understanding that the landscape is both created and destroyed over time and that eroded material is recycled.
- Explain its components: land-building tectonic processes (folding, faulting, volcanism), land-modifying erosion processes (rivers, glaciers, wind, sea), and recycling into sedimentary and metamorphic rock.

**1.2 Tectonic processes — volcanoes and earthquakes**
- Describe the structure of the Earth and the nature of crust and mantle material, and explain plate tectonics including the generation of mantle convection currents.
- Describe the global pattern of plates, plate types, and the boundary types: constructive (diverging), destructive (converging) and conservative.
- Explain folding, faulting and volcanism, and the formation of fold mountains.
- Describe and explain the distribution of fold mountains, earthquakes and volcanoes.
- Distinguish basaltic, andesitic and rhyolitic activity and the landforms produced — shield volcanoes, composite cones, lava domes.
- Describe the features of a volcano (magma chamber, vent, crater) and of an earthquake (focus, epicentre, intensity), and how earthquake magnitude is measured.
- Describe the causes and effects of earthquakes and eruptions on people and the environment, and explain and evaluate how the impacts can be reduced.
- *Named examples:* an earthquake and a volcanic eruption that affected people and the environment — at least one from New Zealand.

**1.3 Weathering and erosion**
- Show an understanding of the four stages of denudation — weathering, erosion, transportation, deposition — and the agents involved: water, ice and wind.
- Explain the physical and chemical weathering processes — exfoliation, pressure release, freeze–thaw, biological, carbonation and hydrolysis — and their role in supplying material for erosion.
- Describe and explain the associated landforms: exfoliation domes, scree slopes, limestone features (pavement, sink holes, caves) and granite tors.
- Explain the formation of a glacier and the work of glaciers in eroding, transporting and depositing.
- Describe and explain glacial landforms: cirques, U-shaped valleys, hanging valleys, finger lakes, fiords, medial and terminal moraine, by plucking and abrasion.
- *Not to be assessed (2025 specification):* the work of wind and two landforms — dunes and mushroom rocks — by abrasion and surface deflation.
- Describe the hydrologic cycle and the position of a drainage basin within it, and explain the main hydrological characteristics and processes of rivers and drainage basins.
- Explain the river erosion processes: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, corrosion (solution) and cavitation.
- Explain river transport and deposition using the Hjulström curve, and the downstream changes described by the Bradshaw and Schumm models.
- Describe and explain river landforms and classify each as erosional or depositional: V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, rapids, meanders, ox-bow lakes, flood plains and deltas.
- Describe and explain the components of a flood hydrograph and why hydrographs vary between drainage basins.
- Explain why rivers flood, and the benefits and risks that rivers present to people.
- Explain and evaluate how river flooding can be managed, using case-study material.
- Understand the methods used to carry out river fieldwork.
- *Named example:* the opportunities presented by a river or rivers, the associated hazards and their management.

**1.4 Weather and climate**
- Describe the elements that make up weather: temperature, humidity, wind, sunshine and precipitation.
- Distinguish weather from climate.
- Identify the instruments used to measure weather — rain gauge, maximum–minimum thermometer, wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (hygrometer), sunshine recorder, barometer, anemometer, wind vane — and the characteristics, siting and use of a Stevenson screen.
- Use and interpret graphs and diagrams showing weather and climate data; construct, interpret and compare climate graphs.
- Explain the processes of evaporation, condensation, cloud formation, precipitation — free convection, forced convection and frontal — dew and frost.
- Explain how uneven heating of the Earth's surface produces areas of high and low pressure and generates wind.
- Describe the global atmospheric circulation pattern at a general level using the tri-cellular model, and relate it to regional climates.
- Show a basic knowledge of synoptic weather charts: isobars, high and low pressure, wind direction and relative strength, and fronts.
- Explain why climate varies between locations globally and regionally.
- Describe and explain New Zealand's general rainfall, sunshine and temperature patterns.
- *Named example:* New Zealand's climate patterns.

**1.5 Ecosystems**
- Define an ecosystem and explain how it results from interactions between living and non-living components.
- Describe and explain the location of tropical rainforests and hot deserts.
- Describe and explain the climate characteristics and seasonal variation of each — rainfall, temperature, humidity, sunshine — and recognise, describe and compare them on climate graphs.
- Describe and explain the interactions of vegetation, soil, wildlife and climate in a named tropical rainforest.
- Describe and evaluate the causes and effects of deforestation on tropical rainforest, for the environment and people, locally and globally.
- *Named examples:* an area of tropical rainforest; an area of hot desert (school requirement).

**1.6 Coasts**
- Explain the work of the sea and wind in eroding, transporting and depositing sediment: abrasion, hydraulic action, corrosion, wave refraction, sea-level change, longshore drift, saltation and sand trapping.
- Describe and explain coastal landforms: headland-and-bay coastlines, cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps, beaches, spits, tombolos and coastal sand dunes.
- Explain the opportunities and hazards that coasts present to people.
- Explain and evaluate how people manage coastal hazards such as erosion and storm-surge flooding.
- Link sediment deposited at the coast and in the sea back to the geomorphic cycle.
- Understand the methods used to carry out coastal fieldwork.
- *Named example:* the opportunities presented by an area of coastline, the associated hazards and their management.

## 3. Economic development and resources (specification Theme 3)

**3.1 Development**
- Define development and distinguish economic, social, political and environmental development.
- Explain the indicators used to measure it — economic, social and composite measures such as HDI and HPI — and evaluate the limitations of each.
- Describe global inequalities in development, including the Brandt Line, and explain them using the SEEP factors and the Rostow and Frank models taught in class.
- Describe the characteristics of regional inequality within a country, and explain how and why it develops.
- *Named examples:* inequalities between two named countries (one HIC, one LIC); regional inequalities within New Zealand.

**3.2 Sectors of production**
- Define the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors and give examples of each.
- Describe and explain how the relative importance of the sectors changes as a country develops (economic transition and the Clark–Fisher model).
- Explain the process of globalisation and evaluate its impacts.
- *Named example:* a transnational corporation and its global links.

**3.3 Primary industry — farming and food shortage**
- Describe farming types: commercial, subsistence, arable, pastoral, mixed, horticulture, intensive and extensive.
- Explain the physical and human factors affecting farm location.
- Describe farming as a system of inputs, processes, outputs and feedback.
- Describe the negative environmental impacts of farming: water extraction, pollution, soil erosion, desertification and enhanced global warming.
- Discuss and evaluate methods of managing the farmed environment sustainably, including the long-term sustainability of dairy farming.
- Explain the natural, economic and political causes of food shortages, their effects on people, and possible solutions (school requirement).
- *Named examples:* the operation of a dairy (cattle) farm in New Zealand; a country or region suffering from food shortages (school requirement).

**3.4 Secondary industry — manufacturing**
- Explain the importance of resources in the development of secondary industry.
- Describe and explain the factors influencing the location and distribution of factories and industrial zones: land, labour, raw materials, fuel and power, transport, markets and political factors.
- Describe manufacturing as a system of inputs, processes, outputs (products and waste) and feedback.
- Describe how manufacturing threatens the environment locally and globally, and evaluate its sustainability.
- *Named example:* a manufacturing factory or industrial zone.

**3.5 Tertiary industry — tourism**
- Describe and explain the growth of tourism and the factors behind it: rising affluence, leisure time, transport, political events and ageing populations.
- Describe the natural and cultural attractions on which tourism depends, and the infrastructure it requires.
- Evaluate the benefits and disadvantages of tourism for a receiving area.
- Explain the environmental risks of tourism and how it can be managed sustainably.
- *Named example:* an area where tourism is important.

**3.6 Energy resources**
- Define renewable and non-renewable.
- Describe the renewable sources (geothermal, wind, hydroelectric, solar, tidal, wave, biofuel) and the non-renewable sources (coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear).
- Evaluate the benefits, disadvantages and environmental impacts of each source.
- Describe the relative importance of different energy sources globally and in countries at different levels of development.
- *Named example:* energy supply in a country or area.

**3.7 Global warming**
- Define global warming and the enhanced greenhouse effect.
- Describe anthropogenic pollution, how it is created and how it degrades the atmosphere.
- Describe the history of greenhouse-gas use and its link to global temperature.
- Name the greenhouse gases, explain where they come from, and explain the carbon cycle.
- Describe the impacts on climate and weather patterns, including effects on the hydrological cycle.
- Describe and evaluate solutions, including reduced reliance on greenhouse gases in farming, industry and homes.
- *Named example:* an area or ecosystem at heightened risk from global warming, through sea-level rise or smog.

## 4. Geographical skills (assessed inside the core questions)

Skills follow the CAIE IGCSE skills section and are tested inside the theme questions. The skills the specification attaches to each theme are: annotated diagrams, flood hydrographs, weather instruments, climate graphs and synoptic charts (natural environment); line and pie graphs, age/sex pyramids, the push–pull, demographic transition and urban land-use models, and fieldwork methodology (population and settlement); line and pie graphs, mapping, photographs, statistics and tables of data (development and resources).

- Read a 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 topographic map: four- and six-figure grid references, scale, measurement of straight and curved distances, 16-point compass directions and bearings from grid north.
- Interpret relief from contours and spot heights; calculate height differences; construct, complete and interpret cross-sections.
- Use the key to identify features; translate map scale into real size and shape; describe relief, drainage, coastlines, settlement pattern, communications and land use from map evidence; give reasons for the site and growth of a settlement; draw sketch maps of settlement patterns.
- Describe human and physical landscapes from oblique photographs, aerial photographs, satellite images, GIS output, field sketches and cartoons.
- Construct, read and compare graphs, maps and diagrams: line, bar, divided bar, histogram, pictogram, pie, scatter, triangular, radial, dispersion, kite, wind rose, flow diagram, isoline and choropleth, plus climate graphs, population pyramids and synoptic charts; plot data when axes and scales are provided.
- Extract and describe trends and patterns from tables of data and written extracts, and suggest an appropriate form of graphical representation.
- Carry out the calculations used with these resources: density, percentage and percentage change, mean, median, mode, range, rates per 1000, natural increase, dependency ratio, gradient, area and scale conversion.
- Apply fieldwork methodology to river, coastal and urban enquiries: hypothesis, site selection, sampling, data collection, presentation, analysis, conclusion and evaluation.
- Answer to the command word, use named examples, and structure paragraphs and essays to the level descriptors.
