# Geography — Pre-Q Syllabus

The course is Pre-Q Geography at Auckland Grammar School. It is based almost entirely on **Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)**, and the school's own 2025 specification sets out the objectives below.

## Term 4 examination, 2026 format

The paper is three hours long and has four sections.

| Section | Content | Marks | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Theme 1: Natural Environment | 50 | 1 hour |
| **B** | Theme 2: Population and Settlement | 25 | 30 minutes |
| **C** | Theme 3: Development and the use of resources | 25 | 30 minutes |
| **D** | Essays: **one from each theme**, so three essays in total | 30 | 1 hour |

- The marks for each question are shown at the end of the question.
- **Maps and diagrams** drawn in written answers can earn marks.
- The use of **examples** to support written answers can earn marks.
- Geographical skills (maps, graphs, calculations, fieldwork) are tested inside Sections A–C. A topographic map is likely.

**Case studies confirmed for 2026 teaching:**
- slums and squatter settlements: **Dharavi, Mumbai (India)** and **Diepsloot, Johannesburg (South Africa)**;
- manufacturing: the **Volkswagen factory, Wolfsburg (Germany)**;
- tertiary industry and tourism: **Vietnam**.

How to answer, timing and the essay level descriptors are on the Exam technique page, the last page in the skills group below.

### Earlier format (2025 specification, kept for older papers)

- One three-hour examination of 100 marks, which is 100% of the Pre-Q mark.
- **75% core questions:** one question from each of the three themes; short answer, some resource-based.
- **25% paragraph/essay:** one question from each theme; some essays may offer options.
- Skills are included in the core, not set as a separate section. A topographic map is likely, not guaranteed.

Earlier school exams follow the same pattern on a smaller scale:
- Term 1: a one-hour paper of about 40 marks with one 10-mark essay.
- Term 2: a two-hour paper of about 80 marks with two 10-mark essays.

Term 1, Term 2 and Term 4 results together are used for Form 6 placement.

**Question families:** definitions; resource description and comparison; process explanation; calculations and data interpretation; labelled and annotated diagrams; named examples; evaluated paragraphs and essays.

**Resources** may be photographs, map extracts, satellite images, drawings, diagrams, graphs, text extracts, statistics and tables of data. They may come from anywhere in the world, and no regional knowledge is required unless the syllabus names it. Exam questions are very unlikely to be about a single case study, but you will be asked to refer to named examples, and essays expect examples to be used to evaluate.

**Equipment:** pencil, rubber, ruler, protractor and calculator, plus a sheet of plain paper for measuring distance and for cross-sections. Large-scale maps are 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 and always carry a full key. Answers to the map question must use map evidence only.

**Scope notes.**
- *Weather instruments.* The school's weather and climate topic page marks two objectives as not required: *describe how weather data is collected* and *make calculations using information from weather instruments*. The 2025 specification still lists the instruments and the Stevenson screen, and a 2024 Term 1 paper asked how humidity data is collected, so the instruments are kept briefly.
- *Wind erosion.* Headed "not to be assessed" in the 2025 specification, but still on the school's weathering topic page (deflation, mesas, dunes, loess). It has a short section on the weathering page.
- *Mass movement.* Not named in the 2025 specification, but needed for denudation, scree and hazard essays; covered briefly on the weathering page.
- *Coral reefs.* The school's coasts topic lists coral reefs as "not examined". They are not covered as a landform; coral bleaching and reef collapse appear only as climate-change impacts (Kiribati).
- *Water supply.* Not a separate objective in the school specification, but the school's Theme 3 summary lists resources and their use and the environmental impact of development, so it is covered with IGCSE 0460's water topic.
- *Form 4 material.* The specification calls Pre-Q a two-year course begun in Form 4, and the rivers work was partly taught then. It is examinable and is kept in full.
- *Extra school requirements.* The school's topic pages add a hot-desert case study, a food-shortage case study and the Rostow, Frank and Clark–Fisher models, which the 2025 specification does not list. They are covered.
- *2026 case studies.* Manufacturing is VW Wolfsburg and tourism is Vietnam; New Zealand Steel (Glenbrook) and Queenstown are kept as supporting New Zealand examples. The school's global-warming slides use Kiribati as the area at risk, with Tuvalu and Delhi as supporting examples. The slums are Dharavi and Diepsloot, with Rio de Janeiro as supporting material.

## Theme 1: Natural Environment
**1.1 The geomorphic cycle** — [The geomorphic cycle and plate tectonics](notes/tectonics.md)
- Show an understanding that the landscape is both created and destroyed over time and that eroded material is recycled.
- Explain its components: land-building tectonic processes (folding, faulting, volcanism), land-modifying erosion processes (rivers, glaciers, wind, sea) and recycling into sedimentary and metamorphic rock.

**1.2 Tectonic processes — volcanoes and earthquakes** — [The geomorphic cycle and plate tectonics](notes/tectonics.md)
- Describe the structure of the Earth and the nature of crust and mantle material, and explain plate movement by mantle convection currents.
- Describe the global pattern of plates, plate types and the boundary types: constructive (diverging), destructive (converging) and conservative.
- Explain folding, faulting and volcanism, and the formation of fold mountains.
- Describe and explain the distribution of fold mountains, earthquakes and volcanoes.
- Distinguish basaltic, andesitic and rhyolitic activity and the landforms produced — shield volcanoes, composite cones, lava domes.
- Describe the features of a volcano (magma chamber, vent, crater) and of an earthquake (focus, epicentre, intensity), and how earthquake magnitude is measured.
- Describe the causes and effects of earthquakes and eruptions on people and the environment, and explain and evaluate how the impacts can be reduced.
- *Named examples:* the Christchurch earthquake (22 February 2011) and the Whakaari / White Island eruption (9 December 2019) — both New Zealand.

**1.3 Weathering and erosion**
- [Weathering, mass movement and wind](notes/weathering.md): the four stages of denudation and the agents involved; physical and chemical weathering — exfoliation, pressure release, freeze–thaw, biological, carbonation and hydrolysis — and their role in supplying material for erosion; the landforms: exfoliation domes, scree slopes, limestone features (pavement, sink holes, caves) and granite tors; mass movement; *not to be assessed (2025 specification):* the work of wind and its landforms.
- [Glaciation](notes/glaciation.md): the formation of a glacier; the work of glaciers in eroding (plucking, abrasion), transporting and depositing; landforms — cirques, U-shaped valleys, hanging valleys, finger lakes, fiords, medial and terminal moraine.
- [Rivers: drainage basins, processes and landforms](notes/rivers.md): the hydrologic cycle and the drainage basin within it; hydrological characteristics and processes of rivers and drainage basins; erosion (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, corrosion, cavitation), transport and deposition, the Hjulström curve, and the Bradshaw and Schumm models; river landforms classified as erosional or depositional — V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, rapids, meanders, ox-bow lakes, flood plains and deltas.
- [Rivers: flooding, management and fieldwork](notes/river-flooding.md): the components of a flood hydrograph and why hydrographs vary between basins; why rivers flood; the benefits and risks rivers present; how flooding can be managed, evaluated with case-study material; the methods of river fieldwork.
- *Named example:* the opportunities presented by a river, its hazards and their management — the Waikato River, with Cyclone Gabrielle (2023) as the flood event and Bangladesh as the LIC contrast.

**1.4 Weather and climate**
- [Weather](notes/weather.md): the elements of weather; weather vs climate; weather instruments and the Stevenson screen; evaporation, condensation, cloud formation, precipitation (free convection, forced convection, frontal), dew and frost; how differences in heating produce high and low pressure and wind; synoptic charts — isobars, highs and lows, wind direction and strength, fronts.
- [Climate and global circulation](notes/climate.md): global atmospheric circulation (the tri-cellular model) and its link to regional climates; why climate varies between locations globally and regionally; constructing, interpreting and comparing climate graphs; New Zealand's rainfall, sunshine and temperature patterns.
- *Named example:* New Zealand's climate patterns.

**1.5 Ecosystems** — [Ecosystems: tropical rainforest and hot desert](notes/ecosystems.md)
- Define an ecosystem and explain how it results from interactions between living and non-living components.
- Describe and explain the location of tropical rainforests and hot deserts, and their climate characteristics on climate graphs.
- Describe and explain the interactions of vegetation, soil, wildlife and climate in a named tropical rainforest.
- Describe and evaluate the causes and effects of deforestation on tropical rainforest, for the environment and people, locally and globally.
- *Named examples:* the Amazon (tropical rainforest); the Sahara and the Sahel (hot desert — school requirement).

**1.6 Coasts** — [Coasts](notes/coasts.md)
- Explain the work of the sea and wind in eroding, transporting and depositing sediment (abrasion, hydraulic action, corrosion, wave refraction, sea-level change, longshore drift, saltation, sand trapping).
- Describe and explain coastal landforms: headland-and-bay coastlines, cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps, beaches, spits, tombolos and coastal sand dunes.
- Explain the opportunities and hazards coasts present, and explain and evaluate how coastal hazards are managed.
- Link sediment deposited at the coast and in the sea back to the geomorphic cycle; understand the methods of coastal fieldwork.
- *Named example:* Muriwai and Mangawhai Heads (with Mount Maunganui as a tombolo).

## Theme 2: Population and Settlement

**2.1 Global population change** ([Population growth and change](notes/population-growth.md))
- Describe and explain global population growth over the last 100 000 years, especially the growth of the last 250 years.

**2.2 Demographic processes** ([Population growth and change](notes/population-growth.md))
- Define birth rate and death rate as the number per 1000 people per year, and show how each, and net migration, is calculated.
- Calculate natural increase, and show how birth rate, death rate and migration together change a country's population.
- Explain how social, economic and other factors, including government policy and disease such as HIV/AIDS, affect birth and death rates.
- Label, interpret and apply the Demographic Transition Model, and name countries at different stages.
- Describe and explain contrasting global patterns of natural population change.
- Over-, under- and optimum population: the population–resource balance.

**2.3 Age/sex pyramids** ([Population structure and policies](notes/population-structure.md))
- Construct and interpret an age/sex pyramid.
- Link pyramid shape to the stages of the Demographic Transition Model.
- Identify types of population structure, give reasons for them, and explain the implications of youthful and ageing populations, including the dependency ratio.

**2.4 Population issues and policies** ([Population structure and policies](notes/population-structure.md))
- Describe and evaluate population policies aimed at high rates of natural increase and at ageing populations.
- *Named examples:* a country with a high rate of natural increase (Niger, with Bangladesh and China's one-child policy); a country with an ageing population (Japan, with Italy and Germany).

**2.5 Reasons for migration** ([Migration](notes/migration.md))
- Explain and give reasons for international migration, distinguishing voluntary, forced and economic movement.
- Apply Lee's push–pull model, including push factors, pull factors and intervening obstacles.

**2.6 Impacts of migration** ([Migration](notes/migration.md))
- Describe and evaluate the impacts of migration on both source and destination areas.
- *Named examples:* an international flow to or from New Zealand (Samoa to New Zealand); an international refugee flow (Syria).

**2.7 Population density and distribution** ([Population density and distribution](notes/population-density.md))
- Using named regions, describe and explain the physical, economic, social and political factors influencing population density and distribution.
- Name and locate on a map an area of high density (Bangladesh, Auckland) and an area of low density (the Sahara, New Zealand's West Coast), and give reasons for each.

**2.8 Patterns of settlement** ([Settlement and service provision](notes/settlement-services.md))
- Describe and explain the general patterns of settlement: dispersed, clustered, nucleated, linear, rural and urban.
- Explain the factors influencing the site, situation, growth and function of settlements, and why some settlements grow more than others.

**2.9 Urbanisation** ([Urban change and issues in HICs](notes/urban-change-hics.md))
- Describe the pattern of, and reasons for, global urbanisation since 1700, both over time and across space.
- Identify the nature of, and reasons for, urbanisation in HICs: suburbanisation, urban sprawl, the rural–urban fringe and urban shadow, counter-urbanisation and re-urbanisation.

**2.10 Service provision** ([Settlement and service provision](notes/settlement-services.md))
- Describe and explain the role of urban areas in providing services: high-, middle- and low-order services, sphere of influence, range and threshold population.
- Give reasons for the hierarchy of settlements and services, including the modern shopping hierarchy and hub-and-spoke networks.
- *Named example:* New Zealand's urban hierarchy and the services at each level.

**2.11 Urban land use (HIC)** ([Urban land use and models](notes/urban-land-use.md), [Urban change and issues in HICs](notes/urban-change-hics.md))
- Describe the land uses found in urban areas (commercial, industrial, residential, transport) and the patterns shown by the Burgess, Hoyt and Harris–Ullman models.
- Explain why each land use is found where it is: bid-rent theory, the PLVI and the core–frame model of the CBD.
- Give reasons for changes in urban land use: invasion and succession, filtering, inner-city decay, redevelopment, regeneration, gentrification, suburban growth, intensification and the rural–urban fringe.
- Explain the problems of urban areas (urban sprawl, infrastructure provision, traffic congestion, pollution, housing) and evaluate possible solutions.
- Understand the methods used to carry out urban fieldwork ([Graphs, statistics and fieldwork](notes/data-fieldwork.md)).
- *Named examples:* an inner-city problem area in an HIC (London Docklands); the Auckland urban area, its growth, its infrastructure problems and the attempts to deal with them.

**2.12 Urban growth (LIC/MIC)** ([Urbanisation in LICs and MICs](notes/urbanisation-lics.md))
- Describe and explain rural-to-urban migration in LICs, including the spatial pattern and the push and pull factors of a specific named flow.
- Describe and evaluate the impacts of that migration on both the urban area and the rural communities left behind.
- Describe land-use patterns in LIC and MIC cities using the Latin American city model, including the relative location of squatter settlements and high-income housing.
- Describe the characteristics and issues of squatter settlements and the informal economy.
- Examine and evaluate strategies to reduce the impacts of rapid urban growth, including rural strategies that slow the migration flow.
- *Named examples:* **Dharavi, Mumbai** and **Diepsloot, Johannesburg** (the school's 2026 slums), with Rio de Janeiro's favelas as supporting material.

## Theme 3: Development and the use of resources

**3.1 Development** — [Development and inequality](notes/development.md)
- Define development and distinguish economic, social, political and environmental development.
- Explain the indicators used to measure it — economic, social and composite measures such as HDI and HPI — and evaluate their limitations.
- Describe global inequalities in development (the development gap, the Brandt Line) and explain them using SEEP and the Rostow and Frank models taught in class.
- Describe the characteristics of regional inequality within a country, and explain how and why it develops.
- *Named examples:* New Zealand (HIC) and Niger (LIC), with Mali from the class data; regional inequalities within New Zealand.

**3.2 Sectors of production** — [Sectors, economic transition and globalisation](notes/globalisation.md)
- Define the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors and give examples of each.
- Describe and explain how the importance of the sectors changes as a country develops (economic transition and the Clark–Fisher model).
- Explain the process of globalisation and evaluate its impacts.
- *Named example:* a transnational corporation and its global links — Fonterra (with the Volkswagen Group).

**3.3 Primary industry — farming and food shortage** — [Farming and food shortages](notes/farming.md)
- Describe farming types: commercial, subsistence, arable, pastoral, mixed, horticulture, intensive and extensive.
- Explain the factors affecting farm location (including von Thünen's model, as taught) and describe farming as a system of inputs, processes, outputs and feedback.
- Describe the negative environmental impacts of farming and evaluate sustainable management, including the long-term sustainability of dairy farming.
- Explain the causes and effects of food shortages and possible solutions (school requirement).
- *Named examples:* the operation of a New Zealand dairy farm; food shortages in Somalia (2011) and the Sahel.

**3.4 Secondary industry — manufacturing** — [Manufacturing industry: VW Wolfsburg](notes/manufacturing.md)
- Explain the importance of resources in the development of secondary industry.
- Describe and explain the factors influencing the location of factories and industrial zones.
- Describe manufacturing as a system of inputs, processes, outputs (products and waste) and feedback.
- Describe how manufacturing threatens the environment locally and globally, and evaluate its sustainability.
- *Named example (2026 school case study):* the Volkswagen factory, Wolfsburg, Germany; supporting NZ example: New Zealand Steel, Glenbrook.

**3.5 Tertiary industry — tourism** — [Tourism: Vietnam](notes/tourism.md)
- Describe and explain the growth of tourism and the factors behind it; the natural and cultural attractions and the infrastructure tourism requires; the Butler model.
- Evaluate the benefits and disadvantages of tourism for a receiving area and the environmental risks; explain how tourism can be managed sustainably.
- *Named example (2026 school case study):* Vietnam; supporting NZ example: Queenstown.

**3.6 Energy resources** — [Energy resources](notes/energy.md)
- Define renewable and non-renewable; describe the sources (geothermal, wind, HEP, solar, tidal, wave, biofuels; nuclear and fossil fuels).
- Evaluate the benefits, disadvantages and environmental impacts of each source.
- Describe the relative importance of energy sources globally and in countries at different levels of development.
- *Named example:* energy supply in New Zealand (with the Three Gorges Dam and Iceland).

**3.7 Global warming** — [Global warming and climate change](notes/global-warming.md)
- Define global warming and the enhanced greenhouse effect; describe anthropogenic pollution, how it is created and how it degrades the atmosphere.
- Describe the history of greenhouse-gas use and its link to global temperature; name the greenhouse gases and their sources; explain the carbon cycle.
- Describe the impacts on climate and weather, including the hydrological cycle; describe and evaluate solutions in farming, industry and homes, and mitigation vs adaptation.
- *Named example:* an area at heightened risk — Kiribati (the school's slides), with Tuvalu (sea-level rise) and Delhi (smog).

**Resources and environmental risk (school topic list; IGCSE 0460 water)** — [Water and the environmental risks of development](notes/environmental-risks.md)
- Describe resources and their use, resource depletion and sustainable development.
- Describe water supply and demand, the causes and effects of water shortage, and ways of managing supply and demand.
- Describe the environmental risks of economic development and evaluate ways of reducing them.
- *Named example:* Auckland's water supply (2020 drought), with Canterbury's aquifers.

## Geographical skills (tested inside the theme sections)

Skills follow the Cambridge IGCSE skills section. The skills the specification attaches to each theme are:
- **Natural Environment:** annotated diagrams, flood hydrographs, weather instruments, climate graphs, synoptic charts.
- **Population and Settlement:** line and pie graphs, age/sex pyramids, the push–pull, demographic transition and urban land-use models, fieldwork methodology.
- **Development:** line and pie graphs, mapping, photographs, statistics and tables of data.

**Map skills** ([Map skills](notes/map-skills.md))
- Read a 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 topographic map: four- and six-figure grid references, scale, straight and curved distances, area, 16-point compass directions and bearings from grid north.
- Interpret relief from contours and spot heights; calculate height differences and gradients; construct, complete and interpret cross-sections.
- Use the key to identify features; translate map scale into real size and shape; describe relief, drainage, coastlines, settlement pattern, communications and land use from map evidence; give reasons for the site and growth of a settlement; draw sketch maps.
- Describe human and physical landscapes from oblique and aerial photographs, satellite images, GIS output, field sketches and cartoons.

**Graphs, statistics and fieldwork** ([Graphs, statistics and fieldwork](notes/data-fieldwork.md))
- Construct, read and compare graphs, maps and diagrams: line, bar, divided bar, histogram, pictogram, pie, scatter, triangular, radial, dispersion, kite, wind rose, flow line, isoline and choropleth, plus climate graphs, population pyramids and synoptic charts; plot data when axes and scales are given.
- Extract and describe trends and patterns from tables of data and written extracts, and suggest an appropriate form of graphical representation.
- Carry out the calculations used with these resources: density, percentage and percentage change, mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range, rates per 1000, natural increase, dependency ratio, gradient, discharge and scale conversion.
- Apply fieldwork methodology to river, coastal and urban enquiries: hypothesis, site selection, sampling, data collection, presentation, analysis, conclusion and evaluation.

**Exam technique** ([Exam technique](notes/exam-technique.md))
- Answer to the command word, use developed named examples and diagrams, and structure paragraphs and essays to the level descriptors.
