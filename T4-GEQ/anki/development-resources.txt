#deck:Geography::3 Economic development and resources
Definition of development	An improvement in the quality of life and standard of living of a population
Economic, social and political development (class definitions)	Economic: value of goods and services and income; social: quality of life, well-being, equality; political: democracy and freedom of speech
GDP per capita: meaning and main weakness	Value of goods and services produced ÷ population; an average that hides inequality and ignores the informal economy
What the Human Development Index combines	Income, education and life expectancy, on a scale from 0 to 1
Two weaknesses of the HDI	Data missing for some countries; ignores the environment and inequality within countries
Happy Planet Index: what it measures and its weakness	How efficiently a country turns resources into long, happy lives without environmental damage; it leaves out the economy
Most useful indicators of economic and social development (mark scheme)	Economic: GNP or GNI per person; social: doctors per person or literacy
SEEP	The social, economic, environmental and political causes of uneven development
Rostow's five stages	Traditional society, pre-take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, high mass consumption
Three criticisms of Rostow's model	Outdated (1960) and based on a few European countries; debt repayments stop take-off; ignores exploitation of developing countries
Frank's dependency theory	The periphery sells low-value raw materials to the core, which processes them into high-value goods and grows rich
New Zealand versus Mali (class data)	GDP per capita US$52 180 v US$950; literacy 99% v 35.5%; primary-sector jobs 5.8% v 65%
NZ regions with the lowest levels of development	Northland and Gisborne/Tairāwhiti (also the East Coast and rural North Island)
Causes of regional inequality in New Zealand	Remoteness, 1980s closures of freezing works, rail and services, unprocessed primary industry, young people leaving, storm damage
Primary sector (exam definition)	Produces energy or basic raw materials — farming, fishing, forestry, mining
Secondary sector (exam definition)	Manufactures goods, transforming a raw material into something else
Tertiary sector (exam definition)	Provides services rather than goods — teaching, retail, refuse collection
Quaternary sector (exam definition)	Provides information services — ICT, consultancy, research and development
Clark–Fisher model	Employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary as a country develops
Why so many people in LICs work in the primary sector	Little education, little capital to build industry, no industrial revolution, subsistence farming
Definition of globalisation	The increasing interconnection of economies, cultures and populations through trade, investment, migration and technology
Definition of a transnational corporation	A company with its headquarters in one country and operations in others
Three benefits of a TNC to a host country	Jobs and training, tax and export earnings, technology and infrastructure with a multiplier effect
Three drawbacks of a TNC to a host country	Profits sent abroad, low wages, weak environmental standards exploited (and it can relocate)
Fonterra as a TNC	Farmer-owned NZ dairy co-operative formed in 2001; exports to well over 100 countries; brands such as Anchor and Anlene
Intensive versus extensive farming	Intensive: high inputs per hectare, high output per hectare; extensive: small inputs over a large area, low output per hectare
Commercial versus subsistence farming	Commercial: grown for sale; subsistence: grown to feed the farmer's household
Four physical factors affecting farm land use	Relief, soil, rainfall, temperature and length of growing season
Why access to a market matters to farmers	Produce is bulky or perishable, so transport cost and freshness count; farmers also buy supplies there
Inputs on a New Zealand dairy farm	Land, grass, water, cows, labour, fertiliser, supplementary feed, machinery, capital
Processes and outputs on a New Zealand dairy farm	Rotational grazing, twice-daily milking, calving, feeding; outputs milk and calves plus effluent, nitrate and methane
Location factors for New Zealand dairying	Mild moist climate with year-round grass, reliable rain or irrigation, flat land, a processing plant within tanker range, ports
Dairy's importance to New Zealand	About NZ$10.2 billion of direct value; roughly one in every three dollars of goods exports
Three environmental impacts of dairy farming	Nitrate leaching and eutrophication; water extraction for irrigation; methane emissions (also pugging)
Four sustainable dairy practices	Riparian fencing and planting, effluent ponds, nutrient budgeting, lower stocking rates
Natural causes of food shortages	Drought, floods, tropical storms, pests and crop disease
Economic and political causes of food shortages	Low investment, poor transport and storage, poverty and high prices, war and conflict
Somalia famine, 2011	Failed rains, high food prices and civil war blocking aid — about 260 000 deaths, half of them children under five
Manufacturing as a system	Inputs (raw materials, energy, labour, capital) → processes → outputs (products and waste) → feedback
Factors affecting the location of factories	Land, labour, raw materials, fuel and power, transport, markets, capital, government policy
Glenbrook steel mill: raw material and environmental issue	Waikato ironsand and coal; one of NZ's largest CO₂ emitters — an electric arc furnace was agreed in 2023
Ways to reduce pollution from industry	Emission limits, scrubbers on chimneys, treating waste water, fines and enforcement, renewable power, strict planning rules
Five reasons tourism has grown	Rising incomes, paid holidays, cheaper air travel, online booking, healthy retired populations
Three benefits and three problems of tourism	Benefits: jobs, foreign currency, infrastructure; problems: seasonal low-paid jobs, leakage, environmental damage
Queenstown: attractions and a key problem	Lakes, mountains, ski fields, the first commercial bungy (1988); housing too expensive for the workers tourism needs
Definition of renewable energy	Energy from a source that is not depleted when used — replenished naturally on a human timescale
Definition of non-renewable energy	Energy from a finite stock used up faster than it forms — coal, oil, natural gas, uranium
New Zealand's electricity mix, 2023	About 88% renewable: hydro 60%, geothermal 18%, gas 9%, wind 7%, coal 2%
Why 88% renewable electricity is not 88% renewable energy	Electricity is only about a quarter of NZ energy use; oil, mostly for transport, dominates the rest
Hydroelectric power: advantages and disadvantages	Reliable, renewable, stores water; floods valleys, displaces people, traps sediment, needs high relief and rainfall
Geothermal power: advantages and disadvantages	Continuous output, small land area, low emissions; only in volcanic areas, releases some gases, subsidence
Wind power: advantages and disadvantages	No fuel cost, no emissions in use; intermittent, visual and noise impact, needs back-up
Solar power: advantages and disadvantages	Free once installed, suits off-grid LICs; nothing at night, less in cloud, needs storage
Coal: advantages and disadvantages	Cheap, reliable, large reserves; the most CO₂ per unit of energy, air pollution, acid rain
Nuclear power: advantages and disadvantages	No CO₂ in generation, huge reliable output; very costly, radioactive waste, accident risk
Fuelwood in LICs	About 40% of the world's people rely on it — cheap and available, but causes deforestation and smoke unless trees are replanted
Location factors for a thermal power station	Near the fuel source or a port, large flat site, river or sea for cooling water
Location factors for a nuclear power station (Sizewell)	Coast for cooling water and fuel imports, away from big populations, stable flat land above flood level, rail and grid links
Three Gorges Dam: size and a major cost	22 500 MW on the Yangtze, the world's largest hydro station; over a million people relocated
World primary energy use in 2023 and the fossil-fuel share	About 172 000 TWh; about 81% from fossil fuels
Definition of the enhanced greenhouse effect	The extra warming caused by human emissions raising greenhouse gas concentrations
Four greenhouse gases and a source of each	CO₂ — burning fossil fuels; methane — cattle; nitrous oxide — fertiliser; CFCs — refrigeration
Change in CO₂ concentration since industrialisation	From about 280 ppm before 1750 to over 420 ppm today
How burning fossil fuels upsets the carbon cycle	It moves carbon from long-term geological stores into the atmosphere faster than plants and oceans can absorb it
Effect of global warming on the hydrological cycle	More evaporation and water vapour → heavier rain and stronger storms; more drought in dry regions
Largest source of New Zealand's greenhouse emissions	Agriculture, about 53% (energy about 37%)
Tuvalu: why it is at risk from global warming	Low atolls mostly under about 2 m above sea level — king-tide flooding, salt water in fresh water and crops, erosion
Why Delhi suffers winter smog	Vehicle, industrial and crop-stubble smoke trapped under temperature inversions
