#deck:Geography::2 The natural environment
Three components of the geomorphic cycle	Land building (folding, faulting, volcanism), land modifying (weathering and erosion), recycling into sedimentary and metamorphic rock
Which processes are land-modifying agents of erosion?	Fluvial, glacial, aeolian and marine — not folding, faulting or volcanism
Causal chain of mantle convection currents	Radioactive decay heats the mantle → hot, less dense rock rises → cools near the crust, becomes denser and sinks → drags the plates
Constructive (divergent) plate boundary	Plates move apart, magma rises — ridges, rift valleys, shield volcanoes (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland)
Destructive (convergent) plate boundary	Denser oceanic plate subducts — trench, fold mountains, composite volcanoes, violent earthquakes
Conservative plate boundary	Plates slide past each other — earthquakes but no volcanoes (San Andreas Fault)
The plate boundary through New Zealand	Pacific–Australian: the Pacific plate subducts under the North Island; the Alpine Fault runs through the South Island
Formation of fold mountains	Converging plates squeeze sea-floor sediments → layers buckle into anticlines and synclines and are thrust up → thickened crust rises
Basaltic magma and its landform	Low silica, runny, gentle frequent eruptions — shield volcano
Andesitic magma and its landform	Intermediate silica, sticky, explosive — composite cone
Rhyolitic magma and its landform	High silica, very sticky, violently explosive — lava dome or caldera
Focus versus epicentre	Focus: where the rock ruptures underground; epicentre: the point on the surface directly above it
Magnitude versus intensity	Magnitude: energy released (Richter or moment magnitude scale); intensity: shaking and damage at a place (Modified Mercalli)
Definition of pyroclastic flow	A fast, extremely hot avalanche of gas, ash and rock down the side of a volcano
Four reasons people live near volcanoes	Fertile soils, geothermal energy, tourism, minerals (also jobs, family ties, low perception of risk)
Why volcanoes are easier to predict than earthquakes	Volcanoes give warning signs — gas, ground swelling, tremor swarms; earthquakes have no reliable short-term warning
Christchurch earthquake: date, magnitude and deaths	22 February 2011; magnitude 6.2; 185 deaths, 115 in the CTV building
Why the Christchurch 2011 earthquake was so destructive	Very shallow focus (about 5 km), epicentre close to the city, struck at lunchtime, liquefaction of soft sediment
Whakaari / White Island eruption: date, type and toll	9 December 2019; phreatic (steam-driven); 22 of the 47 people on the island died
Whakaari's lesson for hazard management	The alert level had been raised to 2 but tours continued — monitoring fails without control of access
Formation of Rangitoto (field trip)	About 600 years ago: underwater eruption → scoria cone with a tuff ring → basaltic lava flows built the shield shape
Weathering versus erosion	Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion removes it by a moving agent
Freeze–thaw weathering	Water in cracks freezes and expands about 9%, prising the rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it
Carbonation	Rainwater plus CO₂ forms weak carbonic acid, which dissolves the calcium carbonate of limestone
Hydrolysis	Slightly acidic water reacts with feldspar in granite, rotting it to clay
Formation of a granite tor	Hydrolysis rots granite along close joints underground; widely jointed blocks survive and stand up once the clay is removed
Formation of glacier ice	More snow falls than melts → compression expels air → névé (firn) → dense glacier ice that moves downslope under gravity
Formation of a cirque	Snow fills a hollow; freeze–thaw and plucking steepen the back wall; rotational slip and abrasion deepen the floor, leaving a rock lip
Definition of a fiord, with a NZ example	A glacial trough eroded below sea level and later drowned — Milford Sound / Piopiotahi
Drainage basin and watershed	A drainage basin is the area drained by a river and its tributaries; the watershed is the ridge dividing it from the next basin
River discharge: formula and unit	Cross-sectional area × mean velocity, in cumecs (m³/s)
Definition of lag time	The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge
Four factors giving a short lag time and high peak	Steep slopes, impermeable rock, saturated or frozen ground, urban surfaces (also bare ground, circular basin)
Hydraulic action	Moving water hits the banks and forces air into cracks, which expands and breaks the rock
Four river transport processes, finest to coarsest	Solution, suspension, saltation, traction
Three things the Hjulström curve shows	Bigger particles need faster flow to be picked up; clay also needs a high velocity (cohesive); once moving, particles stay in transport at lower velocities
Bradshaw model: downstream changes	Increase: discharge, width, depth, velocity, load quantity; decrease: gradient, bed roughness, particle size
Formation of a waterfall	Hard rock over soft; the soft rock is undercut into a plunge pool; the hard overhang collapses; the fall retreats, leaving a gorge
Formation of a meander	Fastest flow on the outside bend erodes a river cliff; slow flow on the inside deposits a slip-off slope; helicoidal flow moves sediment across
Formation of an ox-bow lake	Erosion narrows the meander neck → a flood cuts through → deposition seals off the old loop
Why deposition occurs at a delta	The river meets the sea or a lake, slows and loses energy; flocculation sinks clay; sediment builds faster than it is removed
Waikato River: length and opportunities	About 425 km from Lake Taupō; eight hydro dams, part of Auckland's water supply, irrigation for dairy farms
Cyclone Gabrielle: dates, deaths and responses	12–14 February 2023; 11 deaths; national state of emergency; voluntary buy-outs of Category 3 land
Why Bangladesh floods so often	Low-lying Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta, monsoon rain, Himalayan snowmelt, cyclones and storm surges
Weather versus climate	Weather: the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time; climate: the average weather over at least 30 years
Elements of weather	Temperature, precipitation, humidity, wind, sunshine, cloud cover, air pressure
Stevenson screen: siting and design rules	White, louvred, roofed, on grass, thermometers about 1.2 m up, door facing away from the Sun (south in NZ), well away from objects
Definition of relative humidity	The water vapour present as a percentage of the maximum the air could hold at that temperature
Definition of dew point	The temperature to which air must be cooled to become saturated, so condensation begins
Causal chain of cloud formation	Air rises → expands and cools → reaches dew point → vapour condenses on condensation nuclei → cloud
Four ways air is made to rise	Convection, orographic (relief), frontal, convergence
Steps of convectional rainfall	Sun heats the ground → warm air rises → cools to dew point → cumulonimbus → heavy afternoon rain and thunder
Relief rainfall and rain shadow	Moist air forced over mountains cools → rain on the windward side → air descends, warms and dries → rain shadow and föhn wind
Frontal rainfall	Warm, less dense air is forced to rise over colder, denser air at a front, cools and condenses
How dew and frost form	Clear calm night → ground radiates heat away → air touching it cools to dew point → dew; frost if below 0 °C
What closely spaced isobars mean	A steep pressure gradient — strong winds
The Coriolis effect in the Southern Hemisphere	Moving air is deflected to the left (no deflection at the equator)
Wind circulation round lows and highs in the Southern Hemisphere	Clockwise into lows; anticlockwise out of highs
Buys Ballot's law in New Zealand	Look into the wind and low pressure is on your left
Tri-cellular model: surface pressure at 0°, 30°, 60° and 90°	Low (ITCZ), high (subtropical), low (polar front), high (polar)
Why hot deserts lie at about 30°	Air sinking from the Hadley cell warms, its relative humidity falls and no cloud forms — the subtropical high
Why the trade winds north of the equator blow from the north-east	Air flows from the subtropical high to the equatorial low and the Coriolis force deflects it to the right
Factors that make climate vary from place to place	Latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents, prevailing winds, relief
Climate graph: temperature range and annual rainfall	Range = highest minus lowest monthly temperature; annual rainfall = sum of the 12 monthly bars
Cold front: symbol and weather	Blue triangles; cumulonimbus, short heavy showers, temperature drop; in NZ the wind swings from north-west to south-west
Warm front: symbol and weather	Red semicircles; cloud thickening from cirrus to nimbostratus, long steady rain
Weather in an anticyclone	Sinking air, light winds, clear skies — warm and sunny in summer, frost and fog in winter
Weather in a depression	Rising air, closely spaced isobars, strong winds, cloud and rain, usually with fronts
New Zealand's summer versus winter weather	Summer: under subtropical highs (Hadley–Ferrel boundary), settled; winter: belts shift north, fronts at the Ferrel–Polar boundary bring frontal rain
New Zealand's rainfall pattern and its cause	Wet west, dry east — westerlies forced over the ranges; Milford Sound about 6700 mm, Alexandra under 400 mm
Definition of ecosystem	A community of living organisms interacting with the non-living parts of their environment as one system
Tropical rainforest climate	About 27 °C all year with a range of 1–2 °C; over 2000 mm of rain, falling every month; high humidity
Why rainforest soils are infertile	Rapid decomposition; nutrients are held in the biomass, not the soil; heavy rain leaches the rest
Main cause of deforestation in the Amazon	Cattle ranching (also soya, logging, mining, roads such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway)
Why hot deserts have a large daily temperature range	No cloud: intense insolation by day, and heat radiates straight back to space at night
Causes of desertification in the Sahel	Over-cultivation, cutting trees for fuel, overgrazing, poor soil management, climate change
Constructive versus destructive waves	Constructive: low, long, strong swash, build the beach; destructive: high, steep, strong backwash, remove beach material
Longshore drift	Swash carries sediment up the beach at an angle, backwash drags it straight down — a net zigzag along the coast
Cave to stump sequence	Waves widen a joint into a cave → cut through to an arch → roof collapses leaving a stack → stack eroded to a stump
Formation of a wave-cut platform	A notch is cut at high-tide level → the overhang collapses → the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping rock platform
Formation of a spit	Longshore drift carries sediment past a bend in the coast into open water; the tip curves; salt marsh forms behind
Groynes: how they work and the drawback	They trap sediment moving by longshore drift, building a wider beach — but starve beaches downdrift
