# Geography glossary

Definitions earn marks on their own, and precision matters. Where two terms are routinely confused they are defined next to each other with the distinction stated.

## Population and settlement

**Population density** — the average number of people per square kilometre.
**Population distribution** — the way population is spread across an area. *Density is a number; distribution is a pattern, and a question about distribution wants locations.*

**Birth rate** — the number of live births per 1000 people per year.
**Death rate** — the number of deaths per 1000 people per year.
**Fertility rate** — the average number of children a woman has over her lifetime. *Birth rate is per 1000 of the whole population; fertility rate is per woman, so it is unaffected by age structure.*
**Infant mortality rate** — the number of deaths of children under one year per 1000 live births.
**Life expectancy** — the average number of years a newborn can expect to live.
**Natural increase** — the birth rate minus the death rate; negative when deaths exceed births.
**Net migration** — immigration minus emigration for an area.
**Replacement level fertility** — the fertility rate at which a population exactly replaces itself, around 2.1 children per woman.

**Migration** — a permanent or semi-permanent change of residence.
**Emigration** — leaving a country or area; the source loses people.
**Immigration** — entering a country or area; the destination gains people. *Migration is the movement itself; emigration and immigration are the same movement seen from the two ends.*
**Internal migration** — a move within one country, such as rural to urban.
**International migration** — a move across a national border.
**Voluntary migration** — a move the migrant chooses to make, usually for work or family.
**Forced migration** — a move the migrant has no real choice about, caused by conflict, persecution or disaster.
**Refugee** — a person who has fled their own country because of a well-founded fear of persecution, conflict or disaster.
**Internally displaced person** — a person forced to leave home but still within their own country.
**Push factor** — a negative condition at the origin encouraging a person to leave.
**Pull factor** — an attractive condition at the destination encouraging a person to move there.
**Intervening obstacle** — anything between origin and destination that makes the move harder: distance, cost, borders, language, lack of information.
**Brain drain** — the loss of skilled and educated workers from an area through emigration.
**Remittance** — money sent home by a migrant to family in the source area.

**Demographic Transition Model** — a model describing the change in birth rate, death rate and total population as a country develops, in five stages.
**Population pyramid (age/sex pyramid)** — a pair of back-to-back horizontal bar graphs showing the proportion of males and females in each age group.
**Dependency ratio** — the number of young and elderly dependants per 100 people of working age.
**Youthful population** — a population with a high proportion of under-15s, caused by high birth and fertility rates.
**Ageing population** — a population with a high and rising proportion of over-65s, caused by long life expectancy and low fertility.
**Over-population** — more people than the available resources and technology can support at a reasonable standard of living.
**Under-population** — too few people to use the available resources fully, so potential output is lost.
**Optimum population** — the population that gives the highest standard of living from the resources available.
**Anti-natalist policy** — a government policy intended to reduce the birth rate.
**Pro-natalist policy** — a government policy intended to raise the birth rate.

**Settlement** — a place where people live, from an isolated farm to a city.
**Site** — the actual land on which a settlement is built.
**Situation** — a settlement's position relative to the surrounding region. *Site explains why it began there; situation usually explains why it grew.*
**Dispersed settlement** — isolated buildings with no central cluster.
**Nucleated settlement** — buildings clustered around a central point.
**Linear settlement** — buildings strung out along a route, river or valley.
**Settlement hierarchy** — the ranking of settlements by population, by the services they offer and by the size of the area they serve.
**Sphere of influence (urban field)** — the area served by a settlement or a particular service.
**Range** — the maximum distance people will travel to use a service.
**Threshold population** — the minimum number of customers needed for a service to survive.
**High-order service** — an expensive, infrequently used service needing a large threshold population, such as a hospital or university.
**Low-order service** — a cheap, frequently used service with a small threshold, such as a dairy or post box.
**Primate city** — a city disproportionately larger and more dominant than any other in its country.

**Urbanisation** — an increase in the **proportion** of a population living in urban areas.
**Urban growth** — an increase in the urban **population or built-up area**. *A city can grow in size while national urbanisation stalls, so the two are not interchangeable.*
**Suburbanisation** — the outward growth of housing onto the city edge, with residents commuting back in.
**Counter-urbanisation** — the movement of people out of cities to smaller towns and rural areas.
**Re-urbanisation** — movement back into inner-city areas, usually following regeneration.
**Gentrification** — the buying and improvement of inner-city property by higher-income residents, raising prices and displacing poorer ones.
**Central business district (CBD)** — the commercial and administrative core of a city, with the highest land values and accessibility.
**Bid-rent theory** — the principle that land value falls with distance from the city centre, and that the activity able to pay most for accessibility occupies the centre.
**Inner city** — the older zone just outside the CBD, typically of 19th-century housing and obsolete industry.
**Rural–urban fringe** — the transition zone where the built-up area meets open countryside.
**Urban sprawl** — the uncontrolled outward spread of a built-up area onto surrounding land.
**Greenfield site** — undeveloped land on the edge of a built-up area.
**Brownfield site** — previously developed land available for reuse.
**Intensification** — increasing the number of dwellings within the existing urban area rather than expanding outwards.
**Squatter settlement (informal settlement)** — an area of self-built housing erected without legal title to the land or planning permission.
**Informal sector** — unregistered, untaxed and insecure work such as street trading or waste picking.
**Formal sector** — registered, taxed and regulated employment with contracts and legal protection.

## The natural environment

**Weather** — the state of the atmosphere at one place over hours or days.
**Climate** — the average pattern of weather at a place over a long period, conventionally 30 years or more. *Weather is what is happening; climate is what usually happens.*

**Weathering** — the breakdown of rock in place by physical, chemical or biological processes.
**Erosion** — the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent: water, ice, wind or waves. *Weathering happens where the rock sits; erosion carries the loosened material away.*
**Denudation** — the combined wearing down of the land surface by weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition.
**Transportation** — the movement of eroded material by an agent.
**Deposition** — the laying down of transported material when the agent loses the energy to carry it.
**Mass movement** — the downslope movement of soil and rock under gravity, including slides, slumps and soil creep.

**Freeze–thaw weathering** — repeated freezing and expansion of water in a crack, prising the rock apart.
**Exfoliation** — the peeling away of the outer layers of rock caused by repeated heating and cooling.
**Carbonation** — the chemical dissolving of calcium carbonate in limestone by weakly acidic rainwater.
**Hydrolysis** — the chemical reaction of slightly acidic water with feldspar in granite, rotting it to clay.
**Scree** — angular rock fragments accumulated at the base of a cliff by freeze–thaw.

**Plate tectonics** — the theory that the Earth's crust is divided into plates that move over the mantle, driven by convection currents.
**Constructive (divergent) boundary** — where plates move apart and new crust forms from rising magma.
**Destructive (convergent) boundary** — where plates move together and the denser oceanic plate is subducted.
**Conservative boundary** — where plates slide past one another without creating or destroying crust.
**Subduction** — the sinking of a denser plate beneath a less dense one at a destructive boundary.
**Focus** — the point underground where an earthquake's rupture begins.
**Epicentre** — the point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus.
**Composite cone** — a steep-sided volcano built of alternating ash and andesitic lava layers.
**Shield volcano** — a broad, gently sloping volcano built of runny basaltic lava.
**Lahar** — a fast-moving flow of volcanic ash mixed with water.

**Drainage basin** — the area of land drained by one river and its tributaries.
**Watershed** — the ridge of high land dividing one drainage basin from the next.
**Infiltration** — the soaking of water into the soil.
**Surface runoff (overland flow)** — the flow of water across the ground surface when rainfall exceeds infiltration.
**Throughflow** — the lateral movement of water through the soil towards a channel.
**Baseflow** — the slow seepage of groundwater that sustains a river between storms.
**Discharge** — the volume of water passing a point in a channel per second, in cumecs (m³/s).
**Lag time** — the interval between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
**Flood hydrograph** — a graph of river discharge over time in response to a rainfall event.
**Bankfull discharge** — the discharge that exactly fills the channel; above it, the river floods.
**Hydraulic action** — erosion by the sheer force of moving water pushing into cracks.
**Abrasion (corrasion)** — erosion by the load scraping against the bed and banks.
**Attrition** — the wearing down of load particles as they collide with each other, making them smaller and rounder.
**Corrosion (solution)** — the chemical dissolving of soluble rock by river or sea water.
**Hjulström curve** — a graph of water velocity against particle size showing the velocities at which material is eroded, transported and deposited.
**Meander** — a sweeping bend in a river, with erosion on the outside bank and deposition on the inside.
**Ox-bow lake** — a crescent-shaped lake formed when a meander neck is cut through and the loop is sealed off.
**Flood plain** — the flat valley floor either side of a river, built of alluvium deposited during floods.
**Levée** — a natural raised bank of coarse sediment along a channel edge, built by repeated flooding.
**Delta** — a depositional landform of sediment at a river mouth where the river enters standing water.
**Hard engineering** — flood or coastal defence by built structures such as dams, levées, sea walls and groynes.
**Soft engineering** — flood or coastal defence working with natural processes: zoning, afforestation, beach nourishment, managed retreat.

**Longshore drift** — the movement of sediment along a coast by waves approaching at an angle.
**Wave refraction** — the bending of wave crests around a headland, concentrating energy on the headland.
**Constructive wave** — a low, long, infrequent wave with a strong swash that builds up a beach.
**Destructive wave** — a high, short, frequent wave with a strong backwash that removes beach material.
**Spit** — a long ridge of sand or shingle deposited by longshore drift, attached to the land at one end.
**Tombolo** — a spit that has grown far enough to join an island to the mainland.
**Wave-cut platform** — the gently sloping rock surface left as a cliff retreats.

**Insolation** — incoming solar radiation received at the Earth's surface.
**Adiabatic cooling** — the cooling of a parcel of air as it rises and expands, without heat being added or removed.
**Lapse rate** — the rate at which air temperature falls with increasing altitude.
**Dew point** — the temperature at which air becomes saturated and condensation begins.
**Condensation** — the change of water vapour into liquid water when air is cooled to its dew point.
**Relative humidity** — the amount of water vapour in the air as a percentage of the maximum it could hold at that temperature.
**Precipitation** — any form of water falling from the atmosphere: rain, snow, hail, sleet.
**Convectional rainfall** — rainfall from air rising because the surface beneath it has been strongly heated.
**Relief (orographic) rainfall** — rainfall from air forced to rise over a mountain barrier.
**Frontal rainfall** — rainfall from warm, less dense air being forced to rise over colder air at a front.
**Rain shadow** — the drier area on the leeward side of a mountain barrier.
**Dew** — water condensed directly onto a cold surface on a clear, calm night.
**Frost** — ice formed directly on a surface when the dew point is below 0 °C.
**Isobar** — a line on a map joining points of equal atmospheric pressure.
**Depression (low)** — an area of low pressure with rising air, cloud, rain and fronts.
**Anticyclone (high)** — an area of high pressure with sinking air, settled weather and clear skies.
**Synoptic chart** — a map showing the state of the atmosphere at one time, using isobars, fronts and pressure centres.
**Stevenson screen** — a white, louvred box about 1.25 m above short grass that holds thermometers in standard, comparable conditions.
**Tri-cellular model** — a model of global atmospheric circulation using three pairs of cells: Hadley, Ferrel and Polar.

**Ecosystem** — a community of living organisms interacting with the non-living parts of their environment as one system.
**Biomass** — the total mass of living material in an ecosystem.
**Nutrient cycle** — the movement of nutrients between soil, biomass and litter stores.
**Leaching** — the washing of soluble nutrients downwards out of the upper soil by percolating water.
**Latosol** — the deep, red, leached, nutrient-poor soil typical of tropical rainforests.
**Deforestation** — the clearance of forest for other land uses.
**Desertification** — the degradation of dry land into desert-like conditions through overgrazing, cultivation, deforestation and drought.

## Economic development and resources

**Development** — an improvement in the quality of life and standard of living of a population.
**Economic growth** — an increase in a country's output or income. *Growth is a rise in output; development is the improvement in people's lives that may or may not follow it.*
**Standard of living** — the material conditions a person lives in, measured mainly by income and possessions.
**Quality of life** — overall well-being, including health, education, security, environment and freedom as well as income.
**Gross domestic product (GDP)** — the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
**GDP per capita** — GDP divided by population, an average income per person.
**Purchasing power parity (PPP)** — an adjustment to income figures for differences in local prices, allowing fair comparison.
**Human Development Index (HDI)** — a composite measure of development combining income, education and life expectancy on a 0–1 scale.
**Happy Planet Index (HPI)** — a composite measure of how efficiently a country converts resources into long, happy lives.
**Brandt Line** — the 1980 line dividing a richer "North" from a poorer "South", now badly out of date.
**HIC, MIC, LIC** — high-, middle- and low-income countries, classified by income per head.
**Core and periphery** — the pattern in which investment and services concentrate in favoured regions while remote regions lose people and services.
**Urbanisation of poverty** — the shift of poverty from being mainly rural to mainly urban as cities grow faster than formal employment.

**Primary sector** — extracting or growing raw materials.
**Secondary sector** — processing raw materials and manufacturing goods.
**Tertiary sector** — providing services.
**Quaternary sector** — knowledge, research and information services.
**Economic transition** — the change in the relative importance of the sectors as a country develops.
**Globalisation** — the increasing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and populations through trade, investment, migration and technology.
**Transnational corporation (TNC)** — a company with its headquarters in one country and operations in others.
**Multiplier effect** — the way an initial investment generates further jobs, income and spending in an area.
**Footloose industry** — an industry not tied to raw materials or power, and therefore free in its choice of location.
**Agglomeration** — the clustering of firms in one place to share suppliers, labour and services.

**Commercial farming** — farming to sell the produce.
**Subsistence farming** — farming to feed the farmer's own household.
**Arable farming** — growing crops.
**Pastoral farming** — raising livestock.
**Intensive farming** — high inputs of labour or capital per hectare, giving a high yield per hectare.
**Extensive farming** — farming over a large area with low inputs per hectare.
**Horticulture** — the intensive growing of fruit, vegetables and flowers.
**Food shortage** — a situation in which the available food is insufficient for a population's needs, through failures of production, distribution or affordability.
**Carrying capacity** — the maximum population or stocking rate an area can support without degradation.

**Renewable resource** — a resource replenished on a human timescale, such as wind, solar or hydro energy.
**Non-renewable resource** — a resource present in a finite stock that is used up faster than it forms, such as coal, oil, gas and uranium. *Renewable describes the rate of replacement, not the absence of environmental impact — a dam is renewable and still floods a valley.*
**Fossil fuel** — coal, oil or natural gas, formed from the remains of organisms over geological time.
**Geothermal energy** — energy from heat in the Earth's crust, usable where volcanic activity brings it near the surface.
**Hydroelectric power** — electricity generated by water falling through turbines.
**Energy mix** — the combination of sources a country uses to meet its energy demand.
**Sustainability** — meeting present needs without preventing future generations from meeting theirs.
**Sustainable development** — development that keeps resource use within the rate of replacement and waste within the environment's capacity to absorb it.
**Kaitiakitanga** — guardianship and protection of the environment, a Māori concept of sustainable stewardship.
**Externality** — a cost or benefit of an activity that falls on third parties rather than on the producer.

**Greenhouse effect** — the natural warming of the Earth caused by atmospheric gases absorbing outgoing longwave radiation.
**Enhanced greenhouse effect** — the additional warming caused by human emissions raising greenhouse gas concentrations.
**Global warming** — the long-term rise in average global temperature caused by the enhanced greenhouse effect.
**Anthropogenic** — caused by human activity.
**Carbon cycle** — the movement of carbon between the atmosphere, oceans, biosphere and geological stores.
**Carbon sink** — a store that absorbs more carbon than it releases, such as a forest or the ocean.
**Mitigation** — action to reduce the causes of climate change, mainly by cutting emissions.
**Adaptation** — action to reduce the harm from climate change that is already happening.
**Eutrophication** — the enrichment of water by nitrate and phosphate, causing algal growth that strips oxygen from the water.
**Leakage** — the share of tourist spending that leaves the host area, typically to foreign-owned airlines and hotel chains.
**Ecotourism** — small-scale, locally run tourism designed to conserve the environment and benefit local people.

## Geographical skills

**Scale** — the ratio between a distance on the map and the same distance on the ground.
**Grid reference** — a coordinate locating a place on a map; four figures name a square, six figures locate a point.
**Easting** — a vertical grid line, numbered west to east and quoted first.
**Northing** — a horizontal grid line, numbered south to north and quoted second.
**Bearing** — the clockwise angle of a direction measured from grid north, written as three digits.
**Contour** — a line joining points of equal height above sea level.
**Contour interval** — the vertical difference in height between successive contours.
**Spot height** — a point on a map marked with its exact height.
**Gradient** — the steepness of a slope, expressed as vertical rise over horizontal distance.
**Cross-section** — a side-on profile of the ground along a chosen line, drawn from contour heights.
**Vertical exaggeration** — the factor by which a cross-section's vertical scale is stretched relative to its horizontal scale.
**Relief** — the shape and height of the land surface.
**Drainage density** — the total length of streams per unit area of a drainage basin.
**Sketch map** — a simplified hand-drawn map showing one pattern, with a frame, title, north arrow and key.
**Annotation** — a label that explains what a feature is doing, as opposed to simply naming it.
**Isoline** — a line on a map joining points of equal value.
**Choropleth map** — a map using shading density to show rates or densities by area.
**Flow line** — a map line whose width is proportional to the volume moving between two places.
**Triangular graph** — an equilateral-triangle graph for three components that total 100%.
**Dispersion graph** — a graph plotting individual values on one axis to show their spread.
**Kite diagram** — a symmetrical graph showing species abundance along a transect.
**Wind rose** — a radial graph showing the frequency and strength of wind from each direction.
**Proportional symbol** — a map symbol whose area is scaled to the quantity it represents.
**Mean** — the arithmetic average, the sum of the values divided by their number.
**Median** — the middle value when the data are ranked in order.
**Mode** — the most frequently occurring value in a data set.
**Range** — the difference between the maximum and the minimum value.
**Interquartile range** — the difference between the upper and lower quartiles, describing the middle half of the data and ignoring extremes.
**Anomaly** — a value or place that does not fit the general pattern.
**Correlation** — a statistical relationship between two variables; it does not by itself prove causation.
**Primary data** — data you collect yourself in the field.
**Secondary data** — data collected by someone else, such as census figures or published maps.
**Hypothesis** — a testable statement that a fieldwork enquiry sets out to support or reject.
**Sampling** — selecting part of a population to measure; random, systematic or stratified.
**Transect** — a line along which measurements are taken at intervals.
**SDEEL** — the paragraph structure of Statement, Describe, Explain, Example and Link back to the question.
