# Case studies and named examples

**This file is a template, not a substitute.** The syllabus requires specific illustrative examples, and your teacher's chosen examples are the ones the exam will reward. Fill each slot from your own class notes, the textbook and the named examples taught in lessons. Where a slot below names a real example, it is there because it matches the slot and is the one the course materials point to — the location and date are reliable, the figures are not supplied and must come from your notes.

**It is very unlikely that a whole question will be about one case study.** You will be asked to *refer to named examples to support your answer*, and to use examples to evaluate in essays. That means a case study is only useful if the facts in it do work in an argument.

## What every case study must supply

| Element | What the examiner is looking for |
|---|---|
| **Name** | the actual place, scheme, event or company, spelled correctly |
| **Location** | country, region, and a locating detail — a river, coast, latitude, nearest city |
| **Scale** | local, regional, national or global; area affected; population affected |
| **Date** | the year, and for an event the month and day |
| **Figures** | two or three precise, sourced numbers with units — not "a lot" or "millions" |
| **Causes** | the physical and human causes, in a causal chain |
| **Effects on people** | deaths, injuries, homes, jobs, services, cost, displacement — short and long term |
| **Effects on the environment** | habitat, water, soil, air, landforms |
| **Responses** | immediate response, and longer-term management, with who paid and who decided |
| **Evaluation** | did the response work, judged against a stated criterion, and who gained and lost |

Two or three sharp facts used in an argument beat a page of recited detail. **Never invent a figure.** If you cannot remember the number, write the comparison instead: "deaths were far lower than in the earlier event because the warning system worked."

## Building a case study you can actually use

1. **Write it on one side of one page.** If it does not fit, it is not memorised.
2. **Head it with the five anchors:** name, location, scale, date, and the slot it fills.
3. **Three figures, each with a unit and a year.** No more — you will not recall more under pressure, and a wrong figure is worse than none.
4. **One causal chain** written as arrows, so you can reproduce the mechanism rather than the prose.
5. **Two responses and a verdict on each**, with the criterion named. Most essay marks live here.
6. **Note which other slots it can fill.** The syllabus allows one example to serve more than one topic: a river case study can supply flooding, hazard management, fieldwork and a landform question.
7. **Check the date against the question.** An "ageing population" answer built on a policy that was replaced in 2021 needs the replacement mentioned.
