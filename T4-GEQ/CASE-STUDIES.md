# Case studies and named examples

Every slot the syllabus or the school's topic pages require is filled below with an example the course materials point to. Where your teacher used a different example in class, learn that one too — but these are accurate, dated and ready to use. Figures marked *approximate* are rounded values that vary between sources; quote them as "about". Never invent a figure in the exam: if you cannot remember the number, use the comparison instead.

Entries are grouped by the specification's three themes, and each one links to the notes page that uses it. The **2026 school case studies** are Dharavi and Diepsloot (slums), the Volkswagen factory at Wolfsburg (manufacturing) and Vietnam (tourism); New Zealand examples such as Glenbrook and Queenstown are kept as supporting material. The three sectors of the economy each have one full case study, in order: **New Zealand dairy farming (primary)**, **VW Wolfsburg (secondary)** and **Vietnam tourism (tertiary)**.

**It is very unlikely that a whole question will be about one case study.** You will be asked to *refer to named examples to support your answer*, and essays are capped at **6 out of 10 without examples**. Levels 3 and 4 need *developed* examples — a place, a date and a fact that does work in the argument — not tags such as "as seen in Waikato".

| Element | What the examiner is looking for |
|---|---|
| Name and location | the actual place, event, scheme or company, with a locating detail |
| Date and scale | the year (and day, for an event); area and population affected |
| Figures | two or three precise numbers with units |
| Causes | physical and human, as a causal chain |
| Effects | on people and on the environment; short and long term |
| Responses | immediate and long term, who paid and who decided |
| Evaluation | did it work, judged against a stated criterion; who gained and who lost |

## Theme 1: Natural Environment

### Earthquake — Christchurch, New Zealand, 22 February 2011

*Page:* [The geomorphic cycle and plate tectonics](notes/tectonics.md)

- **When and where:** 12:51 pm on 22 February 2011; epicentre about 10 km south-east of the city centre near Lyttelton; focus only about 5 km deep.
- **Size:** magnitude 6.2 — smaller than the magnitude 7.1 Darfield earthquake of 4 September 2010 that began the sequence (which killed no one), but far more destructive because it was shallow, close to the city and struck at lunchtime.
- **Cause:** movement on a previously unknown fault beneath the Port Hills, caused by stress from the collision of the Pacific and Australian plates (the plate boundary, the Alpine Fault, lies further west).
- **Effects on people:** **185 deaths** — 115 in the collapse of the CTV building and 18 in the Pyne Gould Corporation building; thousands injured; the CBD cordoned off for more than two years; the cathedral spire collapsed; water, sewerage, power and roads broken across the east of the city; total cost estimated at about **NZ\$40 billion**.
- **Effects on the environment:** widespread **liquefaction** in the eastern suburbs, pushing up silt and sinking land; rockfalls and cliff collapses in the Port Hills; land subsidence along the Avon River.
- **Responses:** New Zealand's first national state of emergency; urban search-and-rescue teams from New Zealand and overseas; the Canterbury Earthquake Recovery Authority (2011–2016) ran the rebuild; about 8000 homes on the worst-damaged land were **red-zoned** and bought by the Crown (*approximate*), and much of that land is now the Ōtākaro Avon River Corridor park; a Royal Commission led to stricter rules for earthquake-prone buildings (2016).
- **Evaluation:** most modern houses stood up, but older and poorly designed commercial buildings killed most of the victims — the lesson was enforcement of building standards, not prediction, which remains impossible.
- **Also fills:** hazard-management essays; "why people live in hazardous places"; liquefaction as an environmental effect.

### Volcanic eruption — Whakaari / White Island, New Zealand, 9 December 2019

*Page:* [The geomorphic cycle and plate tectonics](notes/tectonics.md)

- **When and where:** 2:11 pm on 9 December 2019; Whakaari, an andesitic stratovolcano and New Zealand's most active cone volcano, about 48 km off Whakatāne in the Bay of Plenty.
- **Type:** a **phreatic (steam-driven) eruption** — magma heated the hydrothermal system until it blasted out steam, ash and rock with little warning.
- **Effects:** **47 people** were on the island, mostly cruise-ship passengers on guided tours; **22 died** (two bodies were never recovered) and the 25 survivors nearly all had severe burns, stretching burns units across New Zealand and Australia.
- **Warning signs:** GNS Science had raised the volcanic alert level to **2** (moderate to heightened unrest) on 18 November 2019, but tours continued.
- **Responses:** local helicopter pilots flew survivors off within hours; the NZ Defence Force recovered bodies on 13 December; WorkSafe prosecuted the owners and tour operators; the island has been closed to tourists since.
- **Evaluation:** the monitoring worked — unrest was detected — but an alert level describes current activity and does not forecast an eruption, and there was no rule that stopped access. Monitoring without control of access did not protect people.
- **Also fills:** why people visit and live near volcanoes (tourism income); prediction versus prevention.

### Volcano visited on the field trip — Rangitoto, Auckland

*Page:* [The geomorphic cycle and plate tectonics](notes/tectonics.md)

- Auckland's **youngest and largest** volcano, about **600 years old**, in the Auckland Volcanic Field of about 50 volcanoes; summit about 260 m.
- **Formation (as credited by the mark scheme):** magma rose from a hot spot beneath the field → an underwater eruption → a **scoria cone** formed, with a **tuff ring** around its base → a second eruption above sea level → runny **basaltic** lava flowed down the sides, building the broad, gentle **shield** shape.
- **Field-trip variables** from the jetty to the summit: vegetation density, vegetation height, slope steepness, soil depth.

### River: opportunities, hazards and management — the Waikato River

*Page:* [Rivers: flooding, management and fieldwork](notes/river-flooding.md)

- New Zealand's longest river, about **425 km**, flowing from Lake Taupō to the Tasman Sea at Port Waikato; its catchment is about 14 000 km² (*approximate*).
- **Opportunities:** **eight hydroelectric dams** with nine power stations (including Maraetai 360 MW, Arapuni 197 MW, Ohakuri 112 MW and Whakamaru 100 MW); cooling water for Huntly power station; part of **Auckland's water supply**, treated at Tuakau since 2002; irrigation and stock water for Waikato dairy farms on fertile alluvial soils; recreation; cultural and spiritual importance to Waikato-Tainui.
- **Hazards:** flooding of the lower Waikato floodplain and the Waipā tributary — the 1958 flood was the largest recorded; bank erosion; pollution from farm runoff.
- **Management:** the Lower Waikato–Waipā flood control scheme, built after the 1958 flood — **stopbanks**, **floodgates**, **pump stations** and **ponding areas** that store floodwater on farmland; the hydro lakes are also used to hold back flood peaks.
- **Evaluation:** the scheme protects towns and farmland in normal floods, but stopbanks encourage building behind them, pass the problem downstream and can be overtopped in extreme events — the residual risk is managed by warnings and zoning.

### Flood event — Cyclone Gabrielle, New Zealand, February 2023

*Page:* [Rivers: flooding, management and fieldwork](notes/river-flooding.md)

- **When and where:** ex-tropical cyclone Gabrielle struck the northern and eastern North Island on **12–14 February 2023**; the worst flooding was in **Hawke's Bay** (Esk Valley, Heretaunga Plains) and **Tairāwhiti / Gisborne**.
- **Causes:** a deep low drew warm, very moist tropical air over the ranges, giving extreme orographic and frontal rain onto ground already saturated by the January Auckland floods; rivers such as the Esk and Tūtaekurī rose faster than their channels could carry; stopbanks were overtopped and breached; forestry slash blocked rivers and bridges.
- **Effects:** **11 deaths**, including two volunteer firefighters killed by a landslide at Muriwai; the Esk Valley buried in metres of silt; thousands displaced; Napier and Hawke's Bay lost power and communications for days; bridges and roads cut; orchards and farms destroyed; damage estimated by the government at about **NZ\$13.5 billion** (*approximate*).
- **Two weeks earlier:** the **Auckland Anniversary floods (27 January 2023)** brought about 250 mm of rain in 24 hours at Auckland Airport, a record, and 4 deaths. They showed urban flash flooding, with drains and streams overwhelmed in hours.
- **Responses:** a **national state of emergency** (14 February 2023, only the third in New Zealand's history); NZ Defence Force and civil defence rescues by helicopter; properties classified into risk **categories 1, 2 and 3**, where Category 3 land is not safe to live on and owners were offered **voluntary buy-outs**, funded jointly by central and local government; new stopbank investment; a review of forestry slash rules.
- **Evaluation:** buy-outs remove the exposure permanently but break up communities and are expensive; rebuilding stopbanks protects the plains but keeps people behind defences that failed once.

### Contrasting flood setting (LIC) — Bangladesh

*Page:* [Rivers: flooding, management and fieldwork](notes/river-flooding.md)

- **Setting:** most of Bangladesh is the delta of the **Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna**; much of it is less than 10 m above sea level; about 170 million people (*approximate*) at a density of over 1100 per km².
- **Why it floods:** monsoon rain from June to September; snowmelt from the Himalayas; deforestation upstream raising runoff; tropical cyclones and storm surges from the Bay of Bengal; rising sea level; silt raising river beds.
- **Example:** in **1998** about two-thirds of the country was flooded for weeks; over 1000 people died and many millions were made homeless (*approximate*: textbooks quote about 30 million).
- **Responses:** embankments under the Flood Action Plan; raised flood and cyclone shelters; early-warning systems; raised homes and plinths; floating schools and gardens.
- **Evaluation:** losses are greater than in New Zealand not because the hazard is bigger but because more people live on the floodplain, many are poor, and embankments are costly to build and maintain — so warnings and shelters are the affordable, life-saving measures. Climate change is turning some flood victims into **climate refugees**.

### Coast: opportunities, hazards and management — Auckland and Northland coasts

*Page:* [Coasts](notes/coasts.md)

- **Muriwai** (west coast, Auckland): a high-energy black ironsand beach facing the Tasman Sea; erosional landforms in one view — cliffs, a wave-cut platform, caves and the stack **Motutara Island** with its gannet colony. Opportunities: surfing, tourism, the gannet colony, a regional park. Hazards: cliff collapse and landslides — in Cyclone Gabrielle a slip at Muriwai killed two firefighters — and dune erosion. Management: dune planting and fencing, track closures, and managed retreat of houses from the most unstable slopes.
- **Mangawhai Heads** (Northland): a **sand spit** formed by longshore drift across the mouth of the Mangawhai Harbour, sheltering an estuary that is breeding habitat for the **tara iti / New Zealand fairy tern**, the country's rarest breeding bird (around 40 birds). A storm **breached the spit in 1978**, letting the sea pour through and the estuary silt up; the community later rebuilt the spit with a sand bund (the exact year of the closure is not certain in the course sources — check your class notes). A future breach would expose the estuary, the township and the wildlife refuge.
- **Evaluation:** hard defences protect assets but move erosion along the coast; on dynamic sand coasts working with natural processes (dune planting, letting the spit maintain itself) is cheaper and more sustainable.

### Tropical rainforest — the Amazon, South America

*Page:* [Ecosystems: tropical rainforest and hot desert](notes/ecosystems.md)

- **Location:** the Amazon basin, mostly within 10° of the equator; about 5.5 million km² of forest (*approximate*), about 60% of it in Brazil.
- **Climate:** at **Manaus**, about 26.5–28.5 °C every month (annual range about 2 °C); over 2000 mm of rain a year, with a drier season from about June to October (August about 50 mm).
- **Interactions:** constant heat and rain → rapid growth and competition for light → emergent, canopy, under-canopy, shrub and floor layers → most animals live in the canopy → rapid decomposition returns nutrients straight to the roots, leaving a leached, infertile latosol → transpiration recycles moisture into the next day's rain.
- **Deforestation:** cattle ranching is the biggest cause (most cleared land becomes pasture), followed by soya, logging, mining such as the Carajás iron-ore mine, roads such as the **Trans-Amazonian Highway** (from 1972), settlement and dams. Brazilian clearance peaked at about **27 000 km² a year in 2004**, fell to about 4500 km² by 2012 under satellite monitoring and enforcement, then rose again above 10 000 km² a year around 2019–2021 (*approximate*). Roughly a fifth of the original forest has gone. Brazilian clearance fell to about 6000 km² in 2024 (*approximate*).
- **Effects:** loss of biodiversity; soil erosion and silted rivers; less transpiration and rainfall; carbon released, adding to global warming; indigenous peoples such as the Yanomami lose land and suffer disease and violence from illegal gold miners.
- **Responses:** protected areas and indigenous reserves; satellite monitoring and fines; the Soy Moratorium (2006); the international Amazon Fund (2008). Their success has depended on how strongly each government enforced them.

### Hot desert and drought — the Sahara and the Sahel

*Page:* [Ecosystems: tropical rainforest and hot desert](notes/ecosystems.md)

- **The Sahara:** the largest hot desert, about 9 million km², roughly 15–30° N across North Africa; under the subtropical high, with dry north-east trade winds that have crossed land; summer days over 40 °C, cold nights; under 100 mm of rain over much of it; people concentrated at oases and along the Nile.
- **The Sahel** (the class case study): the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, with very variable rain (roughly 200–600 mm, *approximate*) and severe droughts in the 1970s and 1980s. **Desertification** is driven by over-cultivation to feed a rapidly growing population, cutting trees for fuel and housing, overgrazing, poor soil management and climate change. Effects: food shortages, migration for food and water, malnutrition and high infant mortality, dehydration, water-borne disease, and the loss of traditional ways of life — further south in Kenya, Maasai herders face drought and lions attacking livestock as wild prey moves away.
- **Response:** the **Great Green Wall**, launched by the African Union in **2007**, aims to restore a belt of land about 8000 km long across Africa, with a target of 100 million hectares by 2030; progress has been well behind target. Trees hold soil, shade the ground and cut evaporation.

### New Zealand's climate patterns

*Page:* [Climate and global circulation](notes/climate.md)

- About 35–47° S, about 1600 km long, surrounded by ocean, mountainous along its length.
- **Wet west, dry east:** prevailing westerlies are forced over the ranges; Milford Sound gets about 6700 mm a year, while Alexandra in Central Otago gets under 400 mm. The hot, dry Canterbury **nor'wester** is a föhn wind.
- **Sunshine:** highest in the sheltered east and north — Nelson and Blenheim over 2400 hours a year; lowest on the cloudy west coast and in the south.
- **Temperature:** falls from north to south with latitude and sharply with altitude; small seasonal ranges near the coast, larger inland (Central Otago).
- **Seasons:** summer under subtropical highs (Hadley–Ferrel boundary) → settled; winter nearer the Ferrel–Polar boundary → frontal rain.
- **Wind:** funnelled through Cook Strait and Foveaux Strait — Wellington is very windy.

## Theme 2: Population and Settlement

### Country with a high rate of natural increase: The Gambia (and Bangladesh for contrast)

*Page:* [Population structure and policies](notes/population-structure.md)

- **Where:** West Africa; the smallest country on mainland Africa (about 11 300 km²), a narrow strip either side of the Gambia River, surrounded by Senegal except for a short Atlantic coast. Capital Banjul.
- **Figures** (*approximate*):
  - about 2.4 million people at the 2024 census, up from about 1.9 million in 2013 — growth of roughly 2.5% a year, so the population doubles in under 30 years;
  - fertility about 4.5 children per woman, down from about 6 in the 1990s;
  - about 4 in 10 people are under 15; the median age is under 20;
  - over 200 people per km², one of the highest densities in Africa.
- **DTM stage:** late stage 2, moving into stage 3. The death rate has fallen (vaccination, clean water, clinics) while the birth rate is only starting to fall, so the gap — the natural increase — is wide.
- **Causes of high natural increase:**
  - children are farm labour and **old-age security** in a country with no pensions for most people;
  - **early marriage**: child marriage was banned in 2016, but many girls in rural areas still marry before 18, which lengthens their child-bearing years;
  - low use of modern contraception (under 1 in 5 married women, *approximate*) — limited supply in rural clinics, and religious and cultural expectations of large families (polygamy is common);
  - girls leave school early, so they marry and have children younger;
  - falling infant and child mortality: more babies survive, but families have not yet adjusted their size.
- **Issues — and why each is a problem:**
  - **Schools:** each year's group of children is bigger than the last, so classrooms are overcrowded and many schools run double shifts; quality falls and the next workforce is less skilled.
  - **Jobs:** the economy (groundnuts, fishing, tourism, small trade) cannot create jobs as fast as young people reach working age → youth unemployment and underemployment → frustration, and a reason to leave.
  - **Emigration — the "back way":** thousands of young Gambians have risked the Sahara and Mediterranean route to Europe; many die on the way. The country loses its young, active workers, though **remittances** (about a fifth of GDP, *approximate*) support families left behind.
  - **Urban growth:** most people now live in and around Greater Banjul (Serekunda, Brikama), which sprawls onto farmland → informal housing, flooding, poor sanitation and waste problems.
  - **Food:** the country imports most of its rice, so a growing population increases dependence on world food prices.
  - **Health and dependency:** a very young dependency ratio — few workers support many children — and high maternal mortality strain a small health budget.
  - **Population momentum:** so many girls are about to reach child-bearing age that the population will keep growing for decades even if fertility falls.
- **Attempts:**
  - a national population policy (since the 1990s) promoting family planning through health centres and community health workers, with support from the UN Population Fund;
  - school fees removed in stages in the 2010s, and campaigns to keep girls in school;
  - laws banning child marriage (2016) and female genital mutilation (2015) — Parliament rejected an attempt to overturn the FGM ban in 2024.
- **Evaluation:** fertility has fallen — the policies and rising education are working slowly — but it is still about double replacement level. Laws are hard to enforce in rural areas where custom is strong, and momentum guarantees decades more growth. Lasting change depends on girls' education, women's jobs and better survival of children, not just on supplying contraception.
- **Bangladesh, the contrast:** fertility fell from over six children per woman in the 1970s to about two by the 2020s. This came through door-to-door family-planning workers, free contraception, girls' education and women's jobs in the garment industry. Policy worked where it went together with development.

### Anti-natalist policy: China's one-child policy

*Page:* [Population structure and policies](notes/population-structure.md)

- **Dates:** one child per couple from **1979/1980 to 2015**; a two-child policy from **2016**; a three-child policy from **2021**.
- **Measures:** permits to have a child; fines and loss of benefits for extra children; rewards for one-child families; exceptions for rural families and minorities.
- **Effects:** fertility fell from nearly three children per woman at the start to well below replacement, giving hundreds of millions fewer people than otherwise.
- **Side effects:**
  - a heavily male sex ratio at birth, around 120 boys per 100 girls at its peak in the 2000s (*approximate*);
  - a rapidly ageing population and the "4-2-1" family (four grandparents, two parents, one child);
  - a shrinking workforce.
- **Evaluation:** effective at cutting births, but the side effects forced the reversal. Pro-natalist incentives since 2016 have not raised births: China's population **began to fall in 2022**, the first fall since the early 1960s.

### Country with an ageing population: Japan (with Italy and Germany)

*Page:* [Population structure and policies](notes/population-structure.md)

- **Figures (Japan):**
  - about 29% of the population aged 65 or over (2023);
  - fertility about 1.2;
  - the population peaked at about 128 million around 2008 and is now about 124 million;
  - births in 2023 were the lowest on record, about 730 000 (*approximate*).
- **Issues:** pension and healthcare costs; labour shortages; rural villages emptying; fewer taxpayers per pensioner.
- **Attempts:** subsidised childcare and parental leave since the 1990s; raising the pension age; robots and technology in care; a new work visa for foreign workers from **2019**.
- **Evaluation:** births have kept falling and immigration remains limited, so the workforce keeps shrinking.
- **Italy:** fertility about 1.2, about a quarter of people aged 65 or over, and record-low births. Baby bonuses and a universal child allowance (2022) have had little effect against youth unemployment and expensive housing.
- **Germany** (named in a school essay): turned to migration to bring in younger, economically active people; over a million asylum seekers arrived in 2015–16.
- **Greece:** fertility around 1.3–1.4 and a falling population.

### International migration flow to New Zealand: Samoa to New Zealand

*Page:* [Migration](notes/migration.md)

- **Scale:** about 180 000 people of Samoan ethnicity live in New Zealand (2018 census), close to the population of Samoa itself (about 200 000). Samoans are the largest Pacific group in New Zealand.
- **Push factors (Samoa):** few paid jobs, low wages, a small economy dependent on farming, remittances and tourism, limited higher education, cyclone and tsunami risk.
- **Pull factors (New Zealand):** jobs and higher wages, family already settled, education and healthcare, historical ties (New Zealand administered Samoa until independence in 1962).
- **Intervening obstacles and policy:** visas and cost; the **Samoan Quota** allows up to 1100 Samoan citizens a year to gain residence by ballot (since 2002); the **Recognised Seasonal Employer scheme** (2007) brings seasonal workers for horticulture.
- **Impacts on Samoa (source):** remittances are a large share of national income, about a fifth or more of GDP in many years (*approximate*); loss of young and skilled workers; families split; seasonal workers bring back savings and skills.
- **Impacts on New Zealand (destination):**
  - labour for horticulture, manufacturing and services;
  - a large Pacific community, especially in South Auckland, and cultural events such as Pasifika;
  - past discrimination: the "Dawn Raids" against Pacific overstayers in the 1970s, for which the government formally apologised in 2021.

### International refugee flow: Syria

*Page:* [Migration](notes/migration.md)

- **Cause:** civil war from **2011**, with the bombing of cities, persecution and the collapse of services.
- **Scale:** at the peak over 6 million Syrians were refugees abroad and a similar number were displaced inside Syria, about half the pre-war population forced from home.
- **Destinations:**
  - Turkey, with over 3 million at the peak;
  - Lebanon, where refugees made up roughly a quarter of the population;
  - Jordan;
  - Europe: over a million asylum seekers reached Europe in 2015–16, many settling in **Germany**.
- **Impacts on the source:** loss of doctors, teachers and young workers; the economy and infrastructure destroyed; families divided; the population fell sharply. Some refugees have returned since the fall of the Assad government in December 2024.
- **Impacts on destinations:** pressure on housing, schools, water and jobs in Lebanon and Jordan; camps such as Zaatari in Jordan; in Germany, workers for an ageing labour force, but integration costs and a political backlash.

### Areas of high and low population density

*Page:* [Population density and distribution](notes/population-density.md)

- **High: Bangladesh and the Ganges delta.** Over 1100 people per km². Flat, fertile alluvium renewed by floods; abundant water; monsoon rice farming with several harvests a year.
- **High: Auckland.** About a third of New Zealand's population. Harbours, port, airport, mild climate, jobs and services.
- **Low: the Sahara.** Under 1 person per km² across most of it. Aridity, heat, no surface water, poor soils.
- **Low: New Zealand's West Coast.** Only a few people per km² (*approximate*). Steep mountains, very high rainfall, isolation behind the Southern Alps, decline of mining.

### New Zealand's urban hierarchy

*Page:* [Settlement and service provision](notes/settlement-services.md)

The hierarchy runs from Auckland down to small service towns:
1. **Auckland:** dominant in population, industry, income, health and education; two harbours, a warm climate, the main entry point.
2. **Wellington:** the seat of government (capital since 1865), central, with a sheltered harbour.
3. **Christchurch:** dominates the South Island; the Canterbury Plains; the port at Lyttelton.
4. **Hamilton and Dunedin:** Hamilton is the Waikato service centre and has overtaken Dunedin; Dunedin grew with the 1860s gold rush, its port and its university.
5. Regional centres such as Tauranga and Palmerston North.
6. District towns.
7. Small service towns.

### HIC urban area: Auckland

*Pages:* [Urban change and issues in HICs](notes/urban-change-hics.md), [Urban land use and models](notes/urban-land-use.md)

- **Site and situation:** on a narrow isthmus between the Waitematā and Manukau harbours, just over a kilometre wide at Ōtāhuhu, so growth spreads north, west and south. It is New Zealand's main port, international airport and economic centre.
- **Growth over time:**
  - 1840: capital site chosen by Hobson.
  - 1840s: fencible villages at Howick, Panmure and Onehunga.
  - About 30 000 people in 1872 and about 220 000 by 1930.
  - 1885–1914: port-led boom.
  - Tram and rail suburbs.
  - 1959: the Harbour Bridge, then car-based sprawl.
  - Since the 1970s: about 70% of growth by intensification.
- **Population:** about 1.66 million at the 2023 census, about a third of New Zealand's population, and forecast to keep growing.
- **Problems:**
  - urban sprawl onto farmland, including Pukekohe's market gardens;
  - long commutes and traffic congestion on a few motorway corridors;
  - very high house prices;
  - costly water, wastewater and transport infrastructure;
  - flooding of the built-up area on **27 January 2023**.
- **Solutions:**
  - the **Auckland Unitary Plan** (2016), with a **Rural Urban Boundary** and intensification around town centres and rapid-transit corridors (centres-based approach), followed by Plan Change 120 (2025);
  - infill at Stonefields and the Tāmaki regeneration of state housing (from 2015);
  - the **Northern Busway** (2008);
  - the **Western Ring Route**, completed when the Waterview tunnels opened in **2017**;
  - the **City Rail Link**, a 3.45 km twin-tunnel line under the CBD; its opening on 13 September 2026 was announced in August 2026;
  - the Central Interceptor wastewater tunnel; electric trains; new cycleways.
- **Evaluation:** intensification uses land efficiently and supports public transport, but strains school rolls and local services and changes neighbourhood character. Motorway building eases congestion only until new traffic fills it.

### HIC inner-city problem area: London Docklands

*Page:* [Urban change and issues in HICs](notes/urban-change-hics.md)

- **Problem:** London's docks closed between the late 1960s and 1981 as container ships moved to deep-water ports downstream. The area was left with derelict land, unemployment and poor housing.
- **Response:** the **London Docklands Development Corporation (1981–1998)** used public money to attract private investment: offices at **Canary Wharf**, the **Docklands Light Railway** (opened 1987), London City Airport and the Jubilee Line extension.
- **Outcome:** about 24 000 new homes; jobs rose from about 27 000 to over 80 000; the population roughly doubled (*approximate figures from the corporation's final report*).
- **Evaluation:** a physical and economic success, but many new jobs went to commuters rather than former dockworkers, and rising house prices displaced long-standing residents (gentrification).

### LIC/MIC squatter settlement (school case study 1): Dharavi, Mumbai, India

*Page:* [Urbanisation in LICs and MICs](notes/urbanisation-lics.md)

Learn the **ideas**: where it is, why it grew, what goes wrong, what has been tried and whether it works. One or two anchor facts are enough to make the example "developed".

- **Location — the key idea:** a **central** slum. It sits on swampy, once-unwanted land between railway lines in the middle of Mumbai, next to a modern business district, so it is now some of the most valuable land in the city. That is why developers want it.
- **Why it grew:** Mumbai is India's richest city, so it pulls migrants from poor rural states (push: drought, debt, tiny farms; pull: jobs, wages, hope). Formal housing is far too expensive, so newcomers built wherever they could. Dirty industries (tanning, pottery) pushed out of the old centre settled there too. It has grown for over a century and is one of Asia's largest slums.
- **Anchor fact:** perhaps up to **a million people on about 2 km²**, one of the densest places on Earth (estimates vary; there is no reliable census).
- **Problems — and why they matter:**
  - **Overcrowding:** families live in one small room, often above or below a workshop → disease spreads fast, no privacy, no space to study.
  - **Sanitation and water:** shared toilets and standpipes, open drains → water-borne disease (diarrhoea, typhoid) and hours lost queuing, which falls mostly on women and children.
  - **Hazards:** low, flood-prone land, flammable materials and tangled wiring → floods and fires, and narrow lanes block fire engines and ambulances.
  - **Pollution:** tanning, recycling and burning waste → polluted air and creek, respiratory illness.
  - **No legal title:** residents can be evicted, so they hesitate to invest in better homes, and they struggle to get loans or services.
- **Strengths — why it is not only a problem:**
  - a huge **informal economy** (leather, pottery, garments, food and recycling) that employs most residents and sells across India and abroad;
  - strong, mixed communities and self-help groups, such as women's savings groups working with NGOs to build community toilet blocks;
  - homes double as workplaces, so people live next to their income.
- **Solutions tried:**
  - **Slum rehabilitation:** developers build free flats for residents and pay for it by selling extra flats on the freed land.
  - **Large-scale redevelopment:** repeated plans since 2004 to clear the area and rehouse people in tower blocks; the current scheme is led by the **Adani Group** (tender won 2022). Only long-term residents qualify for a free flat inside Dharavi; later arrivals are offered housing outside it.
  - **Community-led upgrading:** NGOs and residents improving toilets, water and lanes in place.
- **Evaluation:**
  - *For redevelopment:* safe flats with water and toilets; less fire and flood risk; schools and roads.
  - *Against:* many residents do not qualify and are moved far from their jobs; tower blocks do not suit home workshops, so the informal economy can be destroyed; the developer profits most from prime land; residents have protested.
  - *Judgement:* success should be measured by how many original residents and businesses stay. In-situ upgrading with residents is cheaper and keeps livelihoods.

### LIC/MIC squatter settlement (school case study 2): Diepsloot, Johannesburg, South Africa

*Page:* [Urbanisation in LICs and MICs](notes/urbanisation-lics.md)

- **Location — the key idea:** a **peripheral** slum on the northern edge of Johannesburg, far from the city centre, right beside wealthy gated estates: a sharp picture of inequality.
- **Why it grew:** it began in **1995** as a "temporary" transit camp for people moved from other informal settlements and flood-prone riverbanks. The temporary residents stayed, and migrants kept arriving from poor rural provinces and neighbouring countries (Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Malawi), pulled by Gauteng's jobs. Apartheid had kept Black South Africans out of the cities; when those controls ended, migration grew far faster than housing.
- **Anchor fact:** from a camp to a township of a few hundred thousand people in about 30 years.
- **Problems — and why they matter:**
  - **Housing:** many live in self-built shacks of corrugated iron and wood → cold, crowded, and easily burned by paraffin stoves.
  - **Services:** many homes lack piped water, a flush toilet or a legal power connection → disease, illegal wiring and fires; unpaved roads flood in summer storms.
  - **Jobs:** high unemployment, and the edge location means long, expensive journeys to work → poverty is hard to escape.
  - **Crime and policing:** few police → high crime and "mob justice"; tension with foreign migrants has led to xenophobic violence.
  - **Health and social issues:** HIV, teenage pregnancy and substance abuse.
- **Strengths:** a busy informal economy of small shops (spaza shops), taverns, salons and street food; community and NGO projects such as food gardens and youth programmes.
- **Solutions tried:**
  - **RDP houses**, free basic government homes (since 1994);
  - **site-and-service** plots with water and toilets on which residents build;
  - **self-help** housing, where residents add their own labour to a subsidy;
  - a planned mixed-income **new town** nearby (Cosmo City) for some residents;
  - NGO projects for jobs, recycling and small businesses.
- **Evaluation:**
  - Government houses have given many families a proper home, but supply never catches up with new arrivals, so shacks keep spreading.
  - Housing poor people on the city edge, far from jobs, repeats the apartheid pattern.
  - Services are improving but patchy, and crime and xenophobia scare off investment.
  - *Judgement:* upgrading in place (secure tenure, water, sanitation, lighting, policing) plus local jobs would help more than moving people.

**Dharavi vs Diepsloot, the comparison examiners reward:** central vs peripheral; old vs new; a productive economy vs high unemployment; the threat of redevelopment vs a backlog of services and crime; developer-led towers vs state houses and serviced plots.

### Supporting LIC/MIC example: Rio de Janeiro's favelas, Brazil

*Page:* [Urbanisation in LICs and MICs](notes/urbanisation-lics.md)

- **Why they grew:** migrants from Brazil's poor, drought-prone north-east, where land is held in large estates and farming is mechanising, plus natural increase among young migrants.
- **Where:** on **steep hillsides** close to the centre and the rich beach suburbs (land too steep for developers but close to jobs) and on flood-prone land. More than a fifth of the city's people live in favelas; **Rocinha** is the largest.
- **Problems:** landslides after heavy rain; steep, narrow alleys that vehicles, pipes and sewers cannot reach; poor sanitation; illegal power connections; drug-gang violence and violent police raids.
- **Strengths:** strong communities and many informal businesses.
- **Solutions:** clearance to distant estates (unpopular, far from jobs) → **Favela-Bairro** upgrading in place from 1994 (paved paths, drains, water, sewerage, crèches) → police "pacification" units from 2008 → legalising electricity.
- **Evaluation:** upgrading in place has been cheaper and more popular than clearance. Pacification reduced violence at first but did not last.

### Primary sector — New Zealand dairy farming

*Page:* [Farming and food shortages](notes/farming.md)

The primary-sector case study, learned in the same depth as Wolfsburg (secondary) and Vietnam (tertiary). It also fills farming as a system, farm location factors, intensive farming, environmental impacts of farming and "is dairy farming sustainable?".

**Scale and trend**
- About **4.7 million** dairy cows (2023/24), an average herd of about **445**, producing about **400 kg of milksolids per cow** a season.
- Cow numbers roughly **doubled from about 2.4 million in 1990 to a peak of about 5 million in the mid-2010s** as sheep farms were converted to dairy, then began falling slowly (*approximate*).
- **Where:** the **Waikato** is the heartland (about 1.1 million cows); **Taranaki**; and, since the 1990s, a big expansion into **Southland** and irrigated **Canterbury**.
- **Value:** NZ's largest goods export, about **one dollar in three** of goods export earnings; the farmgate price reached a record **NZ\$10.16 per kg of milksolids in 2024/25**, but fell as low as about NZ\$3.90 in 2015/16, so incomes swing widely.

**Why dairy farms are where they are**
- **Physical:**
  - mild, moist temperate climate with few frosts → **grass grows almost all year**, so cows graze outdoors and need little housing or bought feed, keeping costs low;
  - reliable, well-spread rainfall (about 1100–1300 mm in the Waikato), or **irrigation** from rivers and aquifers on the dry Canterbury Plains;
  - flat to gently rolling land, safe for heavy cows and easy for machinery;
  - fertile, free-draining **volcanic soils** in the Waikato and on the Taranaki ring plain; alluvial soils in Canterbury.
- **Human:**
  - **processing plants within tanker range**, because milk is perishable and collected every day (Fonterra's Te Rapa near Hamilton, Edendale in Southland) — the reason dairy clusters in a few regions;
  - good rural roads and export ports;
  - **Fonterra**, a farmer-owned co-operative that by law must accept milk from any farmer who wants to supply it, so farmers always have a buyer;
  - technology: rotary milking sheds, herd testing, artificial insemination and breeding (LIC), farm software;
  - capital and bank loans (land is very expensive), and **sharemilking**, a route from worker to farm owner;
  - government: no farm subsidies since the 1980s reforms, so farms must be efficient; the 1990s boom in irrigation schemes opened Canterbury.

**The farm as a system**
- **Inputs:** physical — land, grass, rain or irrigation water, soil, sunshine; human — cows, labour (owner, sharemilker, farm workers, many of them migrants), fertiliser (especially nitrogen), supplementary feed (silage, maize, palm kernel), electricity, fuel, machinery, the milking shed, vet and advisory services, capital.
- **Processes:**
  - **rotational grazing** — cows move to a fresh paddock each day or half-day so the grass regrows;
  - milking **twice a day** in a rotary or herringbone shed; milk chilled in a vat until the tanker comes;
  - a **seasonal cycle** timed so that peak grass growth matches peak milk demand:
    - *winter (June–July):* cows dried off and fed on crops or silage;
    - *spring (July–September):* calving;
    - *late spring (October–December):* the "spring flush" of grass and milk, and mating by artificial insemination;
    - *summer:* irrigation where available, extra feed in dry spells;
    - *autumn (April–May):* cows dried off;
  - making silage, applying fertiliser, spreading effluent, maintaining fences and races.
- **Outputs:** products — milk (paid by kg of milksolids), surplus calves and cull cows for meat; waste — effluent, nitrate and phosphate, sediment, methane and nitrous oxide; money — the milk cheque.
- **Feedback:** the milk price sets next season's decisions. A high price → more cows, more fertiliser and feed, perhaps a new shed or irrigation; a low price → fewer inputs, debt and sometimes farm sales.

**Intensive pastoral farming — and how it has intensified**
- Dairy is **intensive**: high inputs of capital and labour per hectare give high output per hectare.
- It is mostly **pasture-based** (grass grazed in the paddock), the low-cost system that makes NZ milk competitive worldwide.
- Since the 1990s farms have **intensified**:
  - more cows per hectare;
  - more nitrogen fertiliser to grow more grass;
  - more irrigation;
  - more imported feed — palm kernel from South-East Asia is linked to rainforest clearance.
- Intensification raised output and profit, but it is also the main reason the environmental pressure rose.

**Effects — why dairy farming is controversial**
- **Economic (positive):** export earnings that pay for the country's imports; jobs on farms and in processing, transport, vets, machinery and banking; service towns kept alive (Morrinsville, Te Awamutu); the multiplier effect through rural economies.
- **Economic (negative):** dependence on one product and on world prices (and on China), so a price fall hits the whole economy; high farm debt; land prices too high for young farmers.
- **Social:** reliance on migrant workers; long hours and isolation (farmer stress and mental health); farm amalgamation means fewer, larger farms; tension between farmers and the urban public over "dirty dairying".
- **Environmental (the main problem), as causal chains:**
  - **Nitrate:** a cow's urine patch holds far more nitrogen than grass can use, and fertiliser adds more → nitrate leaches through the soil into groundwater and streams → **eutrophication** (algal blooms, low oxygen, fish deaths) in rivers and lakes; nitrate in drinking-water wells in Canterbury is a health concern.
  - **Effluent and E. coli:** dung washed off sheds, races and paddocks → bacteria in rivers → many lowland rivers become unsafe for swimming.
  - **Sediment and phosphate:** cows trampling stream banks and **pugging** wet paddocks → soil washes into waterways → muddy rivers and smothered stream life; winter grazing of crops on bare, wet ground makes this worse.
  - **Water takes:** irrigation draws down Canterbury's rivers and aquifers → lower flows for ecosystems, other farmers and towns.
  - **Climate:** methane from cows' digestion and nitrous oxide from urine and fertiliser → agriculture produces about **half of NZ's greenhouse emissions**, and dairy is the biggest single part.
  - **Biodiversity:** wetlands drained and native bush cleared for pasture.

**Management — and how well it has worked**
- **Voluntary industry action:** after Fish & Game's "Dirty Dairying" campaign (2002), the Dairying and Clean Streams Accord (2003) and the Sustainable Dairying: Water Accord (2013) committed farmers to **fence stock out of waterways**, build bridges and culverts at crossings and manage effluent. Most large waterways on dairy farms are now fenced.
- **On-farm practices:** riparian fencing and planting; effluent ponds and spreading only when the soil can absorb it; **nutrient budgets** and precision fertiliser; lower stocking rates; stand-off pads and wintering barns; retiring steep or wet land; better feed and breeding to cut emissions.
- **Regulation:** regional councils set limits on nitrogen loss and water takes (a nitrogen cap and trading scheme around Lake Taupō; nutrient limits in Canterbury); national freshwater rules on stock exclusion and winter grazing. Pricing of farm greenhouse emissions has been proposed and repeatedly delayed.
- **Market pressure:** overseas buyers want low-carbon, "sustainable" milk, so Fonterra pays farmers incentives for good environmental practice.
- **Evaluation:**
  - Fencing and effluent rules have reduced direct pollution, but nitrate leaching from urine patches is hard to stop without **fewer cows per hectare**, and nitrate already in groundwater can take decades to reach rivers.
  - Emissions are barely falling.
  - *Long-term sustainability:* economically strong but exposed to price swings; environmentally sustainable only if stocking rates match what each catchment's water can cope with. The slow fall in cow numbers since the mid-2010s suggests the industry is moving that way, but the pace depends on enforced rules, not voluntary action.

### Manufacturing factory (2026 school case study) — Volkswagen, Wolfsburg, Germany

*Page:* [Manufacturing industry: VW Wolfsburg](notes/manufacturing.md)

- **Location:** Wolfsburg, Lower Saxony, northern Germany, on the **Mittelland Canal** between Hanover and Magdeburg; beside the Berlin–Hanover railway and the A2/A39 autobahns. VW's headquarters and main plant, described by VW as the **world's largest single car manufacturing complex**.
- **History:** founded in **1938** by the Nazi government to build the "people's car" (the Beetle), with a new town for the workers; in the war it made military vehicles, largely with forced labour; run by the British military 1945–1949, who restarted Beetle production in 1945; one-millionth Beetle **1955**; the **Golf** replaced the Beetle in **1974**.
- **Size (VW fact sheet, March 2024):** site about **6.5 km²** with **1.6 km²** of factory halls, 75 km of roads and 60 km of rail track; about **70 000 employees** at the site (about 11 500 in technical development); about **490 000 vehicles in 2023** — Golf, Golf Variant, Tiguan and Touran.
- **Location factors:** political (chosen by the state in 1938; the state of Lower Saxony holds 20% of VW's voting rights); transport (canal, rail, autobahns, central position in Europe); a huge flat site; the German and EU market; a large skilled workforce (Italian guest workers filled shortages in the 1960s); on-site power; agglomeration and inertia (suppliers, research, headquarters).
- **System:** inputs (steel, aluminium, plastics, glass, components delivered just-in-time, energy from two on-site combined heat and power plants, labour, robots) → processes (press shop, body shop with welding robots, paint shop, assembly, testing) → outputs (cars; CO₂, solvents, scrap metal, waste water; profit) → feedback (profit reinvested; falling demand cuts output).
- **Autostadt:** a customer delivery centre and theme park beside the plant, opened in **2000**; buyers collect cars from two 48 m glass **car towers** each holding 400 cars; about two million visitors a year (*approximate*, older figure).
- **Globalisation and change:** the VW Group sold about **9 million** vehicles in 2024 from more than 100 plants worldwide. The **December 2024** agreement with unions: more than **35 000 German jobs** to go by 2030 through early retirement and buy-outs (no forced redundancies); Wolfsburg cut from four assembly lines to two; **Golf production moves to Puebla, Mexico from 2027**; electric models (ID.3, Cupra Born, later an electric Golf) come to Wolfsburg.
- **Environmental impacts and responses:** emissions, energy and water use, traffic; the two power plants converted from **hard coal to natural gas** (about €400 million), cutting CO₂ by about 60% (about 1.5 million tonnes a year); the **2015 "Dieselgate"** scandal — software that cheated emissions tests in about 11 million diesel cars worldwide, costing VW over €30 billion.
- **Evaluation:** Wolfsburg is one of Germany's richest cities per head, but it is a **company town**: VW's restructuring shows how dependence on one employer spreads job losses through the multiplier effect.

### Supporting NZ manufacturing example — New Zealand Steel, Glenbrook

*Page:* [Manufacturing industry: VW Wolfsburg](notes/manufacturing.md)

- **Location:** Glenbrook, south of Auckland; New Zealand's only integrated steel mill, opened in the late 1960s.
- **Location factors:** raw material — **ironsand** mined on the Waikato coast at Waikato North Head and piped to the mill as a slurry; coal from the Waikato; flat land beside the Manukau Harbour; rail and port links; the Auckland market.
- **System:** inputs (ironsand, coal, limestone, electricity, water, labour) → processes (reducing the ironsand to iron, making and rolling steel) → outputs (steel coil, sheet and roofing products; slag, CO₂, heat, waste water).
- **Environmental threat:** one of New Zealand's largest single sources of carbon dioxide, because coal is used to strip the oxygen from the ironsand.
- **Response:** in **2023** the company and the government agreed to build an **electric arc furnace** that recycles scrap steel, with the government contributing up to NZ\$140 million, to cut emissions by about 800 000 tonnes of CO₂ a year (about 1% of New Zealand's emissions).

### Area where tourism is important (2026 school case study) — Vietnam

*Page:* [Tourism: Vietnam](notes/tourism.md)

- **Growth:** international arrivals about **18 million in 2019** (pre-pandemic record) → collapse in 2020–2021 (borders closed) → reopened March 2022 (about 3.7 million) → about 17.6 million in 2024 → about **21 million in 2025**, a new record up about 20% (Vietnam National Authority of Tourism reports 21.2 million). Target: 25 million in 2026.
- **Value:** 2025 tourism revenue about VND 1 quadrillion (roughly **US\$38 billion**), about **8–9% of GDP** (*approximate*, Vietnam News).
- **Source markets (2025):** China (about 5.3 million), South Korea (about 4.3 million), then Taiwan, the USA and Japan; Asia supplies about four-fifths.
- **Why it grew (causes):**
  - *Political change:* the **Đổi Mới** reforms (from 1986) opened a closed, war-damaged communist economy to foreign investment and visitors → hotels, airlines and tour firms could be set up → tourism became a government priority.
  - *Natural attractions:* limestone karst seascapes (Hạ Long), huge caves (Phong Nha), mountains and rice terraces (Sa Pa), long tropical beaches, the Mekong Delta.
  - *Cultural attractions:* ancient towns (Hội An), imperial cities (Huế), temples, food, minority cultures and war history; several UNESCO World Heritage sites act as "brands" that attract visitors.
  - *Cost and safety:* cheap food, hotels and transport, and a reputation for political stability and personal safety → attractive to backpackers and families alike.
  - *Access:* budget airlines (VietJet) and new airports cut fares; Vietnam is a few hours' flight from China, South Korea and Japan, whose rising middle classes now travel abroad; 90-day e-visas for all nationalities (August 2023) and visa-free entry for many countries removed a barrier.
  - *Marketing and media:* government campaigns, social media and films (*Kong: Skull Island*, 2017, shot at Hạ Long and Tràng An).
  - *Recovery after COVID-19:* pent-up demand from Asia once borders reopened in 2022 drove record numbers by 2025.
- **Key areas:** **Hạ Long Bay** (about 1600 limestone islands; UNESCO 1994, extended to Cát Bà 2023); **Hội An** (old trading port; UNESCO 1999; entry tickets; regular floods); **Sa Pa** (rice terraces, Hmong and Red Dao villages; Fansipan, 3143 m, cable car since 2016); **Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng** (UNESCO 2003; Sơn Đoòng, the world's largest cave passage, strictly limited tours); **Huế** (Imperial City, UNESCO 1993); **Tràng An** (UNESCO 2014); **Phú Quốc** (island resorts).
- **Effects — and why each one matters:**
  - **Economic, positive:**
    - *millions of jobs* in hotels, restaurants, guiding, transport and crafts, many needing little training → work for rural people, women and ethnic minorities with few other options;
    - *foreign currency* → helps pay for imports and development;
    - the *multiplier effect* → farmers, builders and craft-makers sell to the tourist trade;
    - *infrastructure* (airports, roads, power, water) that residents use too.
  - **Economic, negative:**
    - *Leakage:* much of the spending goes to foreign-owned hotels, airlines and tour companies, or to a few big Vietnamese conglomerates (Sun Group built the Fansipan cable car) → little of the money reaches local families, so tourism does less to reduce poverty than the headline figures suggest.
    - *Low-paid, seasonal, insecure work* → no income in the off-season, and no safety net when visitors stop coming. When borders shut in 2020–2021, arrivals fell to almost nothing and huge numbers of tourism workers lost their income overnight.
    - *Dependence on a few markets:* China and South Korea supply nearly half of visitors → a political dispute, economic slowdown or new pandemic in those countries hits Vietnam directly.
    - *Rising land, rent and food prices* in resort areas → locals priced out; farming and fishing families in resort areas such as Phú Quốc have lost land to resort projects.
  - **Social and cultural:**
    - *Benefits:* income for minority families through homestays; revival of crafts such as Hội An lanterns and Sa Pa textiles; better services.
    - *Commercialisation of culture* in Sa Pa: Hmong and Red Dao traditions are performed and sold to tourists ("staged authenticity") → culture becomes a product and its meaning is lost.
    - *Child street-sellers* in Sa Pa sell souvenirs instead of attending school → they miss education, which keeps them in poverty (the cycle of poverty).
    - *Overcrowding:* Hội An's narrow streets are packed at peak times → residents move out and the old town turns into shops and cafés for visitors — a "museum town" losing the community that made it attractive.
    - Traditional ways of life change faster than communities choose, and resentment can build towards visitors.
  - **Environmental:**
    - *Hạ Long Bay:* waste, sewage and fuel from hundreds of tour boats, hotels and the growing city of Hạ Long, plus plastic litter → poorer water quality, algae and damaged coral and mangroves → fewer fish for local fishing families, and a dirtier bay that is less attractive to the visitors it depends on. Nearby coal mining, a port and land reclamation add to the pollution, so tourism is not the only cause.
    - *Sa Pa:* hotels built on steep hillsides and terraces → forest and terraces lost, slopes destabilised → **landslide** risk in heavy monsoon and typhoon rain (Typhoon Yagi in September 2024 caused deadly floods and landslides in Lào Cai province, where Sa Pa is); the rice-terrace landscape that tourists come to see is being built over; water supply is strained.
    - *Phú Quốc:* forest cleared and coral reefs damaged for resorts → lost habitat; dry-season water shortages as hotels compete with residents.
    - *Coasts:* beach resorts built close to the sea face erosion (the beach at Cửa Đại near Hội An has been badly eroded).
    - *Climate:* emissions from long-haul and regional flights add to global warming, which in turn threatens Vietnam's low-lying coasts and the Mekong Delta.
  - **The big idea:** unmanaged tourism can **destroy the attractions it depends on** — dirtier water at Hạ Long, a built-over Sa Pa, a crowded Hội An — pushing a destination towards Butler's stagnation and decline.
- **Who gains and who loses:** large firms, national government revenue and well-placed hotel and homestay owners gain most; fishing families, poorer residents priced out, minority children kept out of school and future generations who inherit a damaged environment lose most.
- **Management:** limits on licensed tour boats and routes and a ban on single-use plastic on Hạ Long tour boats and sites (from 1 September 2019); Hội An ticketing (from 1995, reintroduced 2022, tightened for tour groups 2024) funding restoration, and pedestrian streets; caps and licensed guides for Sơn Đoòng (high-value ecotourism); community-based homestays in Sa Pa; a national "green tourism" strategy to 2030.
- **Evaluation:** numbers keep breaking records, so pressure is rising faster than management; caps and tickets work best at small sites; Hạ Long's pollution is not only from tourism.
- **On Butler's model:** different parts of Vietnam sit at different stages. Hạ Long Bay is near **consolidation or stagnation** (carrying capacity under strain); Sa Pa and Phú Quốc are in rapid **development** (outside firms, fast building); Sơn Đoòng is deliberately held near **involvement** by strict caps.

- **Attractions:** natural — Lake Wakatipu, the Remarkables, ski fields (Coronet Peak, The Remarkables, Cardrona), day trips to Milford Sound; adventure — the world's first commercial bungy jump at the Kawarau Bridge (**1988**), jet-boating, skydiving.

### Energy supply in a country — New Zealand (plus the Three Gorges Dam)

*Page:* [Energy resources](notes/energy.md)

- **Electricity 2023:** about 43 500 GWh — hydro 60%, geothermal 18%, gas 9%, wind 7%, coal 2%; **88% renewable**.
- **All energy:** electricity is only about a quarter of energy use; oil supplies about half of the energy consumers use, and 73% of oil goes on transport.
- **Major plants:** Manapōuri hydro (850 MW, the largest); the Waitaki and Waikato hydro chains; geothermal stations in the Taupō Volcanic Zone; Huntly (954 MW, coal and gas) as dry-year back-up; Taranaki gas stations.
- **Why:** high rainfall and relief for hydro; volcanic heat; westerly winds; Taranaki gas; but no domestic oil refining since Marsden Point closed in 2022, so transport fuel is imported.
- **Plan:** most planned new capacity is wind and solar; the challenge is electrifying transport and industrial heat, and covering dry years.
- **Three Gorges Dam, China:** on the Yangtze; the world's largest hydroelectric station at 22 500 MW; over 2 km wide; fully operating from 2012. Benefits: huge renewable supply, flood control, river navigation. Costs: over a million people relocated, towns such as Fengdu drowned, sediment trapped, ecosystems altered — and coal still dominates China's energy.

### Area at heightened risk from global warming (school slides) — Kiribati

*Page:* [Global warming and climate change](notes/global-warming.md)

- **Geography:** 32 low-lying coral **atolls** (plus Banaba, a raised coral island once mined for phosphate) spread across the central Pacific, roughly halfway between Hawaii and Australia. An atoll is a ring of coral reef built around a sinking volcanic island.
- **People:** over **119 000** (2020 census); more than half live on **(South) Tarawa**, only about **3 m above sea level**.
- **Threats:** sea-level rise (melting ice plus thermal expansion) → king-tide and storm flooding, coastal erosion, salt water in the freshwater lens and crops; **ocean acidification** reduces the calcium carbonate corals need → weaker reefs and reef collapse → less storm protection; fewer reef fish and shellfish → food insecurity for subsistence fishers; losses for fishing and small-scale tourism; reefs' cultural and spiritual value threatened.
- **Responses:** sea walls and mangrove planting; rainwater tanks; the government bought about **20 km² of land in Fiji (2014)** as a possible refuge and for food security; "migration with dignity" training; New Zealand's **Pacific Access Category** allows up to **75** people from Kiribati to migrate each year; aid and climate finance.
- **Evaluation:** adaptation buys time, but Kiribati cannot stop global emissions — a climate-justice case: it has contributed almost nothing to the problem.

### Supporting examples of areas at risk — Tuvalu (sea-level rise) and Delhi (smog)

*Page:* [Global warming and climate change](notes/global-warming.md)

- **Tuvalu:** a Pacific nation of nine low coral islands and atolls, about 11 000 people, with most land less than about 2 m above sea level. Risks: flooding at king tides, salt water contaminating the freshwater lens and pulaka (taro) pits, coastal erosion, stronger storms. Responses: land reclamation and sea walls; the **Falepili Union** treaty with Australia (2023), allowing up to 280 Tuvaluans a year to migrate; a campaign to keep its statehood and maritime boundaries if the land is lost.
- **Delhi (smog):** each winter vehicle exhaust, industry, construction dust and the burning of crop stubble in Punjab and Haryana are trapped under **temperature inversions**, pushing air quality into the "severe" range; responses include odd–even car rationing (2016), bans on construction and older vehicles during emergencies, and a switch of buses to compressed natural gas.

### Water supply — Auckland (resources and environmental risk)

*Page:* [Water and the environmental risks of development](notes/environmental-risks.md)

- **Sources:** dams in the Waitākere and Hūnua Ranges, groundwater (Onehunga aquifer) and the **Waikato River** (treated near Tuakau).
- **2020 drought:** the driest start to a year on record dropped dam storage below half; Watercare imposed **water restrictions from May 2020** (no hoses or sprinklers) and fast-tracked extra treatment capacity taking more water from the Waikato (*approximate*; check exact percentages before quoting).
- **Issues:** population growth, more frequent drought with climate change, competition with farms and other users for Waikato water, and iwi interests in the river.
- **Supporting example — Canterbury aquifers:** irrigation for dairying has drawn down rivers and aquifers, and nitrate has leached into groundwater used for drinking; responses include consent limits on water takes and nitrogen caps.
