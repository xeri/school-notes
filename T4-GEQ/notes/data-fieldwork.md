## Choosing and reading graphs

| Type | Best for | Reading tip |
|---|---|---|
| **Line graph** | continuous change over time (population, temperature, discharge) | describe the overall trend, then the rate of change, then anomalies, with figures and dates |
| **Compound line graph** | several components of one total over time (birth rate, death rate and natural increase) | read the gap between lines |
| **Bar graph** | comparing separate categories | compare the largest and smallest, and quote both |
| **Divided (compound) bar** | the composition of a total, compared between places | read the segment length, not its end position |
| **Histogram** | a continuous variable grouped into classes | the bars touch; the area matters |
| **Pictogram** | a simple comparison for a general audience | check what one symbol represents |
| **Pie graph** | the proportions of a single whole | 1% = 3.6°, so a quarter is 90°; start at 12 o'clock and go clockwise, largest first |
| **Scatter graph** | the relationship between two variables | describe the strength and direction (positive or negative), add a best-fit line, identify anomalies; correlation does not prove cause |
| **Dispersion graph** | the spread of a set of values | shows clustering, the median and the quartiles |
| **Triangular graph** | three components that total 100% (employment by sector, soil texture) | read each axis in the direction its own labels run; the three values must add up to 100 |
| **Population pyramid** | age and sex structure | shape, base, apex, sides, and one quoted figure |
| **Climate graph** | monthly temperature (line) and rainfall (bars) | quote the maximum, the minimum, the annual range and the total rainfall |
| **Hydrograph** | river discharge after a storm | peak discharge, lag time, the rising and falling limbs |
| **Wind rose** | the frequency and strength of wind by direction | the longest arm is the prevailing wind |
| **Radial graph** | data with a directional or cyclical dimension | read outwards from the centre |
| **Kite diagram** | species abundance along a transect | symmetrical about the transect line |

**Plotting.** Use the axes and scale provided, plot accurately (a mark is lost for being half a square out), and complete the bar or point in the same style as the existing ones.

**Suggesting a graph.** Name the type *and* say why it suits the data:
- three components totalling 100%: a triangular graph;
- a relationship between two variables: a scatter graph;
- proportions of one total: a pie graph;
- change over time: a line graph;
- rates by region: a choropleth map;
- movement between places: flow lines.

## Maps that show data

| Map | Use | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Choropleth** | rates or densities by area, shaded darker for higher values | hides variation *inside* each area; sharp jumps at the boundaries |
| **Isoline** | a continuous surface such as rainfall, pressure (isobars), height (contours) or travel time (isochrones) | close lines mean a steep gradient |
| **Dot map** | distribution: one dot = a fixed number | dots can merge in dense areas |
| **Proportional symbol** | a quantity at points (city populations) | symbol **area**, not diameter, is usually proportional |
| **Flow line** | movement between places (migration, traffic) | line width is proportional to volume |
| **Desire line** | the journeys of individuals (shoppers' origins) | shows the sphere of influence |
| **Synoptic chart** | weather: isobars, fronts, wind | see the weather notes |
| **Sketch map / annotated map** | one pattern, simplified | needs a key, north arrow and scale |

## Describing trends and patterns

1. **State the pattern**: overall trend or distribution.
2. **Quote figures** with units and dates. Use paired data at the same point ("190 vehicles at 07:30 in 2016 compared with 67 in 2018").
3. **Note anomalies** and changes in the rate.
4. **Compare** with comparative words: *higher than*, *whereas*, *both*, *by contrast*.

"Using Fig. 1 only" means the evidence must come from the resource; general knowledge is not credited.

## Calculations

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Population density | $\dfrac{\text{population}}{\text{area in km}^2}$ |
| Percentage | $\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}}\times100$ |
| Percentage change | $\dfrac{\text{new}-\text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100$ |
| Rate per 1000 | $\dfrac{\text{events}}{\text{population}}\times1000$ |
| Natural increase | $\text{birth rate} - \text{death rate}$ |
| Dependency ratio | $\dfrac{(0\text{–}14)+(65+)}{15\text{–}64}\times100$ |
| Mean | $\dfrac{\sum x}{n}$ |
| Median | the middle value when the data are ranked |
| Mode | the most frequently occurring value |
| Range | $\text{maximum} - \text{minimum}$ |
| Interquartile range | $Q_3 - Q_1$ |
| Gradient | $\dfrac{\text{vertical rise}}{\text{horizontal distance}}$ |
| River discharge | $\text{cross-sectional area} \times \text{velocity}$ (m³/s) |
| Cross-sectional area | $\text{width}\times\text{mean depth}$ |
| Map distance to ground distance | $\text{map distance}\times\text{scale factor}$ |
| Pie-graph sector | $\text{percentage}\times3.6°$ |

**Mean, median, mode and range.** Data: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13, 15, 17.
- Mean $= 72\div7 = 10.3$.
- Median $= 9$ (the fourth of seven values).
- Mode $= 7$.
- Range $= 17-4 = 13$.

**Interquartile range.** Ranked data: 11, 14, 16, 18, 21, 25, 28, 33 ($n = 8$).
- $Q_1$ is the mean of the 2nd and 3rd values: $(14+16)\div2 = 15$.
- $Q_3$ is the mean of the 6th and 7th: $(25+28)\div2 = 26.5$.
- $\text{IQR} = 26.5-15 = 11.5$. It describes the middle half of the data, so it is not distorted by one extreme value.

**Percentage change.** A town grows from 40 000 to 46 000: $(46\,000-40\,000)\div40\,000\times100 = 15\%$.

**Pie graph.** A sector measured as 126°: $126\div360\times100 = 35\%$.

Always show the working, give the unit, and round sensibly. One decimal place is usually enough, and a population cannot be 3.7 people.

## Fieldwork methodology: the enquiry sequence

| Stage | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **1 Aim and hypothesis** | a single testable statement, such as "channel width increases downstream" or "environmental quality improves with distance from the CBD"; often with a **null hypothesis** ("there is no relationship…") |
| **2 Planning and site selection** | sites chosen to test the hypothesis; safe, accessible and with permission; justified; a risk assessment (traffic, water, weather) |
| **3 Sampling** | random, systematic or stratified; say which and why (below) |
| **4 Data collection** | primary and secondary; equipment; recording sheets prepared in advance |
| **5 Recording** | a table with quantities, units and consistent decimal places; repeat readings and take the average |
| **6 Presentation** | the graph or map that suits the data, properly labelled |
| **7 Analysis** | describe the pattern, use the data as evidence, identify anomalies, then explain them |
| **8 Conclusion** | answer the hypothesis directly: supported, partly supported, or not supported |
| **9 Evaluation** | sources of error, the limits of the sample, the weather on the day, the time of day, and specific improvements |

## Sampling

| Method | How | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Random** | random number tables or generators choose the sites or people | no bias | may miss important areas; sites may be inaccessible |
| **Systematic** | at regular intervals: every 50 m along a transect, every 10th person | even coverage; easy | may miss features between intervals |
| **Stratified** | proportional samples from each sub-group (age groups, zones) | represents every group fairly | needs prior knowledge of the groups |
| **Opportunistic / convenience** | whoever or wherever is available | quick | biased |

More data raises confidence but cannot correct a biased sample. Three sites all on the same straight reach will not test a hypothesis about meanders.

## Primary and secondary data

| Primary (collected yourself) | Secondary (collected by someone else) |
|---|---|
| measurements, counts, questionnaires, interviews, field sketches, photographs | census data, maps, council records, newspapers, websites, earlier surveys |
| up to date and fitted to your hypothesis | covers bigger areas and longer periods |
| time-consuming; small samples | may be out of date, collected for another purpose, or biased |

A Term 2 paper used a **traffic survey before and after a by-pass**. The 2016 results, collected by someone else before the by-pass was built, were **secondary data**. The conclusion needed paired figures from the same times of day.

## River, coastal and Rangitoto fieldwork

| Enquiry | Typical measurements and equipment |
|---|---|
| **River** | channel width (tape measure); depth at regular intervals (metre rule); velocity (flow meter, or a float timed over 10 m with a stopwatch); wetted perimeter; bedload size and roundness (calipers, Powers' roundness chart); gradient (clinometer and ranging poles), repeated at several sites downstream |
| **Coastal** | beach profile (ranging poles, tape and clinometer); sediment size up the profile; longshore drift (float or marked pebble timed along the beach); groyne height difference on each side; wave frequency (waves per minute) |
| **Rangitoto field trip** (a transect enquiry) | from the ferry jetty to the summit, vegetation density and height (quadrats), slope steepness (clinometer) and soil depth all change; measure each at regular intervals and plot against height or distance. The island is young basaltic lava (about 600 years old), so soil is thin or absent in many places, which is why soil depth and vegetation vary so much over short distances |

## Urban fieldwork methods

**Typical hypotheses:**
- land use changes with distance from the CBD (Burgess);
- building height and land value decrease with distance from the CBD (bid rent);
- environmental quality is lower in the inner city than in the suburbs;
- pedestrian numbers are highest at the PLVI;
- the sphere of influence of a shop increases with its order;
- traffic has fallen since the by-pass opened;
- house prices are higher where the environmental quality score is higher.

| Method | How it is done | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Land-use mapping / survey** | record the use of every ground-floor building (and upper floors) along a **transect** from the CBD outwards, using a classification key (retail, office, residential, industrial, open space, vacant) | CBD zones, core and frame, the Burgess and Hoyt models |
| **Building height and age survey** | count the storeys; estimate age from architectural style | bid rent, the age of zones |
| **Pedestrian counts** | count people passing a fixed point for 10 minutes at several sites at the **same times**; use a tally counter | the PLVI, the core–frame boundary |
| **Traffic counts** | count vehicles by type at set times (rush hour and off-peak) | congestion, the by-pass hypothesis |
| **Environmental quality survey (EQS)** | score each site from −3 to +3 (or 1 to 5) on litter, noise, graffiti, greenery, building condition and traffic; bi-polar scale | inner-city decay, regeneration |
| **Questionnaires** | ask shoppers where they have come from, how often and why they visit; stratified by age and sex | sphere of influence, range, threshold, the order of a centre |
| **Interviews** | residents, shopkeepers, council planners | attitudes to intensification or regeneration |
| **Housing survey** | house type, garden size, garages, condition | filtering, gentrification |
| **Field sketches and photographs** | annotated | the character of a zone |
| **Secondary data** | census (population, income, ethnicity), council plans (Unitary Plan zones), property values, historical maps | change over time |

**Presentation:**
- land-use maps and transect diagrams;
- bar and divided-bar graphs;
- scatter graphs (distance against pedestrian count or EQS);
- desire-line maps of shoppers' origins (sphere of influence);
- proportional symbols for counts.

**Evaluation points:**
- counts depend on the time of day, the day of the week and the weather;
- EQS scores are **subjective**, so use the same scorer or average several;
- questionnaires can be biased by who agrees to answer;
- the land-use classification can be ambiguous (mixed-use buildings);
- the transect may not be representative of the whole city;
- **safety**: traffic and stranger danger.
- **Improvements:** repeat on different days, take a larger sample, use several transects, and use secondary data to check.

## Common exam errors

- A hypothesis that cannot be tested ("the city is interesting").
- Naming equipment without saying how it is used.
- A conclusion that does not refer back to the hypothesis.
- An evaluation that says "be more accurate" instead of naming a specific error and a specific improvement.
- Inferring cause from a scatter graph: correlation is not causation.
- Quoting a figure with no unit or date.
