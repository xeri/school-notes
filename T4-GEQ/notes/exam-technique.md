# Exam technique

## The 2026 Term 4 paper

| Section | Theme | Marks | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Theme 1: Natural Environment | 50 | 1 hour |
| **B** | Theme 2: Population and Settlement | 25 | 30 minutes |
| **C** | Theme 3: Development and the use of resources | 25 | 30 minutes |
| **D** | Essays: **one from each theme** (3 essays) | 30 | 1 hour |
| | **Total** | **130** | **3 hours** |

- The marks for each question are shown at the end of the question.
- **Maps and diagrams** drawn in written answers can earn marks.
- The use of **examples** to support written answers can earn marks.
- In Section D, choose **one essay from each theme**, three in total, about 10 marks and 20 minutes each.
- Skills (maps, graphs, calculations) are tested inside Sections A–C. A topographic map is likely.

The 2025 format (100 marks in 3 hours: 75% core questions, 25% essays) is kept on the [syllabus page](../SYLLABUS.md) for older papers.

## Timing plan

- About **1.4 minutes per mark** (180 minutes ÷ 130 marks).
- Keep to the section times: Section A 60 minutes, B 30, C 30, D 60.
- For each essay: **2 minutes planning, 16 minutes writing, 2 minutes checking**.
- If an essay offers options, **write the option letter clearly**. School papers capped an essay at 5 marks when the option was not identified.
- Do not over-write short answers. A 2-mark question needs two creditable points, not a page.

## Command words

The specification uses the Cambridge IGCSE glossary.

| Command | What it demands |
|---|---|
| **Define / state the meaning of / what is meant by** | the meaning of the term in one precise sentence; no example needed |
| **Identify** | pick something out from the information given |
| **Name / state / list** | give the word or words by which it is known; a list names several |
| **Locate** | say where something is, or mark it on a map or diagram |
| **Describe** | what something is like or where it is: pattern, size, shape, trend, location. No reasons |
| **Explain / give reasons for / account for** | why it happens: causes and the mechanism linking them to the result |
| **Compare / what differences are shown** | comparative statements using "whereas", "more than", "by contrast"; two separate descriptions score poorly |
| **Suggest** | apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar resource where you cannot be certain |
| **Evaluate / to what extent / how far do you agree** | evidence on both sides, then a judgement |
| **Justify** | say why you chose that option and why you rejected the others |
| **Give your views / comment on** | say what you think, with a reason |
| **Draw / insert / label / annotate** | a sketch, with names added, and with notes explaining the process |
| **Illustrate your answer** | support it with specific examples or diagrams |
| **Measure** | read the value off the instrument or the scale |
| **Predict** | use your knowledge and the information provided to say what might happen next |
| **Devise / plan** | design something, such as a questionnaire or fieldwork method, to meet a requirement |
| **Study** | look carefully at the figure named; the answer will depend on it |
| **Using the information provided / using Fig. 1 only** | base the answer on the resource only |
| **With the help of / refer to** | use the resource *and* your own knowledge or a case study |

## Describe versus explain

| Describe | Explain |
|---|---|
| "Discharge rose from 8 to 42 m³/s in four hours." | "Discharge rose rapidly because the catchment is steep and urbanised, so infiltration was low and runoff reached the channel within minutes." |
| "The highest density is in the north-west." | "Density is highest in the north-west because the land is flat, the soil is deep alluvium and the port provides employment." |

If your sentence has no *because*, *so*, *this causes* or *as a result*, it is not an explanation.

## Using data as evidence

1. State the pattern.
2. Quote the figure with its unit and date.
3. Say what it shows.

"Life expectancy is far higher in the HIC: 82 years against 58 years in 2020, a gap of 24 years. This indicates much better nutrition and healthcare."

Never quote a number without saying what it proves, and never claim a trend you have not quantified.

## How short answers are marked: mark-scheme insights

Short answers are **point-marked**. "4 @ 1 mark" means four separate creditable points, or fewer points each with a development. For a 4-mark *explain*, write four distinct reasons, or two reasons each developed with "so…" or "because…".

| What the mark scheme does | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| "1 mark for identification, 1 for explanation" (coastal defences, farm location factors) | name the method or factor in official terminology, then say *how* it works; a description of what it looks like does not earn the second mark |
| "2 marks for simple reason + 2 marks for explanation" (why a village stayed small) | give each reason, then develop it with its consequence |
| "Max 2 if no diagram" (convectional rainfall), or "diagram alone enough… must be annotated" (Rangitoto) | draw it, and annotate every label with what is happening |
| "Max 3 if nothing about the Coriolis force" (trade winds) | the mechanism that explains the *direction* or the *key step* is reserved; leave it out and the top mark is gone |
| "Max 2… that do not explore why convection currents rise and fall" | say *why* (density) as well as *what* |
| "Comparison needed" | use comparative words (*higher than*, *whereas*, *both*), not two separate lists |
| "Credit 1 mark for paired data" / "MAX 1 reserved" for statistics | quote two figures from the same point on the resource side by side (190 vehicles in 2016 against 67 in 2018) |
| "Do not use statistics" / "Use statistics" | obey it: figures are not credited where the question bans them, and are required where it asks |
| "Using Fig. 1 only" | evidence must come from the resource; general knowledge is not credited, and naming places without describing a pattern scores 0 |
| "Too vague, do not reward": *government policy*, *migration*, *pollution*, *be eco-friendly*, *people live longer* | a vague phrase scores nothing; give the specific mechanism or method |
| "Anything visible in the picture" (photo questions) | name only what you can see, using the correct landform term |
| "No MAX on site/situation" | points on either site or situation all count |

**Theme 2 questions seen on school papers:**
- define net migration and natural increase;
- compare net migration and natural increase in New Zealand from a graph;
- the problems caused by large-scale emigration (5 marks);
- read Japan's birth rate from a graph;
- why natural decrease occurs in HICs such as Japan (4 marks);
- a rural settlement's pattern, resources, site and situation, and why it stayed small;
- why CBD shops have a large sphere of influence (5 marks);
- a traffic-survey fieldwork question (plotting, primary and secondary data, conclusions with paired data).

## How essays are marked: levels

Each essay is level-marked out of 10. The same four levels appear on every school mark scheme.

| Level | Marks | What the marker looks for |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 9–10 | comprehensive: every element of the question covered; **developed, place-specific examples** woven through the argument; accurate terminology and processes; clear evaluation with an overarching conclusion; knowledge beyond the taught minimum. Markers are told only the top 5–10% reach it |
| 3 | 6–8 | thorough explanation of why, but less comprehensive or balanced; terminology throughout; **at least one developed example**; evaluation visible at the top of the level |
| 2 | 3–5 | some reasons and features identified but weakly developed; mostly description; solutions *named* rather than explained; examples used in name only ("as seen in Omaha…") |
| 1 | 1–2 | the question is understood and started, but nothing is developed |
| 0 | 0 | no creditable content, including an introduction on its own |

Two caps apply to almost every essay:
- **A maximum of 6 marks if no examples are used.**
- Levels 3 and 4 need **developed** examples, not tags. "As seen in Waikato" is not a developed example.

The urbanisation essay scheme adds: "students are not able to achieve Levels 3 or 4 without reference to place-specific or historical-specific information", and "models (if used) should be referred to accurately". Higher answers also explain the *processes* behind a pattern, not just the outcome.

## SDEEL paragraphs

Mark schemes say there should be evidence of SDEEL, though at Level 4 it need not be explicit.

| Step | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **S**tatement | a direct answer to the question, taking a position |
| **D**escribe | what the process, problem or response actually is |
| **E**xplain | the causal chain: why it happens, or why the response should work |
| **E**xample | a named, dated, located example with a fact that does work |
| **L**ink | tie it back to the words of the question |

## Evaluative essays

For "to what extent", "how far do you agree" and "should…":
1. Write SDEEL paragraphs on both sides.
2. State the criteria you are judging by: cost, the number of people helped, durability, environmental effect, who gained and who lost.
3. Finish with a conclusion that answers the question and says **what the judgement depends on**.

A conclusion that merely repeats both sides scores little; one that takes a position and names its condition scores well. Diagrams earn credit in essays when they are referred to in the text.

**Theme 2 essay titles the school has set:**
- declining populations as a problem;
- ageing populations as a "crisis";
- youthful populations: causes, problems and solutions;
- governments and demographic issues (Germany and migration);
- "Migration is always bad";
- types of migration, push and pull, and obstacles;
- "Auckland is overpopulated";
- rapid urban growth in HICs and LICs, its problems and solutions;
- the history of urbanisation;
- the problems of urban environments in HICs and LICs, with squatter settlements.

## Named examples that earn marks

A named example that earns marks has a **place, a date, a scale and at least one specific fact or figure used in an argument**.
- *Developed:* "In Dharavi, Mumbai, up to a million people are packed into about 2 km² and share toilets and standpipes beside open drains, which is why water-borne disease spreads so quickly and why community toilet blocks were the first priority of upgrading."
- *A tag:* "Dharavi is a slum."

## Maps and diagrams in answers

- Draw diagrams in pencil, with a title, labels and **annotations**.
- Models worth drawing from memory for Theme 2:
  - the DTM;
  - pyramid shapes;
  - Lee's push–pull model;
  - the Burgess, Hoyt, Harris–Ullman, core–frame and Latin American city models;
  - the bid-rent graph;
  - the settlement hierarchy;
  - threshold and range;
  - the rural–urban fringe.
- A sketch map of a case study (Auckland's isthmus with the harbours, the CBD, the motorways and the RUB; Dharavi between the railway lines) can earn marks.
- Refer to the diagram in the text: "as shown in my diagram…".

## Common exam errors

- Describing when asked to explain, and giving two separate descriptions when asked to compare.
- Giving a trend without data, or quoting a figure with no unit and no date.
- Using an example as a tag instead of a developed fact.
- Writing an evaluation with no criteria, so the judgement has nothing to rest on.
- Running out of time on the essays because the short answers were over-written. Keep to the section times.
- Not stating which essay option you chose.

## Mistakes from the examiner reports

From the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) principal examiner reports for June 2025 and March 2026. Each one cost candidates marks.

| Mistake | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| **Naming a country instead of a precise place** ("Japan", or a country when an urban area was asked for) | name the city, region or event, with a date: "Christchurch, 22 February 2011" |
| **Long, irrelevant introductions** (general background, location detail, impacts in a causes answer) | start with the first creditable point; introductions earn nothing |
| **Generic case-study development with no place detail** | add the place-specific fact: a named suburb, scheme, figure or date |
| **Giving impacts when asked for causes** (or causes for consequences, or problems of the wrong place) | underline the key word and the place in the question before writing |
| **Subjective or vague words**: "deep", "wide", "big", "better"; "services", "pollution", "overcrowding", "resources", "infrastructure" on their own | say which and how much: "steep inner crater walls", "air pollution from vehicle exhaust", "piped water and sewerage" |
| **Imprecise graph reading or dropped units** (omitting "thousands", misreading the scale or the years) | read to the nearest gridline and copy the axis unit; give exact months or years |
| **Misreading the key words** (writing about all migration when the question said "work"; human features when it said "natural") | answer only what is asked, for the place asked about |
| **"Plates bumping into each other"** for an earthquake's cause | friction locks the plates → pressure builds → sudden **pressure release** → **seismic waves** |
| Writing "erosion" without the process | name it: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution |
| A waterfall with **soft rock on top of hard rock** | hard (resistant) rock **over** soft rock |
| Compass directions not precise enough ("north-east" when a more precise 16-point direction was needed) | use the 16 points; measure bearings with a protractor |
| Bullet-point lists in extended answers | write linked sentences that develop each idea |
