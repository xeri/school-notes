# Economic development and resources

## What development means

**Development** is an improvement in the quality of life and standard of living of a population. It is wider than wealth: the syllabus separates economic, social, political and environmental development, and a country can rise on one and fall on another.

| Type | What it measures | Example indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | income, output, employment | GDP per capita, % employed in agriculture |
| Social | health, education, equality, well-being | life expectancy, literacy, infant mortality |
| Political | democracy, freedom, corruption, rule of law | freedom indices, participation rates |
| Environmental | sustainability, pollution, resource use | ecological footprint, access to clean water |

**Growth is not development.** Growth is an increase in output or income. Development is what happens to people's lives as a result — so growth whose gains go to a small élite, or that destroys the environment people depend on, is growth without development.

## Measuring development

| Indicator | What it is | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | total value of goods and services produced in a year, divided by population | an average, so it hides the gap between rich and poor; excludes the informal and subsistence economy; exchange rates distort comparison |
| GNI per capita (PPP) | national income adjusted for what money actually buys locally | still an average, still hides inequality |
| Birth rate / fertility rate | births per 1000; children per woman | affected by culture and policy, not only development |
| Infant mortality rate | deaths under one year per 1000 live births | needs reliable registration of births and deaths |
| Life expectancy | average years a newborn can expect to live | an average distorted by high infant mortality |
| Literacy rate | % of adults who can read and write | definitions of literacy differ between countries |
| People per doctor | population divided by number of doctors | says nothing about distribution between city and countryside |
| % employed in agriculture | size of the primary sector | high figures can reflect a genuinely productive export sector |
| Access to safe water and sanitation | % of population with access | "access" is defined differently in different surveys |
| **HDI** | composite of income, education and life expectancy, scored 0–1 | combines social and economic measures, so it is more rounded — but ignores the environment, political freedom and internal inequality, and data is missing for some countries |
| **HPI** | how efficiently a country converts resources into long, happy lives | includes sustainability and well-being — but the economic dimension is absent, so it must be read alongside other measures |

**No single indicator is sufficient.** A full answer names the indicator, says what it reveals, and says what it conceals. The standard phrasing of the exam question is to justify your choice *and* explain why you rejected the others.

**Worked example — percentage change.** GNI per capita rises from US$1800 to US$2340.

$$\text{\% change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100 = \frac{2340-1800}{1800}\times100 = 30\%$$

## Inequalities in development

The **Brandt Line** was drawn in 1980 to separate a richer "North" from a poorer "South". It is still a useful shorthand but badly out of date: several countries south of it are now high-income, and the modern HIC / MIC / LIC classification by income per head is more accurate.

Causes of uneven development between countries, grouped as **SEEP**:

| | Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Social | poor health, low literacy, rapid population growth | a sick, unschooled or very youthful population produces less and spends more on dependency |
| Economic | dependence on a few primary exports, debt, weak infrastructure | commodity prices swing, debt interest drains revenue, no capital for investment |
| Environmental | drought, flood, disease, poor soils, landlocked position | repeated losses, high transport costs, land degradation |
| Political | conflict, instability, corruption, colonial legacy, trade barriers | investment withheld, aid misdirected, tariffs on processed exports keep countries in low-value production |

*Causal chain of the trade trap.* A country exports raw materials → prices are set on world markets and fluctuate → export earnings are unreliable → government revenue and investment are unreliable → no capital to build processing industry → the country keeps exporting raw materials at low value while importing manufactured goods at high value → the gap widens.

**Rostow's model** describes development as five stages — traditional society, pre-conditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, age of mass consumption — and assumes every country follows the same ladder. **Frank's dependency theory** instead argues that the "core" developed by extracting surplus from a "periphery", so the periphery's underdevelopment is a product of the relationship, not a stage. Use the two together: Rostow explains sequence, Frank explains why the sequence is not open to everyone.

Inequality **within** a country is normally a core–periphery pattern: investment, services, transport and jobs concentrate in the capital or the largest city and in the most accessible, most fertile or most resource-rich regions, while remote, mountainous or peripheral regions lose their young workers and then their services. New Zealand's version of this contrasts Auckland and the main centres with remote rural districts and parts of the east coast of the North Island, measured by income, employment, health and housing.

Related terms: **formal sector** (registered, taxed, regulated employment with contracts and protection), **informal sector** (unregistered, untaxed, insecure work such as street trading, waste picking and casual labour), and **urbanisation of poverty** (poverty shifting from being mainly rural to being mainly urban as cities grow faster than formal jobs).

## Sectors of production and economic transition

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | extracting or growing raw materials | farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying |
| Secondary | processing and manufacturing | food processing, steel, vehicles, construction |
| Tertiary | providing services | retail, transport, health, education, tourism, banking |
| Quaternary | knowledge, research and information | research and development, biotechnology, IT consultancy, telecommunications |

| Development stage | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary | Quaternary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-industrial (LIC) | dominant | small | small | negligible |
| Industrialising (MIC) | falling | rising sharply | rising | small |
| Post-industrial (HIC) | small | falling | dominant | growing |

*Causal chain of economic transition.* Farm mechanisation raises output per worker → labour is released from the land → factories offer higher wages, drawing that labour to towns → rising incomes raise demand for services → labour costs rise, so routine manufacturing relocates to lower-wage countries → the home economy shifts to services and knowledge work.

A **triangular graph** is the standard way to show employment structure, because the three sector percentages always sum to 100.

## Globalisation and transnational corporations

**Globalisation** is the increasing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and populations through trade, investment, migration, transport and communication technology.

| Driver | Effect |
|---|---|
| containerisation and cheap air freight | goods move further for less |
| the internet and telecommunications | management, design and services can be split from production |
| trade liberalisation and trade blocs | tariffs fall, markets open |
| growth of transnational corporations | production chains spread across many countries |

A **transnational corporation (TNC)** has its headquarters in one country and operations in others. Typically research, design and marketing stay in the home HIC while assembly moves to lower-wage countries.

| Benefits to a host country | Drawbacks to a host country |
|---|---|
| jobs, wages, training and skills transfer | profits repatriated to the home country |
| tax revenue and foreign currency from exports | wages and conditions may be lower than at home |
| technology and management expertise | weaker environmental regulation may be exploited |
| infrastructure built to serve the plant | local firms undercut and forced out |
| multiplier effect on local suppliers | decisions made abroad, and the plant can be closed and relocated |

Evaluate globalisation by scale and by group: the same change can raise national output while lowering wages in a particular region or sector.

## Primary industry: farming

| Classification | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Commercial / subsistence | grown for sale / grown to feed the farmer's own household |
| Arable / pastoral / mixed | crops / livestock / both |
| Horticulture | intensive growing of fruit, vegetables and flowers, often under glass |
| Intensive / extensive | high inputs of labour or capital per hectare, high yield per hectare / large area, low inputs per hectare |

| Location factor | Type | Influence |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | physical | growing season length, temperature, rainfall reliability |
| Relief | physical | flat land for machinery and arable; steep slopes for grazing or forestry |
| Soil | physical | depth, drainage, fertility, pH |
| Water | physical | rainfall or irrigation supply |
| Market access | human | distance and transport cost to buyers and processors |
| Capital | human | machinery, fertiliser, irrigation, housing for stock |
| Labour | human | availability and cost, especially for horticulture |
| Government policy | human | subsidies, quotas, environmental regulation, land tenure |

**Farming as a system.**

| Inputs | Processes | Outputs | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| land, labour, capital, water, stock, feed, fertiliser, fuel, seed, knowledge | ploughing, sowing, irrigating, feeding, milking, harvesting, maintaining | milk, meat, crops, wool; also waste, effluent and nutrient runoff | profit reinvested in more stock or machinery; a bad season cuts the next year's inputs |

Dairying in New Zealand — concentrated in the Waikato, Taranaki, Southland and increasingly Canterbury — is the syllabus's named primary-sector example: temperate climate with year-round grass growth, flat to rolling land, reliable rainfall or irrigation, and port access for exports.

| Environmental impact of farming | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Water pollution | nitrate from fertiliser and effluent leaches into groundwater and streams, causing eutrophication |
| Water extraction | irrigation draws down rivers and aquifers, reducing flow for other users and ecosystems |
| Soil erosion | ploughing, overgrazing and removal of shelter belts expose soil to wind and rain |
| Soil compaction | heavy stock and machinery on wet ground reduce infiltration and raise runoff |
| Desertification | overgrazing and cultivation in semi-arid areas strip vegetation; soil is lost and productivity falls |
| Enhanced global warming | methane from ruminant livestock, nitrous oxide from fertiliser, carbon dioxide from machinery and land clearance |
| Habitat and biodiversity loss | wetland drainage, hedge and bush removal |

| Sustainable practice | How it helps |
|---|---|
| riparian planting and fencing waterways | filters runoff, shades streams, keeps stock out |
| nutrient budgeting and precision fertiliser | applies only what the crop needs, cutting leaching |
| effluent storage and land application at the right time | stops direct discharge to water |
| crop rotation, cover crops, minimum tillage | maintains organic matter and soil structure |
| contour ploughing, terracing, shelter belts | reduces erosion by water and wind |
| stocking rates matched to carrying capacity | avoids overgrazing and compaction |
| agroforestry and retiring marginal land | restores cover on the most erodible ground |

## Food shortages

| Cause | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| drought, flood, tropical storm | natural | crops destroyed or the growing season lost |
| pests and crop disease | natural | yield loss, stored grain destroyed |
| soil exhaustion and desertification | natural/human | long-term fall in productivity |
| low capital investment | economic | no irrigation, fertiliser, seed or machinery |
| poor transport and storage | economic | food exists but spoils or cannot reach markets |
| poverty and high food prices | economic | food is available but unaffordable |
| war and conflict | political | farming abandoned, aid blocked, people displaced |
| rapid population growth | demographic | demand outruns the rise in production |

Effects run from malnutrition, stunting and vulnerability to disease, through children withdrawn from school to work or forage, to distress sale of livestock and land, debt, and migration.

Solutions and the catch in each: **food aid** saves lives but can undercut local farmers' prices; **irrigation** raises yield but can salinise soil and deplete rivers; **high-yielding varieties and fertiliser** raise output but need capital the poorest lack; **land reform** and **micro-credit** widen access but are politically hard; **better storage and roads** cut waste cheaply; **fairer trade terms** raise farmers' incomes. Raising total supply does not by itself feed households that cannot afford or reach the food — most modern food shortages are failures of access, not of global production.

## Secondary industry: manufacturing

Resources matter twice over: raw materials to process and fuel or power to process them with, which is why early industry clustered on coalfields and ore bodies, and why modern footloose industry — using electricity and light components — is far freer in its choice of site.

| Location factor | Pull |
|---|---|
| Raw materials | bulky or perishable inputs pull the plant towards the source (sawmills, dairy factories) |
| Fuel and power | historically coalfields; now a reliable grid connection |
| Transport | port, rail terminal, motorway junction; containerisation favours coastal sites |
| Market | heavy, fragile or perishable products pull the plant towards the customer |
| Labour | supply, skill level and cost; a research-intensive plant needs graduates |
| Land | large, flat, cheap sites with room to expand, usually on the urban fringe |
| Capital and agglomeration | banks, suppliers and shared services already clustered nearby |
| Government | grants, tax breaks, enterprise zones, planning permission, environmental rules |

**Manufacturing as a system:** inputs (raw materials, energy, labour, capital, components) → processes (assembly, machining, finishing, packing, quality control) → outputs (finished products *and* waste, emissions, effluent, heat, noise) → feedback (profit reinvested, or falling demand cutting production).

| Environmental threat | Scale |
|---|---|
| air pollution, smog, acid rain | local to regional |
| effluent and thermal pollution of rivers | local |
| solid and hazardous waste, contaminated land | local, long-lasting |
| noise, visual intrusion, lorry traffic | local |
| greenhouse gas emissions | global |
| resource depletion in extraction regions | global |

## Tertiary industry: tourism

| Cause of growth | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| rising real incomes | more disposable income for travel |
| paid holidays and shorter working hours | more leisure time |
| cheaper air travel and wide-bodied jets | long-haul destinations within reach |
| online booking and social media | easier comparison, booking and promotion |
| ageing, healthy, pensioned populations | time and money to travel outside peak season |
| political stability and visa liberalisation | new destinations opened |

Attractions divide into **natural** (climate, beaches, mountains, wildlife, scenery, geothermal features) and **cultural** (history, architecture, festivals, food, indigenous culture, sport). Both need **infrastructure**: airports and roads, accommodation, food and water supply, power, waste disposal, retail, guiding and security.

| Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|
| jobs in hotels, transport, guiding, retail | many jobs are seasonal, low-paid and unskilled |
| foreign currency earnings and tax revenue | profits leak out to foreign-owned airlines and hotel chains |
| multiplier effect on farming, building and crafts | food and land prices rise for residents |
| infrastructure built that residents also use | roads and water supply strained at peak season |
| incentive to conserve landscapes, wildlife and heritage | footpath erosion, reef and dune damage, litter, water extraction |
| cultural exchange and revival of traditional crafts | loss of local culture to commercialised performance |
| | heavy dependence on a single volatile industry — closures during the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 showed how quickly demand can vanish |

| Sustainable management | How it works |
|---|---|
| visitor caps, permits and timed entry | keeps use within the site's carrying capacity |
| boardwalks, marked tracks, hardened surfaces | concentrates wear where it can be absorbed |
| ecotourism: small groups, local guides, local ownership | keeps income local and keeps pressure low |
| entry fees reinvested in conservation | the visitor pays for the protection |
| off-season promotion | spreads pressure through the year |
| employment and training of local people | raises the share of the benefit retained locally |

## Energy and water resources

| Type | Sources |
|---|---|
| Non-renewable | coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear (uranium) |
| Renewable | hydroelectric, geothermal, wind, solar, tidal, wave, biofuel |

**Renewable** means replenished on a human timescale; it does **not** mean impact-free, and it does not mean reliable.

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | large reserves, cheap, reliable base load | the highest CO₂ per unit, air pollution, acid rain, mining damage |
| Oil | energy-dense, easily transported, feedstock for chemicals | finite, price volatile, spills, CO₂ |
| Natural gas | cleanest fossil fuel, flexible, quick to start | still a fossil fuel, methane leakage, pipeline dependence |
| Nuclear | no CO₂ in generation, huge output from little fuel, reliable | very high build cost, long-lived radioactive waste, accident consequences, long construction |
| Hydroelectric | renewable, reliable, long-lived, can store energy and store water | floods valleys and displaces people, traps sediment, blocks fish migration, needs relief and rainfall |
| Geothermal | continuous output, small land area, low emissions | only possible in volcanic regions, releases trace gases, ground subsidence |
| Wind | no fuel cost, quick to build, no emissions in use | intermittent, visual and noise impact, needs back-up or storage |
| Solar | no fuel cost, suits remote off-grid use, falling cost | no output at night, reduced in cloud, needs storage and land or roof area |
| Tidal and wave | highly predictable, large potential | very high cost, few suitable sites, damages estuary ecosystems |
| Biofuel | uses waste, can be carbon-neutral in principle | competes with food for land and water, deforestation risk, emissions when burned |

Energy mix varies with development: LICs rely more on fuelwood, charcoal and biomass, often with no grid in rural areas; MICs industrialising fast lean on coal and oil; HICs have high total consumption per head and more diversified mixes. New Zealand is unusual in generating a high proportion of its *electricity* renewably — hydro on the Waikato, Clutha and Waitaki, geothermal in the Taupō Volcanic Zone, and growing wind capacity — while transport and industrial heat still rely heavily on imported oil and on gas and coal.

Water is the other critical resource: demand grows with population, irrigation and industry, while supply depends on rainfall, storage and the condition of the catchment. Management uses reservoirs and dams, groundwater abstraction, transfer schemes, desalination, metering and pricing, leak reduction, recycling of greywater, and catchment protection. Over-abstraction lowers water tables, dries wetlands, allows saltwater intrusion in coastal aquifers, and sets upstream users against downstream ones.

## Sustainability and the environmental risks of development

**Sustainable development** meets present needs without preventing future generations from meeting theirs. In practice it means keeping resource use within the rate of replacement, and keeping waste within the environment's capacity to absorb it.

| Risk | Mechanism | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Air pollution and smog | vehicle and industrial emissions trapped under a temperature inversion | emission standards, cleaner fuel, public transport, industrial filters |
| Acid rain | sulphur and nitrogen oxides dissolving in cloud water, falling far downwind | flue-gas desulphurisation, low-sulphur fuel, international agreements |
| Water pollution | industrial effluent, farm runoff, untreated sewage | treatment plants, discharge consents, riparian buffers |
| Deforestation and land degradation | clearance for farming, logging, mining, settlement | protected areas, selective logging, replanting, certification |
| Resource depletion | extraction faster than replacement | recycling, efficiency standards, substitution, quotas |
| Enhanced global warming | greenhouse gas emissions | the section below |

## Global warming and the enhanced greenhouse effect

The **natural greenhouse effect** keeps the Earth habitable: shortwave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere, is absorbed by the surface, and is re-radiated as longwave radiation, part of which is absorbed by greenhouse gases and returned to the surface. The **enhanced greenhouse effect** is the additional warming caused by human emissions raising the concentration of those gases.

| Greenhouse gas | Main human sources |
|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | burning coal, oil and gas; cement manufacture; deforestation |
| Methane | ruminant livestock, rice paddies, landfill, leaks from gas and coal extraction |
| Nitrous oxide | nitrogen fertiliser, vehicle exhaust, some industry |
| Halocarbons (CFCs and successors) | refrigeration, aerosols, foam blowing |

**The carbon cycle.** Carbon moves between four stores — the atmosphere, the oceans, the biosphere (living things and soil), and geological stores (fossil fuels, limestone). Photosynthesis moves carbon from the atmosphere into plants; respiration and decomposition return it; the oceans dissolve and release it; and over geological time burial locks it away as fossil fuel and limestone. Burning fossil fuels transfers carbon from the geological store, where it was effectively permanent, into the atmosphere within a few decades — far faster than photosynthesis and ocean uptake can remove it, so atmospheric concentration rises.

| Impact | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Rising sea level | thermal expansion of seawater plus melting of land ice; low-lying coasts and atolls flood, and groundwater salinises |
| More intense storms and heavier rainfall | a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour and warmer seas supply more energy, intensifying the hydrological cycle |
| Shifting rainfall belts and more drought | changed circulation moves the dry subtropical belts, lengthening droughts in already dry regions |
| Retreating glaciers and seasonal snow | less meltwater in late summer for rivers and irrigation downstream |
| Ocean acidification and coral bleaching | dissolved CO₂ lowers ocean pH; warmer water expels coral symbionts |
| Ecosystem and agricultural shifts | species and growing zones move poleward and upslope; pests spread; some yields rise, others collapse |

| Solution | Scale | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| switch electricity to renewables and nuclear | national | capital cost, intermittency, grid investment |
| energy efficiency: insulation, appliances, industrial process heat | household to national | upfront cost, slow turnover of buildings |
| electrify transport, shift to rail, bus and cycling | urban and national | vehicle cost, charging infrastructure |
| reduce livestock emissions: feed, breeding, effluent capture | farm | cost, limits to what is technically possible |
| afforestation and peatland and wetland restoration | regional | competes for land, slow to take effect |
| carbon pricing and emissions trading | national | political resistance, risk of production moving abroad |
| international agreements | global | enforcement, and disagreement over who should cut first |

Low-lying atoll states such as Tuvalu and Kiribati are the standard illustrative examples of an area at heightened risk from sea-level rise; a major city suffering from smog serves for the atmospheric-degradation version of the same slot.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** "LICs have low GDP per capita" is description; explanation needs the mechanism that keeps it low.
- **Giving a trend without data.** Quote the figure, its unit and its year from the table or graph.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit** — "GDP per capita of 42 000" is not an answer; US$42 000 is.
- **Confusing growth with development**, or **renewable with clean and reliable**.
- **Listing advantages and disadvantages without a verdict.** Evaluation needs criteria, weighting and a conclusion that answers the question asked.
- **Using a case study as decoration.** A named example must supply a location, a date and a fact that does work in the argument.
- **Assuming the whole country behaves like the national average**, when the question is about regional inequality.
