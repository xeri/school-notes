# Economic development and resources

Named examples are summarised here; the dated facts and figures are in [CASE-STUDIES.md](../CASE-STUDIES.md).

## What development means

**Development** is an improvement in the quality of life and standard of living of a population. It is wider than wealth: the syllabus separates economic, social, political and environmental development, and a country can rise on one and fall on another.

| Type | What it means (class definition) | Example indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | the total value of goods and services produced, and the income of a country | GDP or GNI per capita, % employed in agriculture, energy use per person |
| Social | improvement in quality of life, social well-being, equality and social justice | life expectancy, literacy, infant mortality, doctors per 100 000 people |
| Political | the level of democracy and freedom of speech | political freedom score, corruption perception index |
| Environmental | sustainability, pollution, resource use | Environmental Performance Index, ecological footprint, access to clean water |

**Growth is not development.** Growth is an increase in output or income. Development is what happens to people's lives as a result — growth whose gains go to a small élite, or that destroys the environment people depend on, is growth without development.

## Measuring development

| Indicator | What it is | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year, divided by population | an average, so it hides the gap between rich and poor (India has many billionaires but millions below the poverty line, for example in Mumbai's Dharavi); excludes the informal and subsistence economy |
| GNI (GNP) per capita, PPP | national income per person, adjusted for what money buys locally | still an average; still hides inequality |
| Birth rate, fertility rate | births per 1000; children per woman | shaped by culture and policy, not only development |
| Infant mortality rate | deaths under one year per 1000 live births | needs reliable registration of births and deaths |
| Life expectancy | average years a newborn can expect to live | an average dragged down by high infant mortality |
| Literacy rate | % of adults (or of women) who can read and write | definitions differ between countries |
| Doctors per 100 000 people | access to healthcare | says nothing about distribution between city and countryside |
| % employed in agriculture | size of the primary sector | high figures can reflect a productive export sector |
| Energy use per person | kg of oil equivalent per year — a sign of industry and consumption | high in oil states regardless of social development |
| **HDI** | combines income, education and life expectancy into one scale from 0 to 1 | covers social and economic factors, so it is more accurate — but some data is not available for all countries, and it ignores the environment and inequalities within countries |
| **HPI** (Happy Planet Index) | how efficiently a country uses its resources to give its people long, happy lives without long-term damage to the environment | includes sustainability and well-being — but the economic dimension is absent, so it must be used alongside other indicators |

**No single indicator is sufficient.** A full answer names the indicator, says what it reveals and says what it conceals. The standard exam question asks you to justify your choice *and* explain why you rejected the others. For "the most useful indicator of *economic* development" the mark scheme accepted GNP per person (it measures the relative wealth of the country); for *social* development, doctors per person or literacy.

**Class data (one recent year for each):**

| Country | GDP per capita (PPP) | HDI | HPI | Political freedom | Corruption perception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | US$56 000 | 0.946 (very high) | 39.8 | 92/100, free | 70/100 |
| New Zealand | US$53 000 | 0.938 (very high) | 41.5 | 99/100, free | 81/100 |
| Brazil | US$20 000 | 0.786 (high) | 52.4 | 72/100, free | 35/100 |
| Indonesia | US$16 000 | 0.728 (high) | 50.1 | 57/100, partly free | 34/100 |
| Niger | US$1 700 | 0.459 (low) | 29.1 | 33/100, not free | 31/100 |

Brazil and Indonesia score *higher* on the HPI than the UK or New Zealand, because they achieve reasonable well-being with a much smaller ecological footprint — the clearest illustration of why the choice of indicator changes the ranking.

**Worked example — percentage change.** GNI per capita rises from US$1800 to US$2340.

$$\text{\% change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100 = \frac{2340-1800}{1800}\times100 = 30\%$$

## Inequalities in development

The **Brandt Line** was drawn in 1980 to separate a richer "North" from a poorer "South". It is a useful shorthand but badly out of date: several countries south of it, such as Singapore and South Korea, are now high-income, and the HIC / MIC / LIC classification by income per head is more accurate.

Causes of uneven development between countries, grouped as **SEEP**:

| | Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Social | poor health, low literacy, rapid population growth, gender inequality | a sick, unschooled or very youthful population produces less and spends more on dependants |
| Economic | dependence on a few primary exports, debt, weak infrastructure, little investment | commodity prices swing, debt interest drains revenue, no capital to build industry |
| Environmental | drought, flood, tropical disease, poor soils, landlocked position | repeated losses, high transport costs, land degradation |
| Political | conflict, instability, corruption, colonial legacy, trade barriers | investment withheld, aid misused, tariffs on processed exports keep countries in low-value production |

*Causal chain of the trade trap.* A country exports raw materials → prices are set on world markets and fluctuate → export earnings are unreliable → government revenue and investment are unreliable → no capital to build processing industry → the country keeps exporting low-value raw materials while importing high-value manufactured goods → the gap widens.

**Rostow's model (1960)** — five stages of economic development, with savings and investment rising through them:

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 Traditional society | most people work in subsistence agriculture; little technology or trade |
| 2 Pre-take-off (preconditions for take-off) | investment in transport and infrastructure; some commercial farming and mining for export |
| 3 Take-off | a shift from farming to manufacturing; rapid growth in a few industries |
| 4 Drive to maturity | investment and growth spread to many industries; technology used throughout |
| 5 High mass consumption | consumers enjoy a wide range of goods; services dominate |

Criticisms: outdated (1960) and oversimplified, based on a small number of mostly European countries; assumes all countries start equal with the same resources, population and climate; the capital needed to "take off" often only arrives as aid, and **debt repayments** can prevent take-off; and it ignores that developed countries grew at the **expense** of developing ones.

**Frank's dependency theory** was developed in opposition to Rostow. The world is a **core** of developed nations and a **periphery** of developing ones. The periphery exports **low-value raw materials** and depends on the core for its market; the core **processes** them into **high-value goods** and grows rich. Historical trade, including colonialism, made the periphery poor, and the relationship keeps it poor. Use the two together: Rostow explains a sequence; Frank explains why the sequence is not open to everyone.

| Country (class data) | GDP per capita | Literacy | Doctors per 100 000 | Primary sector | Rostow stage | Frank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mali | US$950 | 35.5% | 15 | 65% | traditional / pre-take-off | periphery: exports cotton, gold, livestock |
| Sri Lanka | US$4 325 | 92.4% | 120 | 26.5% | take-off | periphery: exports tea, rubber, textiles |
| China | US$14 730 | 97.2% | 250 | 22.3% | drive to maturity | moving towards the core: exports electrical goods |
| New Zealand | US$52 180 | 99% | 360 | 5.8% | high mass consumption | core, though it still exports primary produce |

**Inequality within a country** is normally a core–periphery pattern: investment, services, transport and jobs concentrate in the capital, the largest city and the most accessible, fertile or resource-rich regions, while remote, mountainous or peripheral regions lose their young workers and then their services. The same pattern appears in Brazil, where the industrial south-east (São Paulo, Rio) is far richer than the north-east and the Amazon.

**New Zealand's regional inequality** (the named example): Auckland and Wellington have higher incomes, more skilled jobs and better services; Northland, Gisborne/Tairāwhiti and parts of the East Coast and rural North Island have lower incomes, higher unemployment, poorer health and housing, and higher deprivation. Causes: remoteness and poor transport links; the closure of rural processing plants, railways and government services during economic restructuring from the 1980s; dependence on primary industries with few local processing jobs; young people leaving for the cities; and repeated storm damage. Responses have included regional development funding such as the Provincial Growth Fund (2018), roads and broadband, and Māori land and business development.

Related terms: **formal sector** (registered, taxed, regulated employment with contracts and protection), **informal sector** (unregistered, untaxed, insecure work such as street trading, waste picking and casual labour), and **urbanisation of poverty** (poverty shifting from being mainly rural to mainly urban as cities grow faster than formal jobs).

## Sectors of production and economic transition

| Sector | Activity (exam definitions) | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | produces energy or basic raw materials | farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying |
| Secondary | manufactures goods, transforming a raw material into something else | food processing, steel, vehicles, construction |
| Tertiary | provides services rather than goods | retail, transport, health, teaching, tourism, banking, refuse collection |
| Quaternary | provides information services: computing, ICT, consultancy, research and development | software, biotechnology, telecommunications, R&D |

**Following a product through the sectors — a packet of crisps:** a farmer grows potatoes (primary) → a factory slices, fries and packs them (secondary) → trucks deliver them and a supermarket sells them (tertiary) → scientists develop new flavours and a firm designs the marketing data systems (quaternary).

The **Clark–Fisher model** shows how employment shifts between sectors as a country develops, based on the changing employment structure of the UK:

| Development stage | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary | Quaternary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-industrial (LIC) | dominant | small | small | negligible |
| Industrialising (MIC) | falling | rising to a peak | rising | small |
| Post-industrial (HIC) | small | falling | dominant | growing |

*Causal chain of economic transition.* Farm mechanisation raises output per worker → labour is released from the land → factories offer higher wages, drawing that labour to towns → rising incomes raise demand for services → labour costs rise, so routine manufacturing relocates to lower-wage countries → the home economy shifts to services and knowledge work.

**Why so many people in LICs such as Mali work in the primary sector:** little education, so few people qualify for other work; little investment or capital to build factories; the country has not had an industrial revolution and is far from industrial maturity; poor access to processing for its natural resources; and most families farm at subsistence level to feed themselves.

A **triangular graph** is the standard way to show employment structure, because the three sector percentages always sum to 100.

## Globalisation and transnational corporations

**Globalisation** is the increasing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and populations through trade, investment, migration, transport and communication technology.

| Driver | Effect |
|---|---|
| containerisation and cheap air freight | goods move further for less |
| the internet and telecommunications | management, design and services can be separated from production |
| trade liberalisation and trade agreements | tariffs fall, markets open |
| growth of transnational corporations | production chains spread across many countries |

A **transnational corporation (TNC)** has its headquarters in one country and operations in others. Typically research, design and marketing stay in the home country while production moves to where raw materials, markets or cheaper labour are.

| Benefits to a host country | Drawbacks to a host country |
|---|---|
| jobs, wages, training and skills transfer | profits sent back to the home country |
| tax revenue and export earnings | wages and conditions may be lower than at home |
| technology and management expertise | weaker environmental regulation may be exploited |
| infrastructure built to serve the plant | local firms undercut and forced out |
| multiplier effect on local suppliers | decisions made abroad; the plant can be closed and relocated |

**Named example: Fonterra** — a New Zealand dairy co-operative owned by its farmer-suppliers, formed in 2001, which collects most of New Zealand's milk, processes it in plants across the country and sells dairy products in well over 100 countries through brands such as Anchor and Anlene, with offices, joint ventures and processing overseas. It shows the TNC pattern from the home-country side: jobs and earnings for New Zealand, but dependence on world dairy prices and exposure to political decisions in importing countries.

Evaluate globalisation by scale and by group: the same change can raise national output while lowering wages in a particular region or sector.

## Primary industry: farming

| Classification | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Commercial / subsistence | grown for sale / grown to feed the farmer's own household |
| Arable / pastoral / mixed | crops / livestock / both |
| Horticulture | intensive growing of fruit, vegetables and flowers, often under glass |
| Intensive | high inputs of labour or capital per hectare, giving high output per hectare |
| Extensive | small inputs of labour, fertiliser and capital relative to the large area farmed; a small output per hectare |

| Location factor | Type | Influence |
|---|---|---|
| Relief | physical | flat land suits machinery and crops; steep slopes suit grazing or forestry |
| Soil | physical | depth, drainage, fertility, pH decide which crops can grow |
| Rainfall | physical | crops and grass need reliable water; irrigation where it is short |
| Temperature, sunshine, frost-free days | physical | the length of the growing season and which crops ripen |
| Water source | physical | a river or aquifer for irrigation and stock |
| Market access | human | produce is bulky, heavy or perishable, so transport cost and freshness pull farms towards markets and processing plants; farmers also buy supplies there |
| Capital | human | machinery, fertiliser, irrigation, buildings |
| Labour | human | availability and cost, especially for horticulture |
| Government policy | human | subsidies, quotas, environmental rules, land ownership |

*This loses marks:* naming a factor without saying how it affects land use. "Relief (1) — flat land allows machinery for crops (2)" is the pattern.

**Farming as a system.**

| Inputs | Processes | Outputs | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| physical: land, climate, soil, water; human: labour, capital, stock, feed, fertiliser, fuel, machinery, knowledge | ploughing, sowing, irrigating, feeding, milking, harvesting, maintaining | products: milk, meat, crops, wool; waste: effluent, nitrate runoff, methane | profit reinvested in more stock or machinery; a bad season or low price cuts the next year's inputs |

An input that has improved output — for example fertiliser (more grass per hectare, so more cows and milk), irrigation (grass growth through dry summers), better breeding (more milk per cow) or rotary milking sheds (more cows milked per hour).

**A New Zealand dairy farm** (the named example). Dairying is concentrated in the Waikato, Taranaki, Southland and, increasingly with irrigation, Canterbury. Location factors: a mild temperate climate with grass growing almost all year; reliable rainfall or irrigation; flat to rolling land; fertile soils; a nearby processing plant, because milk is perishable and is collected daily by tanker; and ports for export. The herd grazes outdoors on rotated paddocks, is milked twice a day, calves in spring, and is supplemented with feed in winter. Dairy is New Zealand's largest goods export — roughly one dollar in every three earned from goods exports.

| Environmental impact of farming | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Water pollution | nitrate from fertiliser and urine, and phosphate and bacteria in effluent, leach into groundwater and streams, causing eutrophication |
| Water extraction | irrigation draws down rivers and aquifers, reducing flow for other users and ecosystems |
| Soil erosion | ploughing, overgrazing and removal of shelter belts expose soil to wind and rain |
| Soil compaction (pugging) | heavy stock and machinery on wet ground reduce infiltration and raise runoff |
| Desertification | overgrazing and cultivation in semi-arid areas strip vegetation; soil is lost and productivity falls |
| Enhanced global warming | methane from cattle, nitrous oxide from fertiliser and urine, CO₂ from machinery and land clearance — agriculture produces about half of New Zealand's greenhouse emissions |
| Habitat and biodiversity loss | wetland drainage, removal of native bush |

| Sustainable practice | How it helps |
|---|---|
| riparian planting and fencing waterways | filters runoff, shades streams, keeps stock out |
| nutrient budgeting and precision fertiliser | applies only what the pasture needs, cutting leaching |
| effluent ponds and spreading at the right time | stops direct discharge to water |
| lower stocking rates matched to the land | less overgrazing, compaction, effluent and methane |
| stand-off pads and wintering barns | stock off wet paddocks, less pugging and leaching |
| crop rotation, cover crops, minimum tillage | maintains organic matter and soil structure |
| contour ploughing, terracing, shelter belts | reduces erosion by water and wind |
| retiring and planting the steepest, most erodible land | restores cover and stores carbon |

*Evaluating long-term sustainability:* dairy brings large export earnings, jobs and rural service towns, but the costs fall on rivers, groundwater and the climate. A judgement depends on whether the mitigations are enforced and whether stock numbers fall where the water quality cannot cope.

## Food shortages

| Cause | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| drought, flood, tropical storm | natural | crops destroyed or the growing season lost |
| pests and crop disease (e.g. locusts) | natural | yield loss, stored grain destroyed |
| soil exhaustion and desertification | natural and human | long-term fall in productivity |
| low capital investment | economic | no irrigation, fertiliser, seed or machinery |
| poor transport, storage and distribution | economic | food exists but spoils or cannot reach markets |
| poverty and high food prices | economic | food is available but unaffordable |
| war and conflict | political | farming abandoned, aid blocked, people displaced |
| rapid population growth | demographic | demand outruns the rise in production |

Effects: malnutrition, stunting and greater vulnerability to disease, especially among young children (raising infant mortality); children withdrawn from school; distress sale of livestock and land; debt; migration to cities and camps; conflict over land and water.

Solutions and the catch in each: **food aid** saves lives but can undercut local farmers' prices; **irrigation** raises yields but can salinise soil and deplete rivers; **high-yielding varieties and fertiliser** (the Green Revolution) raise output but need capital the poorest lack; **land reform** and **micro-credit** widen access but are politically hard; **better storage and roads** cut waste cheaply; **tree planting and soil conservation** reverse desertification slowly; **fairer trade** raises farmers' incomes. Raising total supply does not by itself feed households that cannot afford or reach the food — most modern food shortages are failures of access, not of global production.

**Named example: the Horn of Africa and the Sahel.** In **Somalia in 2011** two failed rainy seasons, high world food prices and civil war — armed groups blocked aid — combined into a famine that killed about a quarter of a million people, half of them young children. Across the **Sahel**, recurring drought and desertification make food shortages chronic.

## Secondary industry: manufacturing

Resources matter twice over: raw materials to process and fuel or power to process them with. Early industry therefore clustered on coalfields and ore bodies; modern **footloose** industry, using electricity and light components, is far freer in its choice of site.

| Location factor | Pull |
|---|---|
| Raw materials | bulky or perishable inputs pull the plant towards the source (sawmills, dairy factories, steel near ore) |
| Fuel and power | historically coalfields; now a reliable grid connection |
| Transport | port, rail terminal, motorway junction; containerisation favours coastal sites |
| Market | heavy, fragile or perishable products pull the plant towards the customer |
| Labour | supply, skill level and cost |
| Land | large, flat, cheap sites with room to expand, usually on the urban fringe |
| Capital and agglomeration | banks, suppliers and shared services already clustered nearby |
| Government (political) | grants, tax breaks, special economic zones, planning permission, environmental rules |

**Manufacturing as a system:** inputs (raw materials, energy, labour, capital, components, land) → processes (smelting, assembly, machining, finishing, packing, quality control) → outputs (products *and* waste: emissions, effluent, slag, heat, noise) → feedback (profit reinvested, or falling demand cutting production).

**Iron and steel in China** (a Term 2 resource): in 1950 the few works were clustered in the north-east near coal and iron ore; by 2010 there were many more, spread more widely, especially in the east near the coast. **Shanghai suits steelmaking** because it has a huge local demand from construction and manufacturing, a port to import coal and iron ore and export steel, flat land, the Yangtze for transport and water, and a large workforce.

**Named example: New Zealand Steel, Glenbrook** (south of Auckland) — the country's only integrated steel mill, located for its raw material (ironsand mined on the Waikato coast at Waikato North Head and piped to the mill), coal from the Waikato, flat land, rail and port links, and the Auckland market. It is one of New Zealand's largest single sources of greenhouse gas, and in 2023 the company and government agreed to co-fund an electric arc furnace to cut its emissions.

| Environmental threat | Scale |
|---|---|
| air pollution, smog, acid rain | local to regional |
| effluent and thermal pollution of rivers | local |
| solid and hazardous waste, contaminated land | local, long-lasting |
| noise, visual intrusion, lorry traffic | local |
| greenhouse gas emissions | global |
| resource depletion in extraction regions | global |

**Reducing the environmental risks of industry** — points a mark scheme credits: restrict emissions; scrubbers on chimneys; treat waste water before disposal; control waste disposal; replace old machines with more efficient ones; fines for polluters and strong enforcement; use renewable energy instead of coal; strict planning rules for new factories; keep industry out of sensitive areas; reuse brownfield sites. *This loses marks:* vague answers like "be eco-friendly" or "reduce pollution" with no method.

## Tertiary industry: tourism

| Cause of growth | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| rising incomes (affluence) | more disposable income for travel |
| paid holidays and shorter working hours | more leisure time |
| cheaper air travel and wide-bodied jets | long-haul destinations within reach |
| online booking and social media | easier comparison, booking and promotion |
| ageing, healthy, retired populations | time and money to travel outside peak season |
| political stability and easier visas | new destinations opened; conflict and pandemics close them |

Attractions divide into **natural** (climate, beaches, mountains, lakes, wildlife, scenery, geothermal features) and **cultural** (history, architecture, festivals, food, Māori and other indigenous culture, sport). Both need **infrastructure**: airports and roads, accommodation, food, water and power supply, waste disposal, retail, tour guides and other services.

| Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|
| jobs in hotels, transport, guiding, retail | many jobs are seasonal, low-paid and unskilled |
| foreign currency earnings and tax revenue | profits leak out to foreign-owned airlines and hotel chains |
| multiplier effect on farming, building and crafts | food, land and housing prices rise for residents |
| infrastructure built that residents also use | roads, water and sewerage strained at peak season |
| incentive to conserve landscapes, wildlife and heritage | footpath erosion, wildlife disturbance, litter, water extraction, emissions from flights |
| cultural exchange and revival of traditional crafts | loss of local culture to commercialised performance |
| | heavy dependence on a volatile industry — the COVID-19 border closures from March 2020 showed how quickly demand can vanish |

**Why countries keep investing in tourism despite market swings** (an essay question): it earns foreign exchange, it employs many people with little training, it spreads income to regions with few other industries, it uses landscapes that cannot be exported any other way, and demand has always recovered after shocks — but diversification reduces the risk.

| Sustainable management | How it works |
|---|---|
| visitor caps, permits and booking systems | keeps use within the site's carrying capacity |
| boardwalks, marked tracks, hardened surfaces | concentrates wear where it can be absorbed |
| ecotourism: small groups, local guides, local ownership | keeps income local and pressure low |
| entry fees and visitor levies reinvested in conservation | the visitor pays for the protection |
| off-season promotion | spreads pressure through the year |
| employment and training of local people | raises the share of the benefit retained locally |

**Named example: Queenstown** — natural attractions (lakes, mountains, ski fields, Milford Sound day trips) and adventure activities (the world's first commercial bungy jump, 1988) bring visitors all year. Benefits: jobs, an international airport, high incomes. Problems: housing too expensive for workers, congestion, pressure on water and sewerage, and near-total collapse during the COVID-19 border closure.

## Energy resources

A **resource** is a naturally occurring substance that can be used. **Renewable** energy comes from a source that is not depleted when used — it is replenished naturally on a human timescale. **Non-renewable** energy comes from a finite stock that is used up faster than it forms.

| Type | Sources |
|---|---|
| Non-renewable | coal, oil, natural gas (fossil fuels), nuclear (uranium) |
| Renewable | hydroelectric, geothermal, wind, solar, tidal, wave, biofuel (and fuelwood, if trees are replanted) |

**Renewable does not mean impact-free or reliable.** A hydro dam is renewable and still floods a valley; wind and solar stop when the wind drops or the sun sets.

| Source | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | fossilised plants burned to raise steam | large reserves, cheap, reliable, easy to stockpile | the highest CO₂ per unit of energy, air pollution and acid rain, mining damage |
| Oil | fossilised marine organisms; refined into fuels | energy-dense, easily transported, fuels vehicles, feedstock for plastics | finite and passing "peak oil", price volatile, spills, CO₂ |
| Natural gas | methane trapped in rock, released through wells | the cleanest fossil fuel, flexible, quick to start, good back-up | still a fossil fuel; methane leaks; fracking can contaminate groundwater |
| Nuclear | uranium fission heats water to steam | no CO₂ in generation, huge output from little fuel, reliable | very high build cost, long-lived radioactive waste, accident risk (Chernobyl, Fukushima), long construction |
| Hydroelectric | falling water from a dam turns turbines | renewable, reliable, long-lived, stores water and energy, cheap once built | floods valleys and displaces people, traps sediment, blocks fish, needs high relief and rainfall |
| Geothermal | steam or hot water from heated rock drives turbines or heats water directly | continuous output, small land area, low emissions | only in volcanic regions, releases some gases, ground subsidence |
| Wind | wind turns turbine blades | no fuel cost, quick to build, no emissions in use | intermittent, visual and noise impact, kills birds, needs back-up |
| Solar | photovoltaic cells, or water heated directly | free once installed, suits remote off-grid places in LICs | no output at night and less in cloud, dust or ash; at high latitudes least sun in winter when demand peaks |
| Tidal and wave | tidal flow or wave motion turns turbines | highly predictable, large potential | very high cost, few sites, damages estuary ecosystems |
| Biofuel and fuelwood | crops, waste or wood burned | uses waste; carbon-neutral in principle if replanted; fuelwood is cheap and available | competes with food for land; deforestation if trees are not replaced; smoke and greenhouse gases |

**Energy and development.** In traditional LIC societies the main fuel is **fuelwood** — about 40% of the world's population rely on it for cooking, heating and light, because they cannot afford power stations, fuel imports or a national grid. As countries develop they build power stations and burn fossil fuels, because the technology exists and the fuels are widely available — coal has dominated China's industrialisation despite the Three Gorges Dam. Developed countries remain heavily dependent on fossil fuels but add more renewables and often nuclear.

**Global energy.** World primary energy use rose from about 140 000 TWh in 2010 to about 172 000 TWh in 2023, with fossil fuels still supplying about 81% of the total. Electricity is changing faster: in 2024 renewables and nuclear together supplied just over 40% of the world's electricity (renewables alone about a third).

**New Zealand's energy mix** (the named example; figures from the class statistics sheets):

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Primary energy supply | oil about 32%, geothermal 22%, natural gas 20%, hydro 10%, coal 7%, other 9% |
| Energy consumed by users | oil 48%, electricity 24%, gas 12.5%, other renewables 9.7%, coal 4.1%, geothermal 1.3% |
| Electricity generation, 2023 | about 43 500 GWh: hydro 60%, geothermal 18%, gas 9%, wind 7%, coal 2%, solar, wood and biogas about 1% each — **88% renewable** |
| Electricity as a share of all energy | only about a quarter — so 88% renewable electricity does not mean 88% renewable energy |
| Oil | about 73% of oil is used in transport, mainly petrol and diesel |
| Energy use by sector | transport 36%, industry 34%, households 11%, commercial and public services 9%, agriculture, forestry and fishing about 5% |
| Greenhouse gas emissions | agriculture about 53%, energy about 37%, industrial processes about 6%, waste about 5% |

Why the mix is what it is: high rainfall and steep relief for hydro (the Waikato, Waitaki and Clutha schemes; Manapōuri, at 850 MW, is the largest station); the Taupō Volcanic Zone for geothermal; strong westerly winds for wind farms; natural gas from Taranaki; Huntly power station (954 MW, coal and gas) as back-up in dry years when the lakes are low. Planned new capacity is mostly wind and solar. The problem is the other three-quarters of energy use — transport and industrial heat still run on imported oil, gas and coal.

**Locating power stations:**
- **Thermal (coal, oil, gas)**: close to the fuel source because coal is bulky and costly to move, or to a port if fuel is imported, or to pipelines and refineries; large areas of flat land; next to a river or the sea for cooling water.
- **Hydroelectric**: high rainfall and reliable river flow; a steep gradient or narrow valley to dam; impermeable, stable rock; low population to relocate. **The Three Gorges Dam** on the Yangtze in China, the world's largest hydroelectric station at 22 500 MW and over 2 km wide, was sited where the river has huge potential, the bedrock is firm, an island eased construction, and the area has had no recent major earthquakes — but over a million people were relocated, towns such as Fengdu were flooded, and sediment is trapped behind the dam.
- **Nuclear**: on the coast for cooling water and for importing fuel and exporting waste; away from large populations; on stable, flat land above flood level; with rail or road links and a grid connection — **Sizewell**, on the Suffolk coast of England, fits every factor.
- **Wind farm**: high, exposed, consistently windy sites, or offshore; near the grid; away from houses; low ecological value.

**Technology and resources.** **Iceland** has no oil or gas but abundant geothermal energy, which it uses for electricity and heating, and has experimented with using that electricity to make hydrogen from water as a transport fuel. It shows technology can reduce resource problems — but only where the right renewable resource exists. **Energy conservation** (reduce, reuse, recycle; insulation, efficient appliances, public transport) is the other side of the supply problem.

**Should LICs rely on renewables?** For: solar and small hydro need no fuel imports, suit places with no grid, and avoid locking in fossil-fuel dependence. Against: high upfront cost, technology and maintenance needs, intermittency without storage, and the argument that today's HICs industrialised on cheap fossil fuels. A strong essay judges by the country's resources, capital and grid.

## Global warming and the enhanced greenhouse effect

The **natural greenhouse effect** keeps the Earth habitable: short-wave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface, which re-radiates long-wave radiation; greenhouse gases absorb part of it and return it to the surface. The **enhanced greenhouse effect** is the extra warming caused by human (anthropogenic) emissions raising the concentration of those gases. **Global warming** is the resulting long-term rise in average global temperature.

**History.** The climate has always changed — ice ages alternating with warmer interglacials, and a cooler "Little Ice Age" in recent centuries — driven by changes in the Earth's orbit, volcanic eruptions and solar output. Since the industrial revolution (about 1750), burning coal, then oil and gas, has raised carbon dioxide from about 280 parts per million to over 420 ppm today. Global average temperature has risen by about 1.2 °C since the late 19th century, and 2024 was the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.

| Greenhouse gas | Main human sources |
|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | burning coal, oil and gas in power stations, vehicles and industry; cement; deforestation |
| Methane | cattle and sheep, rice paddies, landfill, leaks from gas and coal extraction |
| Nitrous oxide | nitrogen fertiliser, animal urine, vehicle exhaust |
| Halocarbons (CFCs and successors) | refrigeration, aerosols, foam blowing |
| Water vapour | increases as the air warms, amplifying the effect |

**Anthropogenic pollution** also degrades the atmosphere directly: smog from vehicle and industrial emissions (worst under a temperature inversion, which traps cool polluted air beneath warmer air), acid rain from sulphur and nitrogen oxides, and ozone depletion by CFCs.

**The carbon cycle.** Carbon moves between four stores — the atmosphere, the oceans, the biosphere (living things and soil) and the lithosphere (fossil fuels, limestone). Photosynthesis moves carbon from the atmosphere into plants; respiration and decomposition return it; the oceans dissolve and release it; over geological time burial locks it away as fossil fuel and limestone. Burning fossil fuels transfers carbon from the geological store, where it was effectively permanent, to the atmosphere within decades — far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can remove it — while deforestation shrinks a major sink. So atmospheric concentration rises, and the oceans absorb some of the excess, becoming more acidic.

| Impact | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Rising sea level | thermal expansion of seawater plus melting land ice; low-lying coasts and atolls flood, and groundwater turns salty |
| More intense storms and heavier rainfall | a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour and warmer seas supply more energy — the hydrological cycle speeds up, with more evaporation and more extreme rain |
| More drought in dry regions | shifting circulation pushes the dry subtropical belts polewards and dries already dry places such as the Sahel |
| Retreating glaciers and snow | less meltwater in late summer for rivers and irrigation |
| Ocean acidification and coral bleaching | dissolved CO₂ lowers ocean pH; warmer water expels coral symbionts |
| Ecosystem and farming shifts | species and growing zones move polewards and uphill; pests spread; some yields rise, others collapse |

| Solution | Scale | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| switch electricity to renewables (and nuclear) | national | capital cost, intermittency, grid investment |
| energy efficiency: insulation, appliances, industrial heat | household to national | upfront cost, slow turnover of buildings |
| electrify transport; shift to rail, bus and cycling | urban and national | vehicle cost, charging infrastructure |
| reduce farm emissions: feed additives, breeding, lower stocking, effluent capture | farm | cost; limits to what is technically possible |
| afforestation; wetland and peatland restoration | regional | competes for land, slow to take effect |
| carbon pricing and emissions trading | national | political resistance, risk of production moving abroad |
| international agreements (Kyoto 1997, Paris 2015) | global | enforcement, and disagreement over who should cut first |

**Named example: Tuvalu** — a Pacific atoll nation whose land is mostly less than about 2 m above sea level, facing flooding at king tides, salt water in its freshwater lens and crops, and coastal erosion; responses include sea walls, land reclamation and a 2023 treaty with Australia allowing some Tuvaluans to migrate each year. For the smog version, **Delhi** suffers some of the world's worst air pollution each winter, when vehicle and industrial emissions and crop-stubble burning are trapped under temperature inversions.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** "LICs have low GDP per capita" is description; explanation needs the mechanism that keeps it low.
- **Giving a trend without data.** Quote the figure, its unit and its year from the table or graph.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit** — "GDP per capita of 42 000" is not an answer; US$42 000 is.
- **Confusing growth with development**, renewable with clean and reliable, or **renewable electricity with renewable energy**.
- **Listing advantages and disadvantages without a verdict.** Evaluation needs criteria, weighting and a conclusion that answers the question asked.
- **Using a case study as decoration.** A named example must supply a location, a date and a fact that does work in the argument.
- **Assuming the whole country behaves like the national average**, when the question is about regional inequality.
- **Vague solutions** — "be eco-friendly", "reduce pollution" — instead of a named method and how it works.
