# Population and settlement

Named examples are summarised here; the dated facts and figures are in [CASE-STUDIES.md](../CASE-STUDIES.md).

## Global population change

For most of human history the population grew extremely slowly, because high birth rates were cancelled out by high death rates from famine, disease and war. Growth since about 1750 has been explosive — the **J-curve**.

| Period | World population (approximate) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 100 000 years ago | a few hundred thousand hunter-gatherers | food supply limited by hunting and gathering |
| about 10 000 years ago | about 5 million | farming begins: settled villages and a food surplus |
| 1 CE | about 250 million | slow growth; famine and epidemics repeatedly cut numbers back |
| about 1800 | 1 billion | the agricultural and industrial revolutions raise food supply |
| 1927 | 2 billion | sanitation, clean water and vaccination cut the death rate in Europe and North America |
| 1960 | 3 billion | medicine and public health spread to LICs; death rates fall but birth rates stay high |
| 1974, 1987, 1999 | 4, 5, 6 billion | each extra billion arrives faster — every 12–13 years |
| 2011 | 7 billion | |
| November 2022 | 8 billion | growth continuing, but the rate of growth has been falling since the 1960s |

*Causal chain of the last 250 years.* The agricultural revolution and then the industrial revolution increase the food supply → clean water, sewerage, vaccination and antibiotics cut the death rate, especially among infants → birth rates stay high for a generation because families still expect to lose children and need their labour → the gap between births and deaths produces rapid growth → as countries urbanise and women are educated, birth rates fall and growth slows. Almost all of today's growth is in Africa and South Asia; much of Europe and East Asia has stopped growing.

## Population density and distribution

**Density** is how many people per unit area — a number. **Distribution** is where those people are — a pattern. A question asking you to *describe the distribution* wants locations and the pattern between them, not a single density figure.

$$\text{population density} = \frac{\text{total population}}{\text{area in km}^2}$$

**Worked example.** A region of 2400 people in 300 km²: $2400 \div 300 = 8$ people/km².

| Factor | Encourages high density | Encourages low density |
|---|---|---|
| Relief | flat lowland, gentle slopes | steep mountains, high altitude |
| Soil | deep, fertile alluvial or volcanic soil | thin, stony, waterlogged or frozen soil |
| Climate | moderate temperature, reliable rainfall | arid, very cold, very wet |
| Water | reliable supply, navigable river | no surface water |
| Resources | minerals, fuel, timber, fishing grounds | no exploitable resource |
| Economic | jobs, industry, port, trade routes | remote, no employment |
| Social | services, schools, hospitals, family already there | isolation, no services |
| Political | stability, investment, planned growth | conflict, insecurity, restricted settlement |

| Named area | Density | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **High: Bangladesh and the Ganges delta** | over 1100 people per km² | flat, fertile, regularly renewed alluvial soil; abundant water; monsoon rain for rice that supports several harvests a year; a long history of intensive farming |
| **High: Auckland** | about a third of New Zealand's population on a narrow isthmus | two sheltered harbours, mild climate, flat to rolling volcanic land, the main port and international airport, jobs and services |
| **Low: the Sahara** | under 1 person per km² over most of it | under 250 mm of rain, extreme heat, no surface water, poor soils; people only at oases and along the Nile |
| **Low: New Zealand's West Coast and Southern Alps** | the West Coast region has only a few people per km² | steep mountains, very high rainfall, thin soils, isolation behind the Alps, few jobs after the decline of gold and coal mining |

Describe density with the vocabulary of the map: *densely populated*, *sparsely populated*, *moderate*, with named places and a direction or distance.

## Population change

$$\text{population change} = (\text{births} - \text{deaths}) \pm \text{net migration}$$

| Measure | Definition | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | live births per 1000 people per year | $\dfrac{\text{births}}{\text{population}}\times1000$ |
| Death rate | deaths per 1000 people per year | $\dfrac{\text{deaths}}{\text{population}}\times1000$ |
| Natural increase rate | birth rate minus death rate; a natural decrease if negative | $\text{BR} - \text{DR}$ (per 1000) |
| Natural increase % | the same, as a percentage | $\dfrac{\text{BR}-\text{DR}}{10}$ |
| Net migration | immigrants minus emigrants over a given period | per 1000, or as a total |
| Fertility rate | average number of children per woman over her lifetime | not per 1000 |
| Infant mortality rate | deaths under one year old per 1000 live births | per 1000 *live births* |

**Worked example.** BR 28/1000, DR 9/1000. Natural increase $= 28 - 9 = 19$ per 1000, i.e. $19/10 = 1.9\%$ per year. If net migration is $-3$ per 1000, total change $= 19 - 3 = 16$ per 1000.

**Worked example (rate from raw numbers).** 1260 births in a population of 45 000: $1260 \div 45\,000 \times 1000 = 28$ per 1000.

| Raises birth rate | Lowers birth rate |
|---|---|
| children needed as farm labour or to earn | mechanised farming, children in school for longer |
| high infant mortality, so parents have more children | low infant mortality |
| no pensions — children support parents in old age | state pensions and healthcare |
| little education or paid work for women; early marriage | educated women with careers, marrying and having children later |
| religion or custom favouring large families | contraception and family planning available and accepted |
| pro-natalist government policy | anti-natalist policy; children are expensive to raise in cities |

| Raises death rate | Lowers death rate |
|---|---|
| famine, unsafe water, poor sanitation | clean water, sewerage, vaccination |
| disease, including HIV/AIDS, malaria | medical care, antibiotics, antiretroviral drugs |
| war and conflict | peace and stable government |
| an ageing population (more old people die each year) | a youthful population, good diet |

**HIV/AIDS** shows how disease changes both rates. In southern Africa in the 1990s and 2000s it killed large numbers of adults of working and child-bearing age → the death rate rose, life expectancy fell by more than a decade in the worst-hit countries, the birth rate fell, and many children were orphaned. Antiretroviral drugs have since brought life expectancy back up.

**Why natural decrease occurs in some HICs such as Japan** — points a mark scheme credits: falling birth and fertility rates and small families; access to and education about contraception; women educated with careers and higher status; children are expensive; later marriage and childbearing; and, alongside, a rising death rate as the population ages. *This loses marks:* vague answers such as "government policy", "migration", "people live longer" or "low birth rate" with no reason attached.

**Global pattern of natural change.** Natural increase is highest in sub-Saharan Africa (Niger, Chad, the DR Congo — often 2.5–3.5% a year), moderate in South Asia and Latin America, close to zero across Europe and North America, and negative in Japan, Italy, much of Eastern Europe and now China. The pattern follows development: the higher a country's income, education and urbanisation, the lower its birth rate.

## The Demographic Transition Model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Total population | Example today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 High fluctuating | high | high, fluctuating | roughly zero | low, stable | no country; isolated groups in the Amazon rainforest |
| 2 Early expanding | high | falling rapidly | very high | rising fast | Niger, Chad, Afghanistan |
| 3 Late expanding | falling rapidly | low, still falling | falling | still rising, more slowly | India, Kenya, Bangladesh |
| 4 Low fluctuating | low, fluctuating | low | roughly zero | high, stable | New Zealand, the USA, the UK |
| 5 Declining | very low, below the death rate | low, slightly rising as the population ages | negative | slowly falling | Japan, Italy, Germany |

*Causal chain through the model.* Clean water, sanitation, vaccination and better food cut the death rate first → births stay high because family expectations change slowly → the widening gap between the curves is the population boom of stage 2 → education of women, urban living, falling infant mortality and contraception then cut the birth rate → the gap closes in stage 4 → where fertility stays below replacement (about 2.1 children per woman) the structure ages and stage 5 begins.

Limitations: it is based on the history of industrialised European countries; it ignores migration entirely; the timing of each stage varies, and today's LICs have moved through stage 2 much faster because medicine was imported; and government policy (China) or disease (HIV/AIDS) can push a country off the expected path.

## Population pyramids

**Constructing a pyramid.** Two horizontal bar graphs back to back: males on the left, females on the right; age groups in five-year bands with the youngest at the bottom; the horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the total population, increasing outwards from the centre in both directions; bars touch; give a title and label both axes.

Read a pyramid in a fixed order: **shape → base → apex → sides → one figure quoted with its unit**, comparing males with females, working with dependent ages, and young with old.

| Feature seen | What it means |
|---|---|
| Wide base | high birth rate, youthful population |
| Base narrower than the bar above it | birth rate recently falling |
| Steeply tapering, concave sides | high death rate at every age, short life expectancy |
| Straight, near-vertical sides | low death rate until old age |
| Wide apex, more women than men at the top | ageing population, high life expectancy; women live longer |
| A notch in one cohort | past war, epidemic, or a sharp dip in births |
| A bulge of young adult men | in-migration of male workers |

| Pyramid shape | DTM stage | Typical country |
|---|---|---|
| Wide base, narrow concave triangle | 2 | Niger |
| Wide base but narrowing at the bottom, straighter sides | 3 | India |
| Column (beehive) with roughly equal bars to old age | 4 | New Zealand |
| Narrow base and wide top (inverted) | 5 | Japan |

| Dependency group | Ages | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Young dependants | 0–14 | schools, child health, future workers |
| Economically active | 15–64 | taxpayers, producers |
| Elderly dependants | 65+ | pensions, healthcare, residential care |

$$\text{dependency ratio} = \frac{(0\text{–}14) + (65+)}{15\text{–}64}\times100$$

**Worked example.** 3 200 000 young, 1 100 000 elderly, 5 500 000 active: $(3\,200\,000 + 1\,100\,000)\div5\,500\,000\times100 = 78$ dependants per 100 people of working age.

## Over-population, under-population and optimum

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Over-population | too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards fall |
| Under-population | too few people to use the resources fully, so potential output is lost |
| Optimum population | the number that gives the highest standard of living with the resources available |

These are about the **population–resource balance**, not about density. A densely populated place with high technology and trade can be comfortably supported; a sparsely populated place with poor soils and no capital can still be over-populated. A 2024 essay asked whether "Auckland is overpopulated" — the evaluation turns on whether housing, transport and water infrastructure keep pace, not on the raw number.

## Youthful and ageing populations

| | Youthful population | Ageing population |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | high birth and fertility rates, falling infant mortality | long life expectancy plus low fertility |
| Pyramid | wide base, narrow apex | narrow base, wide apex |
| Pressure on | schools, child healthcare, housing, future jobs, food and water | pensions, hospitals, residential care |
| Economy | large future workforce, but high young dependency now | shrinking workforce, higher taxes per worker, labour shortages |
| Opportunity | cheap, large labour force attracts investment | experienced workers, "grey" consumer spending, volunteering, grandparent childcare |
| Risk | unemployment, squatter settlements, food and water stress | falling demand, rural depopulation, a smaller army and tax base |

| Policy type | Aim | Typical measures |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-natalist | reduce the birth rate | family planning and free contraception, education of girls, incentives for small families, limits on family size |
| Pro-natalist | raise the birth rate | child payments, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, tax relief, housing help |
| Migration-based | fill a workforce gap | skilled-migration visas, points systems, recruiting specific occupations |
| Ageing-focused | manage dependency | raising the retirement age, private pension saving, retraining older workers, automation and robots |

| Example | Situation | Policy | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **China** | rapid growth after 1950 | **one-child policy** 1979–2015; two children from 2016, three from 2021 | births cut sharply, but the side effects — a heavily male sex ratio, a rapidly ageing population, a shrinking workforce — forced the reversal; the population began to fall in 2022 and the pro-natalist incentives have barely raised births |
| **Niger** | the world's highest fertility, about half the population under 15 | family planning and girls' education promoted, but uptake is low | early marriage, rural poverty and religious and cultural norms keep fertility high: policy without development has little effect |
| **Bangladesh** | over six children per woman in the 1970s | door-to-door family-planning workers, free contraception, girls' schooling, women's employment in the garment industry | fertility fell to about two — one of the clearest successes, because policy went with rising education and jobs for women |
| **Japan** | about 29% aged 65 or over, fertility about 1.2 | childcare and parental-leave support, higher retirement age, robots in care, a new visa for foreign workers (2019) | births have kept falling; migration is limited by cultural reluctance, so the workforce keeps shrinking |
| **Germany** | ageing, low fertility | migration — over a million asylum seekers in 2015–16, mainly from Syria | filled labour gaps and lowered the average age, but integration costs and political tension rose |

*Evaluating a policy* means naming the intended effect, the measured effect, the side effects, and the groups who gained and lost — not listing the measures.

## Migration

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Migration | a permanent or semi-permanent change of residence |
| Emigration | leaving a country — the **source** loses people |
| Immigration | entering a country — the **destination** gains people |
| Net migration | immigration minus emigration over a given period |
| Internal migration | a move within one country, e.g. rural to urban |
| International migration | a move across a national border |
| Voluntary migration | the migrant chooses to move, usually for work or family |
| Forced migration | the migrant has no real choice: war, persecution, disaster |
| Refugee | a person who has fled their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution or conflict |
| Asylum seeker | a person who has applied for refugee status in another country and is awaiting a decision |
| Internally displaced person | forced to move but still inside their own country |
| Economic migrant | moves mainly to improve income or employment |
| Climate refugee | forced to move by the effects of climate change, such as sea-level rise or worsening floods |

**Lee's push–pull model** sets the push factors at the origin against the pull factors at the destination, with **intervening obstacles** in between — distance, cost, border controls and visas, language, lack of information, family ties. The decision also depends on **personal factors** such as age, family and perception, which is why two people facing the same conditions make different choices.

| Push factors | Pull factors |
|---|---|
| unemployment, low wages | jobs, higher wages |
| poor housing, schools and healthcare | better housing, schools, hospitals |
| crop failure, drought, flood, sea-level rise | reliable food supply, safer environment |
| war, persecution, discrimination | safety, political freedom |
| isolation, few prospects | family and community already there, "bright lights" |

| Impact | Source area | Destination area |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | less pressure on jobs, land and services; remittances sent home; returning migrants bring skills and savings | labour shortages filled, skills gained, cultural diversity, a younger age structure, more taxpayers |
| Negative | loss of young, skilled workers (brain drain); fewer taxpayers; an older, more dependent and gender-imbalanced population; farms and services decline; families split | pressure on housing, schools and transport; downward pressure on some wages; social tension; costs of integration |

**Problems when large numbers emigrate** — a 5-mark question: loss of the workforce and lower production; loss of skilled people such as doctors and teachers; fewer taxpayers, so less money for services; a higher dependency ratio as young adults leave; an uneven gender balance when mostly men leave; family break-up; less demand for goods; less attraction for foreign investors; slower economic development.

**New Zealand's population change.** Natural increase has always been positive since the 1960s but has fallen steadily as fertility dropped; net migration swings sharply, sometimes negative (when many New Zealanders leave for Australia) and sometimes the larger contributor, as in the high-immigration years of the mid-2010s.

**Named examples.** **Samoa to New Zealand** — an international flow driven by limited jobs in Samoa and pulled by family networks and work in New Zealand, managed through the Samoan Quota and seasonal-work schemes; Samoa gains remittances but loses young workers, and New Zealand gains labour and a large Pacific community. **Syria** — a refugee flow caused by the civil war from 2011, with millions of refugees in Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan and over a million asylum seekers reaching Europe, particularly Germany, in 2015–16.

*Causal chain, rural-to-urban migration in an LIC.* Mechanisation, land concentrated in large estates and drought cut rural employment → rural wages and services fall behind → perceived urban opportunity, reinforced by family contacts and media → young adults move to the city → formal housing cannot be built fast enough → informal settlement on marginal land (steep slopes, flood-prone ground) → services lag behind → the migrants are of child-bearing age, so natural increase inside the city adds to the growth.

## Settlement patterns, site and hierarchy

| Pattern | Appearance | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| Dispersed | isolated farms and houses, no centre | large landholdings, difficult relief, pastoral farming |
| Nucleated | buildings clustered round a centre | defence, a single water source, a crossroads, cooperative farming |
| Linear | a line of buildings | along a road, river, valley floor, dyke or coastline |
| Clustered | several tight groups | separate water points or patches of better land |
| Rural / urban | small settlements with mainly farming functions / large settlements with industry and services | |

| Site factor | Why a settlement was placed there |
|---|---|
| Wet point | a reliable spring or well in a dry area |
| Dry point | a mound or terrace above the flood level |
| Defence | a hilltop, meander loop or island |
| Bridging point | the lowest or narrowest crossing of a river |
| Shelter | leeward of a hill, a sheltered harbour, south-facing slopes in the Northern Hemisphere |
| Resources | fuel, building stone, timber, fertile soil, gold |
| Route centre | a gap, pass, confluence or crossroads |

**Site** is the land the settlement is built on. **Situation** is its position relative to the surrounding region, routes and resources — and situation usually explains later *growth*. **Function** is what the settlement does: market town, port, resort, administrative centre, mining town.

**Why some settlements stay small** (a 4-mark question on a mountain village): little flat land to build on, steep slopes and snow make building and transport hard → growth is physically limited; isolation from main roads and railways → difficult to trade or commute; no industry → few jobs, so young people leave. Each reason needs its development to earn the second mark.

| Hierarchy level | Settlement | Services | Sphere of influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low order | hamlet, village | dairy, post box, primary school | small, local |
| Middle order | small town | supermarket, secondary school, doctor | district |
| High order | city, regional centre | department store, hospital, university | regional |
| Highest | primate city | specialist finance, national government, international airport | national and international |

- **Range** — the furthest a person will travel to use a service.
- **Threshold population** — the minimum number of customers a service needs to survive.
- **Sphere of influence** — the area from which people travel to a settlement or service.
- As you move up the hierarchy settlements become fewer, larger and further apart, with larger spheres of influence and more high-order services.

**Why a CBD has a large sphere of influence:** its shops and services are high and middle order — specialist, luxury and comparison goods that people buy infrequently, are willing to travel far for, and cannot find near home; there is a wide variety in one place; and the CBD is the most accessible point, at the focus of the transport network.

**New Zealand's urban hierarchy** (the named example):

| Level | Settlements | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Auckland | dominates population, industry, income, health, education and entertainment; sheltered harbours on two coasts, warm climate, main entry point to New Zealand, closest to the rest of the world |
| 2 | Wellington | seat of government and political power; central position; sheltered harbour |
| 3 | Christchurch | dominates the South Island; flat, productive Canterbury Plains; port at Lyttelton; formerly second, now third by population |
| 4 | Hamilton, Dunedin | Hamilton grew as the service town for the Waikato's dairy land and has overtaken Dunedin; Dunedin grew with the 1860s gold rush, its port and university, but has grown little since |
| 5 | Tauranga, Napier–Hastings, New Plymouth, Palmerston North, Rotorua, Invercargill, Nelson, Whangārei | regional centres |
| 6 | Gisborne, Whanganui, Ashburton, Timaru, Blenheim, Masterton, Queenstown, Taupō | district towns |
| 7 | Greymouth, Hokitika, Ōamaru, Levin, Taihape, Matamata, Whakatāne and similar | small service towns |

## Urbanisation, suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation

| Process | Definition |
|---|---|
| Urbanisation | an **increasing proportion** of a population living in urban areas |
| Urban growth | an increase in the urban **population or built-up area** |
| Suburbanisation | outward growth of housing onto the edge of the city, with commuting back in |
| Counter-urbanisation | movement of people from cities to smaller towns and rural areas |
| Re-urbanisation | movement back into the inner city, often after regeneration |
| Gentrification | higher-income residents buying and improving inner-city housing, raising prices |

**The history of urbanisation since 1700.**

| Period | Pattern | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| 1700–1850 | Britain urbanises first — over half its people were urban by about 1851; the world as a whole is still overwhelmingly rural | the industrial revolution: factories on coalfields, mechanised farming releasing labour, canals and railways |
| 1850–1950 | Europe, North America, Australia and New Zealand urbanise; world urban share rises to about 30% by 1950 | industrialisation spreading, mass immigration to cities, public transport |
| 1950–present | rapid urbanisation in LICs and MICs in Latin America, Asia and Africa; the world passed 50% urban around 2007 and is about 55% now; HICs are 75–90% urban and growing slowly | rural-to-urban migration plus high natural increase in the cities; growth of megacities over 10 million |
| Projected | about two-thirds of the world urban by 2050, nearly all the growth in Asia and Africa | |

*Causal chain, suburbanisation in an HIC.* Trams, railways and then car ownership cut the time cost of distance → cheaper land on the edge allows larger houses and gardens → families with rising incomes move out, commuting back to work → shops, schools and business parks follow → the city sprawls → the inner city loses its wealthier residents and its rates income, and its housing and services decay → the edge gains congestion, lost farmland and higher infrastructure costs.

**The rural–urban fringe** is the zone where the built-up area meets the countryside. After the 1960s it gained private housing estates on **greenfield** (never-built-on) sites, out-of-town retail parks, business parks, golf courses and motorway junctions, because land there is large, cheap and accessible. Conflict arises between developers and farmers, conservationists and existing residents. In Auckland the fringe has spread south and north-west across farmland.

## Urban land use (HIC)

| Zone | Characteristics | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| Central business district | shops, offices, high-rise, highest land values, few residents | the most accessible point, so the highest bid rent |
| Inner city / zone of transition | old terraced housing, former factories, derelict land, often deprivation | built in the 19th-century industrial phase near the factories; now obsolete |
| Inner suburbs | older, smaller housing for workers | within walking or tram distance of the old jobs |
| Outer suburbs | semi-detached and detached housing, gardens, lower density | cheaper land, car and rail access, demand for space |
| Rural–urban fringe | out-of-town retail, business parks, new estates | large cheap sites, motorway access, greenfield land |
| Industry | along rivers, railways and motorways; modern estates on the fringe | transport access and large flat sites |

- **Burgess (concentric zone) model** — rings around the CBD: zone of transition, low-income housing, medium-income housing, high-income commuter zone. Age of buildings falls and wealth rises outwards.
- **Hoyt (sector) model** — wedges radiating along transport routes from the CBD, so industry and low-income housing follow a railway or river corridor, while high-income housing occupies a separate sector away from the industry.
- **Core–frame model** of the CBD — an intensively used **core** of department stores, prime retail, offices and banks with the tallest buildings and the highest land values, surrounded by a **frame** of car parks, bus and rail stations, warehousing, small workshops and car showrooms that need more space and less footfall.
- **Latin American city model** — a CBD with a commercial **spine** and an élite residential sector extending outwards along it; around this, zones of maturity (established housing), in-situ accretion (housing being improved), and on the edge the peripheral **squatter settlements** of the poorest residents, with disamenity zones on the worst land.

**Bid-rent theory** explains the pattern: land is most expensive at the centre because it is the most accessible point. Retailers' profits depend most on passing customers, so they outbid everyone for the centre; industry and offices take the next ring; housing occupies the cheaper outer land, where each household can afford the most space. Secondary peaks occur where ring roads and main roads cross.

In LIC and MIC cities the social gradient is **reversed** compared with the Burgess model: the wealthy live close to the centre and the poorest on the edge, because the edge is where unclaimed, unwanted land is available and services are fewest.

## Urban problems and solutions

| Problem | Cause | Attempted solution | What to judge it on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing shortage, squatter settlements | growth faster than formal building | self-help schemes, site-and-service plots, slum upgrading, low-cost housing, intensification | tenure security, cost to residents, number actually housed |
| Poor water, sanitation and power | services not extended to informal areas | standpipes, sewerage extension, metered connections | coverage, reliability, affordability |
| Traffic congestion | dispersed homes and jobs, rising car use | public transport, rail and busways, ring roads, congestion charging, cycleways | journey times, uptake, induced extra traffic |
| Air and water pollution | vehicles, industry, untreated waste | emission limits, cleaner fuel, waste treatment | measured air and water quality |
| Urban sprawl, loss of farmland | demand for cheap land on the fringe | urban limits, green belts, brownfield redevelopment, intensification | land saved, effect on house prices |
| Inner-city decay | industry closed, wealthier residents left | redevelopment, regeneration agencies, gentrification | jobs and homes created, and whether the original residents benefited |
| Unemployment and informal work | more arrivals than formal jobs | training, micro-credit, support for small enterprise | jobs created, incomes raised |
| Crime and inequality | poverty and weak services | policing, community programmes, service investment | recorded crime, residents' own accounts |

**Named examples.**
- **Auckland** (HIC): growth on a narrow isthmus between two harbours forces sprawl north, west and south; long commutes, congestion, expensive housing and costly water and transport infrastructure. Responses include the Auckland Unitary Plan (2016) with a Rural Urban Boundary, intensification along rail and busway corridors, the Northern Busway, the Western Ring Route completed by the Waterview tunnels in 2017, and the City Rail Link. Intensification has costs too — strain on school rolls and services, and a change in neighbourhood character — and that is where the evaluation marks sit.
- **London Docklands** (HIC inner city): the docks closed between the 1960s and 1981, leaving derelict land and high unemployment; the London Docklands Development Corporation (1981–1998) attracted offices (Canary Wharf), housing and the Docklands Light Railway. Jobs and homes multiplied, but many new jobs went to commuters and house prices pushed out existing residents — gentrification.
- **Rio de Janeiro** (MIC): rural-to-urban migration, especially from Brazil's poor, drought-prone north-east, plus natural increase, created favelas on steep hillsides and flood-prone land. Responses moved from clearance to **upgrading** — the Favela-Bairro programme from the mid-1990s added paved paths, drains, water, sewerage and community buildings while keeping residents in place.

**Impacts of rural-to-urban migration in an LIC**

| | On the city | On the rural communities left behind |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | a large, cheap workforce for industry and services; enterprise in the informal sector; enough people to support services | remittances sent home; less pressure on land and food; returning migrants bring skills and ideas |
| Negative | squatter settlements, overloaded water, sanitation, schools and transport; congestion, pollution, unemployment and crime | loss of young adults, especially men, leaving an ageing, female and child population; farm labour shortages; schools and services close as numbers fall |

**Rural strategies to slow migration** reduce the push: rural electrification, clean water, schools and clinics in villages, land reform, micro-credit for farmers, and better farm prices — the cheapest way to take pressure off the city is to make staying worthwhile.

## Squatter settlements

| Characteristic | Issue it causes |
|---|---|
| self-built from scrap materials, later brick | fire, collapse in storms or landslides |
| on steep slopes, river banks, near rubbish tips — land nobody else wants | landslides and floods |
| no legal title to the land | no incentive to improve, threat of eviction |
| no piped water, sewerage or rubbish collection | water-borne disease such as cholera and diarrhoea |
| illegal electricity connections | fires and electrocution |
| overcrowding | disease spreads fast |
| informal-sector jobs | low, irregular income, no protection |
| strong community, self-help and enterprise | a basis for upgrading rather than clearance |

The **informal economy** (street trading, waste picking, casual work) employs most people in squatter settlements. It is good for the people because it provides an income and needs no qualifications, but bad for the country because it pays no tax and offers no security.

## Urban fieldwork

Typical enquiries: *land use changes with distance from the CBD*; *environmental quality is lower in the inner city*; *the sphere of influence of a shop increases with its order*. Methods: land-use mapping along a transect from the CBD, building-height and age surveys, pedestrian and traffic counts at fixed times, environmental-quality scoring, and questionnaires asking where shoppers have come from. Present on maps, bar graphs and divided bar graphs; draw conclusions against the hypothesis; evaluate the sample size, time of day and weather. A Term 2 paper used a traffic survey before and after a by-pass: earlier survey results from another source are **secondary data**, and the conclusion needed paired figures from the same times of day.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** "The north has a higher density" is description. Explanation needs *because* and a mechanism.
- **Giving a trend with no data.** Always anchor a trend to a figure from the resource, with its unit and its year.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit.** "28" is worth nothing; "28 births per 1000 per year" is worth the mark.
- **Confusing urbanisation with urban growth**, or **migration with net migration**.
- **Treating the Demographic Transition Model as a prediction** rather than a generalisation, and forgetting it excludes migration.
- **Naming a model instead of explaining it.** "Burgess model" is not an explanation of why the CBD has the highest land values.
- **Comparing by writing two separate descriptions.** A comparison needs *whereas*, *higher than*, *by contrast*.
- **Listing policy measures without judging them.** Evaluation needs criteria, evidence and a verdict.
- **Using an example as a tag.** "As seen in Auckland…" earns nothing; a fact about Auckland that supports the point does.
