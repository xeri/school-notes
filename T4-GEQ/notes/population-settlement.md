# Population and settlement

## Population density and distribution

**Density** is how many people per unit area — a number. **Distribution** is where those people are — a pattern. A question asking you to *describe the distribution* wants locations and the pattern between them, not a single density figure.

$$\text{population density} = \frac{\text{total population}}{\text{area in km}^2}$$

**Worked example.** A region of 2400 people in 300 km²: $2400 \div 300 = 8$ people/km².

| Factor | Encourages high density | Encourages low density |
|---|---|---|
| Relief | flat lowland, gentle slopes | steep mountains, high altitude |
| Soil | deep, fertile alluvial soil | thin, stony, waterlogged or frozen soil |
| Climate | moderate temperature, reliable rainfall | arid, very cold, very wet |
| Water | reliable supply, navigable river | no surface water |
| Resources | minerals, fuel, timber, fishing grounds | no exploitable resource |
| Economic | jobs, industry, port, trade routes | remote, no employment |
| Social | services, schools, hospitals | isolation, no services |
| Political | stability, investment, planned growth | conflict, insecurity |

Describe density with the vocabulary of the map: *densely populated*, *sparsely populated*, *moderate*, with named places and a direction or distance.

## Population change

$$\text{population change} = (\text{births} - \text{deaths}) \pm \text{net migration}$$

| Measure | Definition | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | live births per 1000 people per year | $\dfrac{\text{births}}{\text{population}}\times1000$ |
| Death rate | deaths per 1000 people per year | $\dfrac{\text{deaths}}{\text{population}}\times1000$ |
| Natural increase rate | birth rate minus death rate | $\text{BR} - \text{DR}$ (per 1000) |
| Natural increase % | the same, as a percentage | $\dfrac{\text{BR}-\text{DR}}{10}$ |
| Net migration | immigrants minus emigrants | per 1000, or as a total |
| Fertility rate | average children per woman over her lifetime | not per 1000 |

**Worked example.** BR 28/1000, DR 9/1000. Natural increase $= 28 - 9 = 19$ per 1000, i.e. $19/10 = 1.9\%$ per year. If net migration is $-3$ per 1000, total change $= 19 - 3 = 16$ per 1000.

| Raises birth rate | Lowers birth rate |
|---|---|
| children needed as farm labour | mechanised farming, children in school |
| high infant mortality, so parents have more | low infant mortality |
| no pensions, children support parents in old age | state pensions and healthcare |
| little education or employment for women | educated women marrying and working later |
| religion or custom favouring large families | cheap, available contraception |
| pro-natalist government policy | anti-natalist policy, cost of raising a child |

| Raises death rate | Lowers death rate |
|---|---|
| famine, unsafe water, poor sanitation | clean water, sewerage, vaccination |
| disease, including HIV/AIDS | medical care, antiretroviral treatment |
| war and conflict | peace and stable government |
| very youthful or very aged structure | balanced structure, good diet |

## The Demographic Transition Model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Total population |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 High fluctuating | high | high, fluctuating | roughly zero | low, stable |
| 2 Early expanding | high | falling rapidly | very high | rising fast |
| 3 Late expanding | falling rapidly | low | falling | still rising, more slowly |
| 4 Low fluctuating | low, fluctuating | low | roughly zero | high, stable |
| 5 Declining | very low | low, slightly rising | negative | slowly falling |

*Causal chain through the model.* Clean water, sanitation and medicine cut the death rate first → births stay high because family expectations change slowly → the gap between the curves is the population boom of stage 2 → education, urban living, falling infant mortality and contraception then cut the birth rate → the gap closes in stage 4 → where fertility stays below replacement the structure ages and stage 5 begins.

Limitations: it is based on the history of industrialised countries, it ignores migration entirely, and the timing of each stage varies, so a country can move through stages 2 and 3 far faster than the original European pattern.

## Population pyramids

Read a pyramid in a fixed order: **shape → base → apex → sides → one figure quoted with its unit**.

| Feature seen | What it means |
|---|---|
| Wide base | high birth rate, youthful population |
| Base narrower than the bar above it | birth rate recently falling |
| Steeply tapering sides | high death rate at every age, short life expectancy |
| Straight, near-vertical sides | low death rate until old age |
| Wide apex | ageing population, high life expectancy |
| A notch in one cohort | past war, epidemic, or a sharp dip in births |
| Male bulge in working ages | in-migration of male labour |

| Dependency group | Ages | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Young dependants | 0–14 | schools, child health, future workers |
| Economically active | 15–64 | taxpayers, producers |
| Elderly dependants | 65+ | pensions, healthcare, residential care |

$$\text{dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{(0--14)} + \text{(65+)}}{\text{15--64}}\times100$$

**Worked example.** 3 200 000 young, 1 100 000 elderly, 5 500 000 active: $(3\,200\,000 + 1\,100\,000)\div5\,500\,000\times100 = 78$ dependants per 100 workers.

## Over-population, under-population and optimum

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Over-population | too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards fall |
| Under-population | too few people to use the resources fully, so potential output is lost |
| Optimum population | the number that gives the highest standard of living with the resources available |

These are about the **population–resource balance**, not about density. A densely populated place with high technology and trade can be comfortably supported; a sparsely populated place with poor soils and no capital can still be over-populated.

## Youthful and ageing populations

| | Youthful population | Ageing population |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | high birth and fertility rates, falling infant mortality | long life expectancy plus low fertility |
| Pyramid | wide base, narrow apex | narrow base, wide apex |
| Pressure on | schools, child healthcare, housing, future jobs | pensions, hospitals, residential care |
| Economy | large future workforce, but high young dependency now | shrinking workforce, higher taxes per worker |
| Opportunity | cheap, large labour force attracts investment | experienced workers, "grey" consumer spending, volunteering |
| Risk | unemployment, squatter settlements, food and water stress | labour shortage, falling demand, rural depopulation |

| Policy type | Aim | Typical measures |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-natalist | reduce the birth rate | family planning, contraception, education of girls, incentives for small families, limits on family size |
| Pro-natalist | raise the birth rate | child payments, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, tax relief, housing help |
| Migration-based | fill a workforce gap | skilled-migration visas, points systems, recruitment of specific occupations |
| Ageing-focused | manage dependency | raising the retirement age, encouraging private saving, retraining older workers |

China's one-child policy ran from 1979 to 2015 and was replaced by a two-child and then, in 2021, a three-child policy — a worked illustration of an anti-natalist policy whose success created the opposite problem. Several HICs, including Japan, Italy and Germany, now face the ageing end of the same spectrum.

*Evaluating a policy* means naming the intended effect, the measured effect, the side effects, and the groups who gained and lost — not simply listing the measures.

## Migration

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Migration | a permanent or semi-permanent change of residence |
| Emigration | leaving a country — the **source** loses people |
| Immigration | entering a country — the **destination** gains people |
| Net migration | immigration minus emigration |
| Internal migration | a move within one country, e.g. rural to urban |
| International migration | a move across a national border |
| Voluntary migration | the migrant chooses to move, usually for work or family |
| Forced migration | the migrant has no real choice: war, persecution, disaster |
| Refugee | a person who has fled their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution or conflict |
| Internally displaced person | forced to move but still inside their own country |
| Economic migrant | moves mainly to improve income or employment |

**Lee's push–pull model** sets push factors at the origin against pull factors at the destination, with **intervening obstacles** in between — distance, cost, border controls, language, lack of information, family ties. The decision also depends on the migrant's **personal perception**, which is why two people facing the same conditions make different choices.

| Push factors | Pull factors |
|---|---|
| unemployment, low wages | jobs, higher wages |
| poor housing and services | better housing, schools, hospitals |
| crop failure, drought, flood | reliable food supply, safer environment |
| war, persecution, discrimination | safety, political freedom |
| isolation, few prospects | family and community already there, "bright lights" |

| Impact | Source area | Destination area |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | less pressure on jobs, land and services; remittances sent home; returning migrants bring skills | labour supply filled, skills gained, cultural diversity, younger age structure, taxes paid |
| Negative | loss of young, skilled workers (brain drain); ageing, male- or female-depleted structure; farms and services decline | pressure on housing, schools and transport; downward pressure on some wages; social tension; costs of integration |

*Causal chain, rural-to-urban migration in an LIC.* Mechanisation and land fragmentation cut rural employment → rural wages and services fall behind → perceived urban opportunity, amplified by family contacts and media → young adults move to the city → formal housing cannot be built fast enough → informal settlement on marginal land (steep slopes, flood-prone ground) → services lag behind → further natural increase inside the city because the migrants are of child-bearing age, so the city grows faster than the migration flow alone would suggest.

## Settlement patterns, site and hierarchy

| Pattern | Appearance | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| Dispersed | isolated farms and houses, no centre | large landholdings, difficult relief, pastoral farming |
| Nucleated | buildings clustered round a centre | defence, a single water source, a crossroads, cooperative farming |
| Linear | a line of buildings | along a road, river, valley floor, dyke or coastline |
| Clustered | several tight groups | separate water points or patches of better land |

| Site factor | Why a settlement was placed there |
|---|---|
| Wet point | a reliable spring or well in a dry area |
| Dry point | a mound or terrace above the flood level |
| Defence | a hilltop, meander loop or island |
| Bridging point | the lowest or narrowest crossing of a river |
| Shelter | leeward of a hill, a sheltered harbour |
| Resources | fuel, building stone, timber, fertile soil |
| Route centre | a gap, pass, confluence or crossroads |

**Site** is the land the settlement is built on. **Situation** is its position relative to the surrounding region, and situation usually explains later *growth*.

| Hierarchy level | Settlement | Services | Sphere of influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low order | hamlet, village | dairy, post box, primary school | small, local |
| Middle order | small town | supermarket, secondary school, doctor | district |
| High order | city, regional centre | department store, hospital, university | regional |
| Highest | primate city | specialist finance, national government, international airport | national |

- **Range** — the furthest a person will travel to use a service.
- **Threshold population** — the minimum number of customers a service needs to survive.
- As settlements rise through the hierarchy they become fewer, larger and further apart, with larger spheres of influence and more high-order services.

New Zealand's hierarchy is dominated by Auckland, which holds roughly a third of the national population and leads in industry, health, education and entertainment; Wellington follows as the seat of government; Christchurch dominates the South Island; Hamilton and Dunedin form the next level; then regional centres such as Tauranga, Napier–Hastings, New Plymouth, Palmerston North and Invercargill; then small towns and service villages.

## Urban land use

| Zone | Characteristics | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| Central business district | shops, offices, high-rise, highest land values, low residential population | maximum accessibility, so highest bid rent |
| Inner city / transition zone | old terraced housing, former factories, derelict land, often deprivation | built in the 19th-century industrial phase, now obsolete |
| Suburbs | semi-detached and detached housing, gardens, lower density | cheaper land, car and rail access, demand for space |
| Rural–urban fringe | out-of-town retail, business parks, golf courses, new estates | large cheap sites, motorway access, greenfield land |
| Industry | riverside, rail or motorway corridors; modern estates on the fringe | transport access and large flat sites |

- **Burgess (concentric zone) model** — rings of decreasing age and density outwards from the CBD.
- **Hoyt (sector) model** — wedges radiating along transport routes, so industry and low-cost housing follow a rail or river corridor.
- **Core–frame model** of the CBD — an intensively used core of prime retail and offices, with a frame of car parks, bus stations, warehousing and small workshops.
- **Latin American (Griffin–Ford) model** — a modern CBD with a commercial spine and élite housing extending outwards along it, surrounded by zones of maturity, in-situ accretion, and then peripheral squatter settlements of the poorest residents.

**Bid-rent theory** explains the pattern: land value falls with distance from the CBD, but retailing's profits depend most sharply on footfall, so retailing outbids industry for the centre, industry outbids housing in the middle, and housing occupies the cheap outer land where space per household is greatest.

In LIC and MIC cities the social gradient is **reversed** compared with the classic HIC models: the wealthy live close to the centre and the poorest on the edge.

## Urbanisation, suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation

| Process | Definition |
|---|---|
| Urbanisation | an **increasing proportion** of a population living in urban areas |
| Urban growth | an increase in the urban **population or built-up area** |
| Suburbanisation | outward growth of housing onto the edge of the city, with commuting back in |
| Counter-urbanisation | movement of people from cities to smaller towns and rural areas |
| Re-urbanisation | movement back into the inner city, often after regeneration |
| Gentrification | higher-income residents buying and improving inner-city housing, raising prices |

Urbanisation and urban growth are not the same thing: a city can grow while national urbanisation stalls, and the global urban share passed 50% at around 2007 on United Nations estimates.

*Causal chain, suburbanisation in an HIC.* Rail and then car ownership cut the time cost of distance → cheaper peripheral land allows larger houses and gardens → families with rising incomes move out → shops and schools follow the population → the inner city loses its wealthier residents and its rate income → inner-city housing and services decay → the fringe gains congestion, lost farmland and higher infrastructure costs.

## Problems of rapid urban growth and the attempted solutions

| Problem | Cause | Attempted solution | What to judge it on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing shortage, squatter settlements | growth faster than formal building | self-help schemes, site-and-service plots, slum upgrading, low-cost housing | tenure security, cost to residents, number actually housed |
| Poor water, sanitation and power | services not extended to informal areas | standpipes, sewerage extension, metered connections | coverage, reliability, affordability |
| Traffic congestion | dispersed homes and jobs, rising car use | public transport, rail and busways, ring roads, congestion charging, cycleways | journey times, uptake, induced extra traffic |
| Air and water pollution | vehicles, industry, untreated waste | emission limits, cleaner fuel, waste treatment | measured air and water quality |
| Urban sprawl, loss of farmland | demand for cheap land on the fringe | urban limits, green belts, brownfield redevelopment, intensification | land saved, effect on house prices |
| Unemployment and informal work | more arrivals than formal jobs | training, micro-credit, support for small enterprise | jobs created, incomes raised |
| Crime and inequality | poverty and weak services | policing, community programmes, service investment | recorded crime, residents' own accounts |

Auckland shows the HIC version: sprawl across its isthmus, congestion, and infrastructure cost, met with intensification around transport corridors, motorway investment and an urban limit — and intensification has its own costs in school capacity, service demand and neighbourhood character, which is exactly where the evaluation marks sit. Rio de Janeiro shows the LIC/MIC version: favelas on steep hillsides, with upgrading rather than clearance as the main response since the 1990s.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** "The north has a higher density" is description. Explanation needs *because* and a mechanism.
- **Giving a trend with no data.** Always anchor a trend to a figure from the resource, with its unit and its year.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit.** "28" is worth nothing; "28 births per 1000 per year" is worth the mark.
- **Confusing urbanisation with urban growth**, or **migration with net migration**.
- **Treating the Demographic Transition Model as a prediction** rather than a generalisation, and forgetting it excludes migration.
- **Naming a model instead of explaining it.** "Burgess model" is not an explanation of why the CBD has the highest land values.
- **Listing policy measures without judging them.** Evaluation needs criteria, evidence and a verdict.
