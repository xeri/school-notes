# Named models, processes and solutions: the index

Every named model, process, landform and solution in the course, one row each, grouped by exam section. The full explanations and diagrams live on the topic pages; this page links to them and gives you the minimum to say in an answer.

**How to use a row:** read it → close the page → draw the diagram from memory → say the three steps (cause → process → result) → add one strength or limitation and the named example → check.

- **Diagram** links open the existing diagram. "Draw: …" means there is no diagram in the notes; sketch what is described.
- **Strength / limitation** is given for models and solutions; processes have none.
- Rows marked **(thin in notes — learn from here)** are only a line or two on the topic pages. They have a fuller explanation in [Fuller explanations](#fuller-explanations) at the bottom of this page.
- Which section each topic belongs to, and which look-alike terms to keep apart, is on [Overlaps](../notes/overlaps.md).

## Fuller explanations

**Braided channel.** A braided river runs in many shallow threads that split and rejoin round gravel bars (eyots) across a wide, flat bed. It needs (1) a large load of **coarse bedload**, often from glaciers, scree or easily eroded banks; (2) a very **variable discharge**, high after snowmelt or storms and low otherwise; (3) a steep enough gradient to bring the gravel down. When discharge falls, the river loses competence and drops the coarsest gravel in mid-channel; the bar diverts the flow, which erodes the banks and widens the channel, so more bars form. The Canterbury rivers (Waimakariri, Rakaia) are classic examples, fed by the Southern Alps. Schumm's model predicts this shape for bedload rivers.

**Washlands.** A washland is part of the floodplain kept free of buildings so that it can flood on purpose. In a flood, water spills onto it and is stored; this lowers and delays the peak further downstream, protecting towns. The land is used for grazing, parks or wetland between floods. It is soft engineering: cheap and good for wildlife, but the land cannot be built on and farmers lose use of it for a while. On the lower Waikato, **ponding areas** on farmland do this job inside the flood-control scheme.

**Sub-aerial processes.** These are the land-based processes that attack a cliff from above, out of reach of the waves: weathering (freeze–thaw, salt crystallisation, wetting and drying, solution), rain wash and runoff, and mass movement (rockfall, slides, slumps). They loosen material and drop it to the cliff foot, where the waves remove it. Where wave erosion at the foot is faster than sub-aerial processes, cliffs stay steep; where sub-aerial processes dominate, cliffs become lower-angled and slope back.

**Coastal slumping.** Slumping is a rotational slide, common on cliffs of soft rock, clay or deep soil. Heavy rain soaks into the cliff → the material gets heavier and the water reduces friction between particles → a block breaks away along a **curved slip plane** and rotates downwards, tilting backwards and leaving a crescent-shaped scar and stepped terraces. Waves then remove the debris at the foot, steepening the cliff again so the cycle repeats. The slips at Muriwai in Cyclone Gabrielle (February 2023) show how extreme rain triggers coastal mass movement.

**Concordant and discordant coasts.** On a **concordant** coast the rock bands run **parallel** to the coastline. The outer resistant band protects the softer rock behind; if the sea breaks through at a weakness, it erodes the soft rock behind quickly into a rounded cove (Lulworth Cove, Dorset). On a **discordant** coast the bands run at **right angles** to the coast, so the sea meets hard and soft rock side by side: the soft rock is eroded back into bays and the hard rock is left as headlands (Swanage Bay, Dorset). This is differential erosion.

**Sand-dune succession.** Dunes become older, higher, more vegetated and more stable with distance inland.
1. **Embryo dunes**, just above the high-tide line: pioneer plants that tolerate salt, wind and burial (spinifex and pīngao in New Zealand) trap sand.
2. **Foredunes and yellow dunes**: marram grass grows up through new sand, binding it; the sand is still mobile and yellow because there is little humus.
3. **Grey dunes**: dead plant matter adds humus, darkening the sand; mosses, lichens, grasses and low shrubs grow; the dune is stable.
4. **Dune slacks**: damp hollows where the surface reaches the water table.
5. **Mature dunes**: scrub and finally forest, the climax vegetation.
Inland, salt and wind fall, humus and soil depth rise, and the number of species rises. Trampling a foredune breaks this sequence and starts a **blowout**, which is why dunes are fenced and replanted.

**Benioff zone.** At a destructive boundary the sinking oceanic plate grinds against the plate above. Earthquakes happen along this contact and within the sinking slab. Plotted on a cross-section, the foci form a band that slopes down from the trench under the overriding plate, from shallow near the trench to several hundred kilometres deep (down to about 700 km). This sloping band is the **Benioff (Wadati–Benioff) zone**; it shows the angle of subduction. Under the North Island, earthquake foci get deeper westwards from the Hikurangi subduction zone, which is how we know the Pacific plate is sinking westward beneath the Australian plate.

**Coriolis effect.** Because the Earth rotates, anything moving freely over its surface (air, ocean water) is deflected: to the **left in the Southern Hemisphere** and to the **right in the Northern**. The effect is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles. So air flowing from the subtropical high (about 30°) towards the equatorial low becomes the **south-east trades** south of the equator and the **north-east trades** north of it; air flowing polewards from 30° becomes the **westerlies** that cross New Zealand. It also makes winds circle lows and highs instead of blowing straight in or out: clockwise into a low in the Southern Hemisphere. Mark schemes cap a trade-wind answer at 3 marks without it.

**Cycle of urban change.** HIC cities pass through four stages, often overlapping:
1. **Urbanisation** — industry draws people in from the countryside.
2. **Suburbanisation** — transport and rising incomes let people move to new suburbs and commute.
3. **Counter-urbanisation** — congestion, crime and prices push people beyond the city to small towns and villages.
4. **Re-urbanisation** — regeneration, apartments and central jobs draw people back to the inner city.

Inside it runs the **cycle of decline and regeneration**: deindustrialisation → job losses → the wealthier leave → rates income falls and buildings decay → crime and a poor image keep investment away (a downward spiral) → regeneration spending breaks the spiral → gentrification and re-urbanisation. London Docklands went through every step: docks closed by 1981, the LDDC regenerated it (1981–98), and house prices rose and displaced older residents.

**Over-urbanisation.** Urbanisation is healthy when jobs and services grow with the city. Over-urbanisation is when the urban population grows **faster than the city's jobs, housing and infrastructure**, usually because rural push factors and urban natural increase drive growth rather than industrial pull. The results are squatter settlements, a large informal economy, unemployment and underemployment, and overloaded water, sanitation and transport. It describes many LIC cities (Lagos, Kinshasa, Dhaka), not HIC cities, which urbanised with industry.

**Cycle of poverty.** Low income means poor diet, poor health and little schooling, and no savings. Poor health and education lower productivity; no savings means no investment in better tools, seed or business. Low productivity and no investment keep income low, so the cycle repeats, and it passes to the next generation. It can be broken from any point: education (especially girls'), health care, micro-credit, fair prices or remittances.

**Multiplier effect.** An injection of money into an area — a new factory, mine or tourist resort — spreads beyond the first jobs. Workers spend their wages in local shops; suppliers win orders and hire; the council gains taxes and builds services; this attracts more firms. Each round adds jobs and income. It also works **in reverse**: when a major employer closes or cuts back, the loss spreads to suppliers and shops (a negative multiplier). VW Wolfsburg is a company town, so the planned 2030 job cuts threaten the whole local economy; in Vietnam, tourist spending supports farmers, builders and craft workers.
