# Water and the environmental risks of development

Theme 3: the school's topic list asks for "resources and their use" and "the impact of development and resource use on the environment — problems and solutions". This page gathers those ideas, plus water supply (IGCSE 0460 topic 3.6), and links them to the other Theme 3 pages. Named examples: Auckland's water supply, Canterbury's aquifers, and the examples on the farming, manufacturing, tourism, energy and global-warming pages.

## Resources and sustainable development

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Natural resource** | anything in nature people can use: water, soil, minerals, forests, fish, energy sources, land |
| **Renewable / non-renewable** | replenished naturally / finite (see [Energy](../notes/energy.md)) |
| **Resource depletion** | using a resource faster than it can be replaced (over-fishing, aquifer drawdown, deforestation) |
| **Sustainable development** | development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own |
| **Conservation** | protecting and managing resources and environments so they last |
| **Ecological footprint** | the area of productive land and sea needed to supply a person's or country's resources and absorb its waste; HICs have the largest |
| **Circular economy** | reduce, reuse, repair and recycle so materials stay in use instead of becoming waste |

**Why resource use rises with development:** population growth, rising incomes and consumption, industrialisation, urbanisation, more transport, globalisation of trade.

## Water supply

**Water use:** agriculture (about 70% of global freshwater withdrawals, mostly irrigation), industry (about 20%: cooling, processing, power), domestic (about 10%: drinking, cooking, washing). HICs use far more water per person; industry takes a bigger share in HICs, agriculture in LICs.

**Sources:** rivers and lakes; **reservoirs** behind dams; **groundwater** pumped from **aquifers** (wells, bores); rainwater tanks; **desalination** of sea water (expensive, energy-hungry — Middle East); recycled wastewater.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Water stress** | demand approaches or exceeds available supply |
| **Physical water scarcity** | not enough water exists (deserts, drought) |
| **Economic water scarcity** | water exists but people cannot afford the infrastructure to supply it (much of sub-Saharan Africa) |
| **Water security** | reliable access to enough safe water |

**Why water shortages happen:** low or unreliable rainfall, drought (El Niño), rising population and demand, irrigation, pollution making water unusable, over-pumping of aquifers, lack of money for dams, pipes and treatment, climate change.

**Effects:** disease from unsafe water (cholera, diarrhoea), time spent collecting water (often by women and girls), crop failure and food shortages, limits on industry, conflict over shared rivers.

**Managing supply and demand:**

| Increase supply | Reduce demand |
|---|---|
| dams and reservoirs (also HEP, flood control) — but displace people and trap sediment | metering and pricing |
| wells and boreholes (hand pumps in LICs are appropriate technology) | efficient irrigation (drip, sprinklers at night) |
| transfer schemes (pipelines between basins) | water-saving appliances, fixing leaks |
| desalination | recycling grey water; rainwater tanks |
| rainwater harvesting, small dams | restrictions in droughts; public education |

### Named example: Auckland's water supply

- Auckland's water comes from **dams in the Waitākere and Hūnua Ranges**, **groundwater** (Onehunga aquifer) and the **Waikato River** (a treatment plant near Tuakau).
- **2020 drought:** the driest start to a year on record left dam storage **below half**, so Watercare imposed **water restrictions** (May 2020 onwards: no hoses or sprinklers) and fast-tracked extra treatment capacity taking more water from the Waikato.
- **Issues:** a growing city, climate change (more drought), competition with farms and other users for Waikato water, and iwi interests in the river.

### Supporting example: Canterbury's aquifers

Irrigation for dairy has drawn heavily on Canterbury's rivers and aquifers; **nitrate** from fertiliser and urine has leached into groundwater used for drinking water in some areas, and lowland streams have shrunk. Responses: consent limits on water takes, irrigation schemes from alpine rivers, nitrogen caps and farm environment plans.

## Environmental risks of economic development

| Risk | Main causes | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Air pollution** | fossil-fuel power, industry, vehicles, burning (fires, crop stubble) | smog, acid rain, lung disease, global warming |
| **Water pollution** | industrial effluent, sewage, farm runoff (nitrate, phosphate, E. coli), mining, oil spills, plastics | eutrophication, fish deaths, unsafe drinking water, disease |
| **Soil degradation** | overgrazing, over-cultivation, deforestation, salinisation from irrigation, contamination | erosion, desertification, falling yields |
| **Deforestation and habitat loss** | farming, logging, mining, roads, dams, urban sprawl | biodiversity loss, carbon release, flooding |
| **Resource depletion** | over-fishing, aquifer drawdown, mining | collapse of fish stocks, water shortages |
| **Noise and visual pollution** | industry, airports, roads, wind farms, mining | stress, lower quality of life |
| **Waste** | consumer society, packaging, e-waste, landfill | methane, toxic leachate, ocean plastic (Great Pacific Garbage Patch) |
| **Climate change** | greenhouse-gas emissions from all of the above | see [Global warming](../notes/global-warming.md) |

**Where each sector's risks are covered:** farming (water pollution, extraction, soil erosion, desertification, methane) → [Farming](../notes/farming.md); manufacturing (air and water pollution, emissions) → [Manufacturing](../notes/manufacturing.md); tourism (erosion, waste, wildlife disturbance) → [Tourism](../notes/tourism.md); energy (mining, spills, dams, nuclear waste) → [Energy](../notes/energy.md); deforestation → [Ecosystems](../notes/ecosystems.md).

**Pollution in LICs and NICs vs HICs:** rapidly industrialising countries often have weaker laws or enforcement, older technology and a priority on growth, so pollution is often worst there (Chinese and Indian cities); HICs have stricter regulation and have "exported" some polluting industry (a **pollution haven** effect) — but HICs have the largest per-person consumption and emissions.

## Solutions: balancing development and the environment

| Approach | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Laws and regulation** | emission limits, resource consents, environmental impact assessments, protected areas, fines for polluters |
| **Economic tools** | carbon pricing and emissions trading, pollution taxes, subsidies for clean technology, deposit schemes |
| **Technology** | scrubbers and filters, wastewater treatment, renewable energy, electric vehicles, precision farming |
| **Planning** | zoning industry away from homes and sensitive areas; reusing brownfield sites; green belts |
| **Recycling and the circular economy** | reduce, reuse, recycle; product stewardship |
| **Conservation** | national parks, marine reserves, replanting, riparian planting, kaitiakitanga (Māori guardianship of the environment) |
| **International co-operation** | Paris Agreement, Montreal Protocol, the Sustainable Development Goals |
| **Individual action** | consumption, transport and diet choices |

*Essay framing:* "Environmental damage is the price of development" — weigh the gains (jobs, incomes, services, poverty reduction) against the costs (health, lost resources, climate), consider who pays (often the poor and future generations), and show that cleaner technology and regulation can reduce the trade-off, though often at a cost LICs find hard to bear.

## This loses marks

- "Pollution" with no type, source or effect.
- Solutions such as "be eco-friendly" with no method.
- Ignoring that views differ between countries at different levels of development.
