# The natural environment

The precise 2026 weather exclusions are unconfirmed, so the instruments are kept here in compact form; wind erosion is marked "not to be assessed" in the syllabus but still listed in the topics, and coral reefs are recorded as not examined.

## The geomorphic cycle

The landscape is simultaneously built and destroyed, and the debris is recycled.

| Stage | Processes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Land building | folding, faulting, volcanism | new relief: fold mountains, volcanoes, fault scarps |
| Land modifying | weathering, erosion, transport by water, ice and wind | relief worn down, valleys cut, sediment moved |
| Recycling | deposition, compaction and cementation; heat and pressure | sedimentary rock, then metamorphic rock, re-uplifted |

The cycle never finishes: uplift and denudation operate at the same time, and the shape you see is the current balance between them.

## Plate tectonics

| Layer | Composition | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Inner core | solid iron and nickel | extreme pressure keeps it solid |
| Outer core | liquid iron and nickel | generates the magnetic field |
| Mantle | dense semi-molten silicate rock | convection currents here drive plate movement |
| Crust | oceanic (thin, dense, basaltic) and continental (thick, less dense, granitic) | oceanic crust is destroyed and renewed; continental crust is not |

*Causal chain of plate movement.* Radioactive decay heats the lower mantle → heated rock expands, becomes less dense and rises → it spreads beneath the crust, cools, becomes denser and sinks → the resulting convection cell drags the plate above it → plates move a few centimetres a year.

| Boundary | Movement | Processes | Landforms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constructive (divergent) | plates pull apart | magma rises to fill the gap | mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, shield volcanoes, shallow quakes |
| Destructive (convergent, ocean–continent) | plates collide, denser oceanic plate subducts | melting in the subduction zone, friction | deep-sea trench, fold mountains, composite cones, deep violent quakes |
| Collision (continent–continent) | plates collide, neither subducts | sediment squeezed and uplifted | high fold mountains, strong quakes, no volcanoes |
| Conservative | plates slide past each other | friction, locking and sudden release | fault line, offset features, shallow quakes, no volcanoes |

**Folding** happens when compression buckles rock into upfolds (anticlines) and downfolds (synclines); sustained compression builds fold mountains. **Faulting** happens when rock fractures under tension or compression and the blocks move, producing fault scarps, block mountains and rift valleys.

| Magma type | Silica | Viscosity | Eruption | Landform |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basaltic | low | runny | frequent, gentle, lava flows | shield volcano, lava plateau |
| Andesitic | intermediate | sticky | explosive, ash and lava alternating | composite cone |
| Rhyolitic | high | very sticky | violently explosive, pyroclastic flow | lava dome, caldera |

Label a volcano with: magma chamber, main vent, side vent, crater, layers of ash and lava. Label an earthquake with: focus (where the rock ruptures), epicentre (the point on the surface directly above the focus), and intensity (the strength of shaking felt).

| | Effects on people | Effects on the environment |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake | collapsed buildings, deaths, injuries, homelessness, broken water, power and transport links, fire, lost business | landslides, liquefaction, subsidence, tsunami, altered drainage |
| Eruption | deaths from pyroclastic flow and gas, ash on crops and roofs, evacuation, lost tourism | vegetation destroyed, lahars, ash fall, longer-term soil fertility, cooler regional temperatures |

| Reducing the impact | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction and monitoring | seismometers, patterns of past events (no reliable short-term prediction) | gas emissions, ground deformation, tremor swarms — far more predictable |
| Protection | aseismic design, base isolation, cross-bracing, flexible gas pipes | strengthened roofs, lava diversion channels, barriers |
| Preparation | drills, emergency kits, trained services, building codes enforced | hazard maps, exclusion zones, evacuation routes and transport |
| Planning | avoid building on soft sediment or fault lines | restrict settlement on the hazard-mapped slopes |

Prediction is the weak link for earthquakes and the strong one for volcanoes; that difference is the heart of most evaluation answers. ## Weathering

**Weathering** breaks rock down *in place*. **Erosion** removes the loosened material. Confusing the two loses marks in almost every denudation question.

| Process | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Freeze–thaw | physical | water in a crack freezes, expands about 9%, prises the rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it |
| Exfoliation | physical | repeated daily heating and cooling expands and contracts the outer skin until it peels off in sheets |
| Pressure release | physical | removal of overlying rock lets the rock beneath expand and crack parallel to the surface |
| Biological | physical/chemical | root wedging, burrowing animals, acids from lichen |
| Carbonation | chemical | rainwater plus dissolved CO₂ forms weak carbonic acid, which dissolves calcium carbonate in limestone |
| Hydrolysis | chemical | slightly acidic water reacts with feldspar in granite, rotting it to clay |

| Landform | Rock | Formed by |
|---|---|---|
| Scree slope | any jointed rock | freeze–thaw fragments falling and accumulating at the cliff base |
| Exfoliation dome | granite in hot deserts | layers peeling off a massive rounded outcrop |
| Limestone pavement | limestone | carbonation widening joints into grikes, leaving blocks (clints) |
| Sink hole and cave | limestone | surface water exploiting a widened joint, dissolving downwards and then along bedding planes |
| Granite tor | granite | hydrolysis along widely spaced joints, leaving unweathered blocks upstanding once the clay is eroded away |

## Glaciers

*Causal chain of glacier formation.* Snow accumulates in a hollow where more falls in winter than melts in summer → the weight compresses the lower snow, expelling air → snow becomes névé, then granular firn, then dense blue glacier ice → once thick enough the ice deforms under its own weight and moves downslope.

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| Plucking | meltwater at the base refreezes onto rock, and the moving ice tears blocks away |
| Abrasion | embedded rock fragments scour the bedrock, polishing it and cutting striations |
| Freeze–thaw above the ice | shatters the valley sides, supplying debris onto the glacier surface |
| Rotational slip | ice rotating within a hollow deepens it into an armchair shape |

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Cirque | erosional | snow collects in a north- or south-facing hollow, freeze–thaw steepens the back wall, rotational slip and abrasion deepen the floor, leaving a rock lip |
| Arête | erosional | the knife-edge ridge where two cirques erode back-to-back |
| Pyramidal peak | erosional | three or more cirques eroding into the same summit |
| U-shaped (glacial trough) valley | erosional | ice fills a former V-shaped river valley and erodes the sides as well as the bed, truncating spurs |
| Hanging valley | erosional | a smaller tributary glacier erodes less deeply, so its valley is left above the main trough, often with a waterfall |
| Finger lake | erosional | a long, deep lake in an overdeepened section of the trough |
| Fiord | erosional | a glacial trough eroded below sea level and later drowned |
| Terminal moraine | depositional | debris dumped in a ridge at the furthest advance of the ice |
| Medial moraine | depositional | two lateral moraines merging where tributary glaciers join |

## Wind

Abrasion by wind-blown sand undercuts rock, and **surface deflation** lifts loose fine material away, lowering the surface into hollows. **Mushroom rocks** form because sand is carried in the lowest metre of the airstream, so the base of an outcrop is undercut faster than its top. **Dunes** form where an obstacle slows the wind, dropping sand that builds a gentle windward slope and a steep leeward slip face, migrating downwind by saltation.

## The drainage basin and the hydrologic cycle

A **drainage basin** is the area drained by one river and its tributaries, bounded by a **watershed** — the ridge of high ground separating it from the next basin.

| Element | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Precipitation | input | all water reaching the ground |
| Interception | store | water held on leaves and stems |
| Infiltration | flow | water soaking into the soil |
| Percolation | flow | water passing from soil into rock |
| Soil moisture / groundwater | store | water held in the soil and in bedrock |
| Throughflow | flow | lateral movement through the soil to the channel |
| Baseflow | flow | slow groundwater seepage sustaining the river between storms |
| Surface runoff (overland flow) | flow | water flowing over the surface when rain exceeds infiltration |
| Channel flow | flow | water in the river itself |
| Evaporation and transpiration | output | water returned to the atmosphere |
| Discharge | output | water leaving the basin through the channel |

$$\text{discharge }(Q) = \text{cross-sectional area} \times \text{mean velocity}$$

Measured in cumecs (m³/s). **Worked example.** Width 8 m, mean depth 0.75 m, mean velocity 1.2 m/s: area $= 8\times0.75 = 6$ m²; $Q = 6\times1.2 = 7.2$ m³/s.

## Flood hydrographs

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rising limb | the steep rise in discharge after the storm |
| Peak discharge | the maximum discharge reached |
| Peak rainfall | the maximum rainfall intensity |
| Lag time | the gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge |
| Falling (recessional) limb | the return towards normal flow |
| Baseflow | the discharge before and after the storm event |
| Bankfull discharge | the discharge that just fills the channel; beyond it, flooding |

| Factor | Short lag, high peak ("flashy") | Long lag, low peak |
|---|---|---|
| Rainfall | intense, prolonged, or falling on frozen or saturated ground | light, steady, on dry absorbent ground |
| Relief | steep slopes | gentle slopes |
| Rock | impermeable | permeable |
| Soil | thin, clay, already saturated | deep, sandy |
| Vegetation | bare ground, ploughed land | dense forest, high interception |
| Land use | urban, tarmac, roofs, drains | woodland, wetland, pasture |
| Basin shape and drainage density | circular, high density | elongated, low density |

*Causal chain, why urbanisation makes floods worse.* Tarmac and roofs are impermeable → infiltration falls and surface runoff rises → drains and gutters deliver water to the channel in minutes instead of hours → lag time shortens and peak discharge rises → the same rainfall now exceeds bankfull discharge → flooding, and the floodplain is already built on, so the damage is greater.

## River processes

| Erosion process | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Hydraulic action | the sheer force of moving water pushing into cracks, compressing trapped air |
| Abrasion (corrasion) | the load scraping and grinding against bed and banks |
| Attrition | load particles colliding with each other, becoming smaller and rounder downstream |
| Corrosion (solution) | chemical dissolving of soluble rock, notably limestone |
| Cavitation | air bubbles collapsing against the bank, each collapse a tiny shock wave |

| Transport process | Material |
|---|---|
| Solution | dissolved minerals |
| Suspension | fine clay and silt carried in the flow |
| Saltation | sand-sized grains bouncing along the bed |
| Traction | large boulders rolled and dragged along the bed |

The **Hjulström curve** plots velocity against particle size and shows three things: larger particles need higher velocities to be entrained; very fine clay also needs a surprisingly high velocity to lift because it is cohesive; and once moving, a particle stays in transport at a velocity *lower* than the one needed to pick it up. Deposition therefore happens whenever velocity falls below the settling velocity for that size — so the coarsest load is dropped first.

The **Bradshaw model** sets out the downstream changes: discharge, channel width, channel depth, velocity and load quantity all increase, while gradient, bed roughness and load particle size all decrease. **Schumm's work** relates channel shape and pattern to discharge and sediment load, so a change in either alters the channel form.

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| V-shaped valley | erosional | vertical erosion cuts down, freeze–thaw and mass movement retreat the sides |
| Interlocking spurs | erosional | the river winds round projections of harder rock it cannot erode through |
| Waterfall | erosional | hard rock over soft; the soft rock is undercut into a plunge pool, the hard cap is unsupported and collapses, and the fall retreats upstream leaving a gorge |
| Rapids | erosional | bands of alternating hardness causing a steep, turbulent, broken descent |
| Meander | both | faster flow on the outside bank erodes a river cliff; slower flow on the inside deposits a slip-off slope; corkscrew (helicoidal) flow transfers sediment across the channel |
| Ox-bow lake | both | the meander neck narrows, a flood cuts straight through, deposition seals both ends and leaves a cut-off loop |
| Flood plain | depositional | repeated flooding spreads fine alluvium across the valley floor; migrating meanders widen it |
| Levée | depositional | the coarsest sediment is dropped first at the channel edge during a flood, building a natural raised bank |
| Delta | depositional | the river meets standing water, velocity collapses, sediment accumulates faster than the sea can remove it, and the channel splits into distributaries |

## Rivers and people: opportunities, flooding and management

| Opportunity | Hazard |
|---|---|
| fertile alluvial soil for farming | flooding of homes and farmland |
| water for drinking, irrigation and industry | drought and low flow |
| hydroelectric power | bank erosion and loss of land |
| transport and trade | waterborne disease and pollution |
| fishing and recreation | channel change destroying infrastructure |

Rivers flood when inputs exceed the channel's capacity: prolonged or intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, saturated or frozen ground, deforestation, urbanisation, and a channel narrowed or shallowed by sediment or by building on the floodplain.

| Approach | Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | embankments and levées | immediate, protects known assets | expensive, raises risk downstream, catastrophic if overtopped |
| Hard | channel straightening and dredging | moves water away faster | shifts the problem downstream, damages habitat, needs repeating |
| Hard | flood-relief channel, dam and reservoir | controls flow, adds water supply and power | very high cost, displaces people, traps sediment |
| Soft | floodplain zoning | cheap, permanent, prevents new exposure | cannot undo existing development |
| Soft | afforestation and wetland restoration | raises interception and storage, improves habitat | slow, needs land, limited in extreme events |
| Soft | flood warning and preparation | cheap, saves lives | does not prevent property damage |
| Soft | managed retreat and voluntary buy-outs | removes exposure permanently | costly, politically difficult, communities broken up |

Cyclone Gabrielle in February 2023 brought extreme rainfall, river flooding, damaging winds and storm surge to the northern and eastern North Island of New Zealand, with the Esk Valley in Hawke's Bay among the worst-affected places and voluntary buy-outs among the responses considered afterwards. Bangladesh, on the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta, is the standard contrasting example of a low-income, low-lying, densely populated floodplain. Use class notes for figures.

## Weather and climate

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at one place over hours or days. **Climate** is the average pattern of weather over a long period, conventionally 30 years or more.

| Element | Unit | Instrument | Use and siting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | °C | maximum–minimum thermometer | inside a Stevenson screen; records the day's extremes |
| Humidity | % relative humidity | wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (hygrometer) | the wet bulb reads lower as water evaporates; the difference gives humidity |
| Rainfall | mm | rain gauge | sunk into short grass, rim above ground, away from buildings and trees |
| Wind speed | km/h or knots | anemometer | 10 m above open ground |
| Wind direction | compass point | wind vane | named for the direction the wind comes *from* |
| Sunshine | hours | sunshine recorder | unshaded; a lens burns a trace on a card |
| Pressure | millibars (hPa) | barometer | indoors, at a known altitude |

A **Stevenson screen** is a white, double-louvred wooden box on legs about 1.25 m above short grass, with the door facing the pole. White paint reflects radiation, louvres allow free air circulation while blocking direct sun and rain, the legs lift it clear of ground heating, and the fixed height makes readings comparable between stations.

| Cloud group | Height | Members | Weather signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | above about 6 km | cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus | thin, wispy, ice crystals; often an approaching warm front |
| Middle — "alto" | about 2–6 km | altostratus, altocumulus | sun visible as a bright spot; rain possible |
| Low | below about 2 km | stratus, stratocumulus, nimbostratus | nimbostratus is the main steady-rain producer |
| Vertical | low base, great depth | cumulus, cumulonimbus | cumulonimbus brings heavy showers, thunder, hail |

*Causal chain of cloud and rain.* The surface heats the air above it → the air expands, becomes less dense and rises → rising air cools adiabatically → at the dew point it reaches saturation → water vapour condenses onto condensation nuclei → droplets collide and coalesce until they are too heavy to be held up → precipitation.

| Rainfall type | Uplift mechanism | Typical cloud |
|---|---|---|
| Convectional (free convection) | strong surface heating lifting air | cumulonimbus; heavy afternoon showers |
| Relief / orographic (forced convection) | air forced to rise over a mountain barrier | stratus and nimbostratus; wet windward slope, rain shadow to lee |
| Frontal | warm, less dense air forced to rise over cold air at a front | nimbostratus along the warm front, cumulonimbus at the cold front |

**Dew** forms when the ground cools on a clear, calm night until the air in contact with it reaches its dew point and condenses on the surface. **Frost** is the same process where the dew point is below 0 °C, so the vapour freezes directly onto the surface.

*Causal chain of wind.* The Sun heats the surface unevenly → warmed air rises, leaving lower pressure at the surface → cooler air sinks elsewhere, creating higher pressure → air flows from high to low pressure, and that flow is wind → the steeper the pressure gradient (the closer the isobars) the stronger the wind → the Coriolis effect deflects it, so it spirals rather than flowing straight.

| Tri-cellular model | Latitude | Surface air | Climate produced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hadley cell, rising limb | 0° | low pressure, convergence | equatorial: hot, wet all year |
| Hadley cell, sinking limb | about 30° | high pressure, divergence | hot desert: hot, very dry |
| Ferrel cell, rising limb | about 60° | low pressure, polar front | temperate: changeable, frontal rain |
| Polar cell, sinking limb | 90° | high pressure | polar: cold, very dry |

On a **synoptic chart**: isobars join points of equal pressure; closely spaced isobars mean strong wind; a **low (depression)** has rising air, cloud, rain and fronts; a **high (anticyclone)** has sinking air, settled weather, clear skies, and frost or fog in winter.

Climate varies with **latitude** (angle of the Sun's rays and day length), **altitude** (temperature falls with height), **distance from the sea** (water heats and cools slowly, so coasts have cooler summers and milder winters than continental interiors), **ocean currents**, **prevailing winds** and **relief**.

New Zealand's climate is maritime and strongly westerly-dominated, grading from warm subtropical in the far north to cool temperate in the far south, with severe alpine conditions in the mountains. The Southern Alps force the prevailing westerlies to rise, so the West Coast is very wet while Canterbury and Central Otago lie in the rain shadow; sunshine is highest in the sheltered eastern and northern coastal areas.

## Ecosystems

An **ecosystem** is a community of living organisms interacting with the non-living parts of their environment — climate, relief, soil and water — as one system.

| | Tropical rainforest | Hot desert |
|---|---|---|
| Location | within about 5–10° of the equator; Amazon, Congo, Indonesia | about 15–30° north and south; Sahara, Arabian, Atacama, central Australia |
| Cause of location | rising air at the equatorial low-pressure belt | sinking, warming air of the subtropical high-pressure belt, plus cold offshore currents and continental interiors |
| Temperature | about 26–28 °C all year, tiny annual range | very high by day, often near freezing at night, large annual and daily range |
| Rainfall | heavy and all year, often over 2000 mm | very low, under about 250 mm, unreliable |
| Humidity | very high | very low |
| Vegetation | dense, layered: emergents, canopy, under-canopy, shrub, forest floor; buttress roots, drip-tip leaves, lianas, epiphytes | sparse, drought-adapted: succulents, deep or wide root systems, waxy or spiny leaves, short-lived flowering after rain |
| Soil | latosol: deep, red, leached, nutrient-poor; nutrients held in the biomass and cycled rapidly through rapid decomposition of litter | thin, stony, little organic matter, salts drawn to the surface |
| Biodiversity | the highest on land | low, but highly specialised |

*Causal chain, why rainforest soils are poor despite the lush vegetation.* High temperature and moisture make decomposition very rapid → nutrients are released and immediately taken up by shallow tree roots → almost all nutrients are stored in living biomass, not the soil → heavy rain leaches what remains downwards → remove the trees and the nutrient store leaves with them, so cleared land loses fertility within a few seasons.

| Deforestation: cause | Deforestation: effect |
|---|---|
| cattle ranching and commercial farming | loss of habitat and biodiversity, species extinction |
| logging for hardwood | soil erosion and gullying once roots and canopy are gone |
| mining and quarrying | rivers silted, water quality falls |
| road building and settlement | reduced interception and transpiration, so local rainfall falls and runoff rises |
| hydroelectric reservoirs | carbon stored in the trees released, adding to global warming |
| subsistence and shifting cultivation under population pressure | indigenous peoples lose land, livelihood and culture |

## Coasts

| Wave type | Constructive | Destructive |
|---|---|---|
| Height and length | low, long | high, short |
| Frequency | few per minute | many per minute |
| Action | strong swash, weak backwash — builds the beach | weak swash, strong backwash — removes the beach |

**Wave refraction** bends wave crests round a headland so that energy is concentrated on the headland and dissipated in the bay — which is why headlands erode and bays accumulate beaches. **Longshore drift** moves sediment along the coast where waves approach at an angle: swash carries material obliquely up the beach, backwash pulls it straight back down under gravity, and the net result is a zigzag movement along the shore.

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Headland and bay | erosional | alternating hard and soft rock at right angles to the coast; soft rock erodes back into bays, hard rock stands out |
| Cliff and wave-cut platform | erosional | hydraulic action and abrasion cut a wave-cut notch at the high-tide line, the overhang collapses, the cliff retreats and leaves a gently sloping rock platform |
| Cave, arch, stack, stump | erosional | waves exploit a joint or fault in a headland → crack widens to a cave → the cave is cut through to form an arch → the arch roof collapses leaving an isolated stack → the stack is undercut to a stump |
| Beach | depositional | constructive waves build up sand and shingle in a sheltered bay; shingle at the back, sand towards the sea |
| Spit | depositional | longshore drift carries sediment past a change in coastline direction or a river mouth into open water, where it accumulates above sea level; the end often curves landward under a second wave direction, with salt marsh in the sheltered water behind |
| Tombolo | depositional | a spit that grows far enough to join an island to the mainland |
| Sand dune | depositional | dry sand blown inland from the beach is trapped by driftwood or marram grass, builds up, is colonised and stabilised, forming successive ridges |

| Coastal opportunity | Coastal hazard |
|---|---|
| ports, trade and fishing | erosion and loss of land and buildings |
| tourism and recreation | storm surge and coastal flooding |
| flat land for settlement and industry | sea-level rise |
| salt, sand and aggregate extraction | tsunami on tectonic coasts |

| Management | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sea wall | reflects wave energy | strong, reassuring, doubles as a promenade | very expensive, reflects energy onto the beach, ugly |
| Rock armour (rip-rap) | absorbs and dissipates wave energy | cheaper, effective | rock often imported, can be moved by the largest storms |
| Groynes | trap sediment moving by longshore drift | builds the beach, which itself absorbs energy | starves the coast downdrift, moving the problem along |
| Beach nourishment | replaces sand lost to erosion | natural appearance, keeps the beach as a defence | needs repeating, dredging damages the source area |
| Managed retreat | lets the sea flood low-value land | cheap, creates salt-marsh habitat that absorbs energy | land and property lost, compensation disputes |
| Do nothing | accepts natural change | no cost | unacceptable where assets are exposed |

The Mangawhai Heads spit in Northland is a New Zealand depositional example whose possible breaching has been debated locally — a good slot for a dated case study built from class material.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** A list of named landforms is not an explanation of the process that made them.
- **Confusing weathering with erosion**, or **weather with climate**.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit** — discharge in m³/s, rainfall in mm, pressure in millibars.
- **Giving a trend with no data** from the hydrograph, climate graph or synoptic chart in front of you.
- **Labelling instead of annotating.** A diagram earns marks when the labels say *what the process is doing*, with arrows showing direction of movement.
- **Explaining a river landform without saying where in the course it is**, or treating meanders as a feature of the upper course.
- **Claiming a management scheme "works"** without naming the criterion, the place and the date.
