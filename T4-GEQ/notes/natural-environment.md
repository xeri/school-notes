## The geomorphic cycle

The landscape is simultaneously built and destroyed, and the debris is recycled.

| Stage | Processes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Land building (tectonic) | folding, faulting, volcanism | new relief: fold mountains, volcanoes, fault scarps, block mountains |
| Land modifying (denudation) | weathering, then erosion, transport and deposition by rivers, ice, wind and the sea | relief worn down, valleys cut, sediment moved to lowlands and the sea |
| Recycling | sediment buried, compacted and cemented into sedimentary rock; heat and pressure alter rock into metamorphic rock; uplift returns it to the surface | the same material is re-exposed and eroded again |

*Causal chain.* Plate movement folds, faults and erupts rock into high relief → weathering loosens the rock and agents of erosion carry it downhill → rivers and coasts deposit it as sediment in lowlands and on the sea floor → burial compacts and cements it into sedimentary rock, and heat and pressure at depth turn some into metamorphic rock → plate movement uplifts it again. The cycle never finishes: uplift and denudation operate at the same time, and the landscape you see is the current balance between them.

**Land-building processes** are folding, faulting and volcanism. **Land-modifying agents** are fluvial (rivers), glacial (ice), aeolian (wind) and marine (waves). A Term 2 question asked students to pick out the land-modifying agents from a list — *aeolian, fluvial, glacial*, not faulting, folding or volcanism.

## Plate tectonics

| Layer | Composition | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Inner core | solid iron and nickel | extreme pressure keeps it solid despite temperatures of about 5000 °C or more |
| Outer core | liquid iron and nickel | generates the Earth's magnetic field |
| Mantle | dense silicate rock, solid but able to flow slowly; partly molten near the top | convection currents here drive plate movement |
| Crust | oceanic (thin, about 5–10 km, dense, basaltic) and continental (thick, about 25–70 km, less dense, granitic) | oceanic crust is created and destroyed; continental crust is too light to be subducted |

*Causal chain of plate movement.* Radioactive decay heats the mantle → heated rock expands, becomes less dense and rises → near the crust it spreads sideways, cools, becomes denser and sinks → this convection current drags the plate above it, and the sinking edge of a plate pulls the rest after it (slab pull) → plates move a few centimetres a year. *This loses marks:* an answer that never says *why* the material rises and sinks (density) is capped at 2 of 3 marks.

| Boundary | Movement | Processes | Landforms | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constructive (divergent) | plates pull apart | magma rises to fill the gap | mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, shield volcanoes, shallow earthquakes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland |
| Destructive (convergent, ocean–continent or ocean–ocean) | plates collide; the denser oceanic plate subducts | the subducting plate melts at depth; friction | deep-sea trench, fold mountains, composite cones, deep and violent earthquakes | Hikurangi subduction zone under the North Island; the Andes |
| Collision (continent–continent) | plates collide; neither subducts | sediment and crust squeezed, thrust and uplifted | high fold mountains, strong earthquakes, no volcanoes | Himalayas |
| Conservative | plates slide past each other | friction, locking and sudden release | fault line, offset streams and roads, shallow earthquakes, no volcanoes | San Andreas Fault; the Alpine Fault (which also has a compression component) |

**New Zealand sits on the boundary between the Pacific and Australian plates.** East of the North Island the Pacific plate subducts westward under the Australian plate at the Hikurangi subduction zone, which feeds the Taupō Volcanic Zone (Ruapehu, Tongariro, Ngāuruhoe, Whakaari, the Taupō and Okataina calderas). Through the South Island the plates slide past and squeeze together along the Alpine Fault, uplifting the Southern Alps. South-west of Fiordland the direction flips and the Australian plate subducts under the Pacific plate.

**Distribution.** Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in long, narrow belts along plate boundaries, because that is where plates collide, separate or grind past each other. The **Pacific Ring of Fire**, which includes New Zealand, holds most of the world's active volcanoes and strongest earthquakes. Fold mountains line convergent margins — the Andes and Rockies along the Pacific edge of the Americas, and the Alps and Himalayas where continents collide. Plate interiors have few, except where a hot spot (Hawaii) or an intraplate field (Auckland) brings magma up.

**Folding** happens when compression buckles layers of sedimentary rock into upfolds (anticlines) and downfolds (synclines). **Faulting** happens when rock fractures under tension or compression and the blocks move along the fault, producing fault scarps, block mountains and rift valleys.

*Causal chain of fold mountains.* Two plates converge → sediment accumulated on the sea floor between them is scraped up and squeezed → the layers buckle into anticlines and synclines, and are thrust over one another along faults → the crust thickens, and because continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle it is pushed upwards → a range of fold mountains forms, still rising while the plates converge. Examples: Himalayas, Alps, Andes. A fully annotated diagram, with the plates named and their direction of movement shown, can earn the marks on its own.

| Magma type | Silica | Viscosity and gas | Eruption | Landform |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basaltic | low | runny, gas escapes easily | frequent, gentle, lava flows | shield volcano, lava plateau |
| Andesitic | intermediate | sticky, traps gas | explosive, alternating ash and lava | composite cone (stratovolcano) |
| Rhyolitic | high | very sticky, traps much gas | violently explosive, pyroclastic flows, huge ash falls | lava dome, caldera |

Label a volcano with: magma chamber, main vent, side (secondary) vent, crater, layers of ash and lava. Label an earthquake with: **focus** (the point underground where the rock ruptures), **epicentre** (the point on the surface directly above the focus), seismic waves spreading outwards, and **intensity** (the strength of shaking felt at a place, which falls with distance from the epicentre).

**Measuring earthquakes.** Seismologists record ground movement with **seismometers**. **Magnitude** — the energy released — is measured on the **Richter scale** or, today, the **moment magnitude scale**; both are logarithmic, so each whole number is ten times the ground movement and about 32 times the energy. **Intensity** — the observed effects at a place — is measured on the **Modified Mercalli scale** (I–XII).

| Volcanic hazard | What it is |
|---|---|
| Pyroclastic flow | a fast, extremely hot avalanche of gas, ash and rock down the flanks — the main killer |
| Lahar | a mudflow of ash mixed with water from rain, a crater lake or melted snow, following valleys |
| Ash fall (tephra) | collapses roofs, ruins crops, contaminates water, grounds aircraft |
| Volcanic bombs | large lumps of lava thrown from the vent |
| Lava flow | destroys everything in its path but usually moves slowly enough to escape |
| Gases | carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide; acid rain and suffocation in hollows |

| | Effects on people | Effects on the environment |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake | collapsed buildings, deaths, injuries, homelessness, broken water, sewer, power and road links, fire, lost business and jobs | landslides, liquefaction, subsidence or uplift, tsunami, altered drainage |
| Eruption | deaths from pyroclastic flows and gas, burns, ash on crops and roofs, evacuation, lost tourism | vegetation destroyed, lahars, ash fall; longer term, fertile soils and new land |

**Why people live in hazardous areas:** fertile volcanic and alluvial soils, geothermal energy and tourism, minerals, flat coastal land and harbours, jobs and family ties, the cost of moving, and *hazard perception* — many people underestimate a risk they have not personally experienced. Higher-level essay answers discuss this perception explicitly.

| Reducing the impact | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction and monitoring | seismometers, records of past events; no reliable short-term prediction | gas emissions, ground swelling (tiltmeters, GPS), earthquake swarms — far more predictable; New Zealand uses a volcanic alert level system |
| Protection | aseismic design: base isolation, cross-bracing, deep foundations, flexible pipes, retrofitting earthquake-prone buildings | strengthened roofs, lava diversion channels and barriers |
| Preparation | drills such as ShakeOut, emergency kits, trained services, building codes enforced | hazard maps, exclusion zones, evacuation routes and plans |
| Planning | avoid building on soft sediment and active faults; red-zone land that cannot be made safe | restrict settlement on hazard-mapped slopes and in lahar paths |

Prediction is the weak link for earthquakes and the strong one for volcanoes — but Whakaari in 2019 showed that monitoring and a raised alert level do not protect people if access is still allowed. That contrast is the heart of most evaluation answers.

**Named examples.** The **Christchurch earthquake of 22 February 2011** (magnitude 6.2, 185 deaths, widespread liquefaction) and the **Whakaari / White Island eruption of 9 December 2019** (a steam-driven eruption that killed 22 of the 47 people on the island) are both New Zealand events, which satisfies the requirement that one tectonic example comes from New Zealand. **Rangitoto**, visited on the field trip, is Auckland's youngest volcano (about 600 years old): an underwater eruption built a scoria cone and tuff ring, then later eruptions above sea level spread basaltic lava flows into the broad shield shape.

## Weathering

**Weathering** breaks rock down *in place*. **Erosion** removes the loosened material. *This loses marks:* confusing the two in a denudation question.

| Process | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Freeze–thaw | physical | water in a crack freezes and expands by about 9%, prising the rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it — most effective where temperatures swing around 0 °C |
| Exfoliation (thermal expansion and contraction) | physical | daily heating and cooling expand and contract the outer skin faster than the interior, until it peels off in sheets — typical of hot deserts |
| Pressure release | physical | removal of overlying rock lets the rock beneath expand and crack in sheets parallel to the surface |
| Biological | physical and chemical | roots widen cracks, burrowing animals break up rock, lichen and decaying vegetation release acids |
| Carbonation | chemical | rainwater absorbs CO₂ to form weak carbonic acid, which dissolves the calcium carbonate of limestone |
| Hydrolysis | chemical | slightly acidic water reacts with feldspar in granite, rotting it to clay (kaolin) |

Weathering supplies the loose material that the agents of erosion then carry away — without it, rivers, ice and waves would have far less load with which to abrade.

| Landform | Rock | Formed by |
|---|---|---|
| Scree slope | any jointed rock | freeze–thaw fragments falling from a cliff or crag and accumulating at its base |
| Exfoliation dome | massive granite | sheets peeling off a rounded outcrop by exfoliation and pressure release |
| Limestone pavement | limestone | carbonation widens joints into deep grooves (grikes), leaving flat blocks (clints) |
| Sink hole (swallow hole) | limestone | a stream disappears down a joint widened by solution |
| Cave | limestone | water dissolves downwards through joints and then along bedding planes; Waitomo is the New Zealand example |
| Granite tor | granite | hydrolysis rots the rock along closely spaced joints underground; where joints are widely spaced, blocks survive; once the clay is eroded away they stand up as a tor |

## Glaciers

*Causal chain of glacier formation.* Snow accumulates in a hollow where more falls in winter than melts in summer → the weight of later falls compresses the lower snow and expels air → snow becomes granular névé (firn) and then dense blue glacier ice → once thick enough, the ice deforms under its own weight and moves downslope under gravity.

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| Plucking | meltwater at the base freezes onto jointed bedrock, and the moving ice tears blocks away |
| Abrasion | rock fragments frozen into the base scour the bedrock like sandpaper, polishing it and cutting striations |
| Freeze–thaw above the ice | shatters the valley sides and back walls, supplying debris onto the glacier |
| Rotational slip | ice rotating in a hollow deepens it into an armchair shape |
| Transport | debris is carried on, within and beneath the ice |
| Deposition | when the ice melts it drops its load unsorted as moraine (till) |

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Cirque (corrie) | erosional | snow collects in a shaded hollow, freeze–thaw and plucking steepen the back wall, rotational slip and abrasion deepen the floor, leaving a rock lip; after melting it may hold a tarn |
| U-shaped valley (glacial trough) | erosional | ice fills a former V-shaped river valley and erodes the sides as well as the floor, straightening and deepening it and cutting off spurs |
| Hanging valley | erosional | a smaller tributary glacier erodes less deeply, so its valley is left high above the main trough, often with a waterfall |
| Finger lake (ribbon lake) | erosional | a long, deep lake filling an over-deepened section of the trough, e.g. Lake Wakatipu |
| Fiord | erosional | a glacial trough eroded below sea level and drowned as sea level rose, e.g. Milford Sound / Piopiotahi |
| Medial moraine | depositional | two lateral moraines merge where tributary glaciers join, leaving a ridge down the middle of the glacier |
| Terminal moraine | depositional | debris dumped in a ridge across the valley at the furthest advance of the ice |

**Crevasses** are deep cracks in the brittle upper ice, opening where the glacier speeds up, goes over a step in its bed, or bends.

## Wind (not to be assessed in the 2025 specification)

Wind erodes by **abrasion** (sand blasting rock) and **deflation** (lifting loose fine material away, lowering the surface into hollows), and transports sand by saltation close to the ground. **Mushroom rocks** form because the sand is carried in the lowest metre or so of the airstream, so the base of an outcrop is undercut faster than its top. **Dunes** form where an obstacle slows the wind and sand is dropped, building a gentle windward slope and a steep leeward slip face that migrates downwind. The school's topic list also names **mesas** (flat-topped hills capped by resistant rock) and **loess** (deposits of fine wind-blown silt).

## The drainage basin and the hydrologic cycle

The **hydrologic cycle** is the closed circulation of water between the oceans, atmosphere and land: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and the return of water to the sea through runoff and groundwater. A **drainage basin** is an *open* system within it: the area drained by one river and its tributaries, bounded by a **watershed** — the ridge of high ground separating it from the next basin. Other parts: **source**, **tributary**, **confluence** (where two rivers join) and **mouth**.

| Element | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Precipitation | input | all water reaching the ground |
| Interception | store | water held on leaves and stems |
| Infiltration | flow | water soaking into the soil |
| Percolation | flow | water passing from the soil into the rock |
| Soil moisture, groundwater | store | water held in the soil and in permeable rock |
| Throughflow | flow | lateral movement through the soil to the channel |
| Groundwater flow, baseflow | flow | slow seepage through rock, sustaining the river between storms |
| Surface runoff (overland flow) | flow | water flowing over the surface when rain exceeds the infiltration rate or the ground is saturated |
| Channel flow | flow | water in the river itself |
| Evaporation and transpiration | output | water returned to the atmosphere |
| Discharge | output | water leaving the basin through the river mouth |

$$\text{discharge }(Q) = \text{cross-sectional area} \times \text{mean velocity}$$

Measured in cumecs (m³/s). **Worked example.** Width 8 m, mean depth 0.75 m, mean velocity 1.2 m/s: area $= 8\times0.75 = 6$ m²; $Q = 6\times1.2 = 7.2$ m³/s.

## Flood hydrographs

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rising limb | the steep rise in discharge after the storm |
| Peak discharge | the maximum discharge reached |
| Peak rainfall | the time of the maximum rainfall |
| Lag time | the gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge |
| Falling (recession) limb | the return towards normal flow |
| Baseflow | the normal discharge sustained by groundwater, before and after the storm |
| Bankfull discharge | the discharge that just fills the channel; beyond it, flooding |

| Factor | Short lag, high peak ("flashy") | Long lag, low peak |
|---|---|---|
| Rainfall | intense, prolonged, or falling on frozen or saturated ground | light, steady, on dry absorbent ground |
| Relief | steep slopes | gentle slopes |
| Rock | impermeable | permeable |
| Soil | thin, clay, already saturated | deep, sandy |
| Vegetation | bare or ploughed ground | dense forest, high interception |
| Land use | urban: tarmac, roofs, drains | woodland, wetland, pasture |
| Basin shape and drainage density | circular, high density | elongated, low density |

*Causal chain, why urbanisation makes floods worse.* Tarmac and roofs are impermeable → infiltration falls and surface runoff rises → drains and gutters deliver water to the channel in minutes instead of hours → lag time shortens and peak discharge rises → the same rainfall now exceeds bankfull discharge → flooding, and because the floodplain is already built on, the damage is greater.

## River processes and the long profile

| Erosion process | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Hydraulic action | the force of moving water hitting the banks and forcing air into cracks, which expands and breaks the rock |
| Abrasion (corrasion) | the load scraping and grinding against the bed and banks |
| Attrition | load particles colliding with each other, becoming smaller and rounder downstream |
| Corrosion (solution) | chemical dissolving of soluble rock, notably limestone |
| Cavitation | air bubbles collapsing in fast, turbulent water, each collapse a tiny shock wave against the bank |

**Vertical erosion** deepens the channel (dominant in the upper course); **lateral erosion** widens it (dominant in the middle and lower course).

| Transport process | Material |
|---|---|
| Solution | dissolved minerals |
| Suspension | fine clay and silt carried in the flow — it makes the river look brown |
| Saltation | sand and small pebbles bouncing along the bed |
| Traction | boulders rolled and dragged along the bed |

The **Hjulström curve** plots water velocity against particle size and shows three things: larger particles need higher velocities to be picked up (entrained); very fine clay also needs a surprisingly high velocity because its particles are cohesive and lie flat; and once moving, a particle stays in transport at a velocity *lower* than the one needed to lift it. Deposition happens when velocity falls below the settling velocity for that size — so the coarsest load is dropped first and the finest last.

The **Bradshaw model** sets out the downstream changes: discharge, channel width, channel depth, cross-sectional area, mean velocity and load quantity all **increase**, while gradient, bed roughness and load particle size all **decrease**. The **Schumm model** relates channel shape to the type of load: a river carrying mainly fine suspended load has a narrow, deep, sinuous channel; one carrying mainly coarse bedload has a wide, shallow, often braided channel. Velocity rises downstream, even though the gradient falls, because the smoother, deeper channel wastes less energy on friction.

| Course | Valley and channel | Main processes | Landforms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | steep gradient, narrow shallow channel, V-shaped valley | vertical erosion, large angular load | V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, rapids, gorges |
| Middle | gentler gradient, wider channel, broader valley | lateral erosion and deposition | meanders, river cliffs, slip-off slopes, small flood plain |
| Lower | very gentle gradient, wide deep channel, flat valley floor | deposition, fine load | large meanders, ox-bow lakes, flood plain, levées, delta |

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| V-shaped valley | erosional | vertical erosion cuts down; weathering and mass movement wear back the sides into a V |
| Interlocking spurs | erosional | the river winds round projections of harder rock it cannot erode through |
| Waterfall | erosional | hard rock over soft; the soft rock is undercut by hydraulic action and abrasion into a plunge pool, the hard cap overhangs and collapses, and the fall retreats upstream leaving a gorge |
| Rapids | erosional | bands of alternating hard and soft rock make a steep, turbulent, broken stretch |
| Meander | erosional and depositional | fastest flow swings to the outside of the bend and erodes a river cliff; slow flow on the inside deposits a slip-off slope; corkscrew (helicoidal) flow carries sediment from the outer to the inner bank |
| Ox-bow lake | erosional and depositional | erosion narrows the meander neck; a flood cuts straight through; deposition seals the old loop off as a crescent-shaped lake |
| Flood plain | depositional | when the river floods it spreads out, slows and drops fine alluvium across the valley floor; migrating meanders widen it |
| Levée | depositional | in a flood the coarsest sediment is dropped first at the channel edge, building a natural raised bank |
| Delta | depositional | the river meets the sea or a lake, velocity falls sharply, sediment is deposited faster than it can be removed (salt water also makes clay particles clump and sink — flocculation), and the channel splits into distributaries |

## Rivers and people: opportunities, flooding and management

| Opportunity | Hazard |
|---|---|
| fertile alluvial soil for farming | flooding of homes, farmland and roads |
| water for drinking, irrigation and industry | drought and low flow |
| hydroelectric power | bank erosion and loss of land |
| transport and trade | pollution and waterborne disease |
| fishing, recreation and tourism | silt and debris left by floods; channel change destroying infrastructure |

Rivers flood when water enters the channel faster than it can carry it away: prolonged or intense rainfall (often from a tropical cyclone or a stalled front), rapid snowmelt, saturated or frozen ground, deforestation, urbanisation, and channels narrowed or shallowed by sediment or by building on the floodplain.

| Approach | Method | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | stopbanks (artificial levées) | raise the bank so more water stays in the channel | immediate, protects known assets | expensive, pushes water downstream, catastrophic if breached |
| Hard | channel straightening and dredging | water leaves the area faster | quick local relief | moves the problem downstream, damages habitat, needs repeating |
| Hard | dam and reservoir | stores floodwater and releases it slowly | controls flow, adds water supply and HEP | very high cost, displaces people, traps sediment |
| Hard | flood-relief channel, floodgates and pumps | divert or remove excess water | protects a specific town or farmland | costly; overwhelmed by extreme events |
| Soft | floodplain zoning | no new building where floods are likely | cheap, permanent | cannot remove existing development |
| Soft | afforestation and wetland restoration | more interception and storage, longer lag time | improves habitat | slow, needs land, limited in extreme events |
| Soft | flood warnings and preparation | people move themselves and goods | cheap, saves lives | does not prevent property damage |
| Soft | managed retreat and voluntary buy-outs | people leave the highest-risk land | removes the exposure permanently | costly, politically difficult, communities broken up |

**Named examples.** The **Waikato River** — New Zealand's longest at about 425 km, draining Lake Taupō — is the opportunities-and-management example: eight hydroelectric dams, Auckland's water supply, irrigation and farming, and stopbanks, floodgates and pumps on the lower river. **Cyclone Gabrielle** (12–14 February 2023) brought record rainfall to the eastern North Island; in Hawke's Bay the Esk Valley was buried in silt and stopbanks failed, and the response included a national state of emergency and voluntary buy-outs of the highest-risk properties. **Bangladesh**, on the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta, is the contrasting LIC example of a low-lying, densely populated floodplain.

**River fieldwork.** Measure at several sites downstream: width (tape), depth at regular intervals (metre rule), velocity (float and stopwatch over a measured distance, or a flow meter), bedload size and roundness (calipers and a roundness chart), gradient (clinometer). Calculate cross-sectional area and discharge, and test a hypothesis such as *channel width increases downstream* against the Bradshaw model. Safety (wellingtons, never alone, avoid high flow) and access decide site selection as much as the hypothesis does.

## Weather and climate

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time — over hours or days. **Climate** is the average weather of a place measured over a long period, at least 30 years. *Weather is what is happening; climate is what you expect to happen.*

The **elements of weather** are temperature, precipitation, humidity, wind (speed and direction), sunshine, cloud cover and air pressure.

| Element | Unit | Instrument | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | °C | maximum–minimum thermometer | records the current, highest and lowest temperatures since it was reset |
| Humidity | % relative humidity | wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (hygrometer) | the wet bulb reads lower because evaporation cools it; the difference is looked up in a table to give relative humidity |
| Rainfall | mm | rain gauge | sunk into short grass with the rim above ground to stop splash, away from buildings and trees |
| Wind speed | knots, m/s or km/h | anemometer (rotating cups) | mounted high above open ground |
| Wind direction | compass point | wind vane | the arrow points *to where the wind comes from*; a wind from the south is a southerly |
| Sunshine | hours | sunshine recorder | a glass sphere burns a trace on a card while the sun shines |
| Air pressure | millibars (mb), the same as hectopascals (hPa) | barometer | average sea-level pressure is about 1013 mb |

**Stevenson screen** — the rules a mark scheme credits:
- painted **white**, to reflect the Sun's radiation;
- **louvred** sides, so air circulates freely but direct sunlight and rain are kept out;
- a solid **roof**;
- on legs so the box is about **1 m** and the thermometers about **1.2 m** above the ground, clear of heat radiated from the surface;
- standing on **grass**, not concrete or tarmac;
- at least **twice the height of the nearest object** away from it, in an open site;
- the door facing **south in the Southern Hemisphere** (north in the Northern), so the Sun never shines on the thermometers when it is opened.

**Reading a maximum–minimum thermometer.** The present temperature is the top of the mercury in both arms; the maximum is the bottom of the index marker in the maximum arm, the minimum the bottom of the index in the minimum arm (whose scale reads *downwards*). Reset the markers with a magnet each day at the same time.

### Humidity, condensation and cloud

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Absolute humidity | the mass of water vapour in a given volume of air, in g/m³ |
| Saturation | air holding the maximum water vapour it can at that temperature |
| Relative humidity | the water vapour actually present as a percentage of the maximum the air could hold at that temperature; 70% means the air could hold a further 30% before saturating |
| Dew point | the temperature to which air must be cooled to become saturated; any further cooling causes condensation |
| Evaporation | liquid water becoming water vapour; it takes in energy |
| Condensation | water vapour becoming liquid water; it releases that energy (latent heat) |
| Condensation (hygroscopic) nuclei | tiny particles of dust, smoke or salt on which vapour condenses |

Warm air can hold far more water vapour than cold air. So **cooling air raises its relative humidity** and **warming air lowers it**, even though the amount of vapour has not changed.

*Causal chain of cloud and rain.* Air is made to rise → it expands as pressure falls and cools (adiabatic cooling) → its relative humidity rises until it reaches dew point → water vapour condenses onto condensation nuclei, forming cloud droplets or ice crystals → droplets collide and merge until they are too heavy for the rising air to hold up → precipitation.

Air can be made to rise in **four ways**: strong heating of the ground (**convection**), mountains in the way (**orographic** or relief), warm air meeting cold air at a front (**frontal**), and air flowing together from different directions and being forced up (**convergence**, as at the ITCZ). A Term 1 paper asked students to match all four to diagrams.

| Cloud | Height (class booklet) | Appearance | Weather |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cirrus (high) | usually above about 5500 m | thin, white, wispy "curl of hair", ice crystals | fair, but thickening cirrus often signals an approaching warm front |
| Cirrostratus, cirrocumulus (high) | high | thin sheet / small fluffy ripples | — |
| Altostratus, altocumulus (middle, "alto") | middle | grey sheet with the Sun a bright spot / larger rounded masses | rain possible |
| Stratus (low) | below about 2000 m, sometimes reaching the ground | grey layer covering the sky | drizzle, light rain or snow |
| Nimbostratus (low) | low, thick | dark grey layer | the main producer of steady rain ("nimbus" means rainy) |
| Cumulus | base about 600–1200 m | separate heaps like cotton wool | fair, or light showers |
| Cumulonimbus | towering, up to about 10 km tall, anvil top | giant cauliflower | heavy rain, thunder, lightning, hail |

| Precipitation type | Uplift | Steps | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Convectional** (free convection) | the Sun heats the ground strongly, warming the air above | heated air expands and rises in thermals → cools to dew point → towering cumulonimbus → heavy afternoon downpours with thunder and lightning | equatorial regions almost every afternoon; continental interiors in summer |
| **Relief / orographic** (forced convection) | prevailing moist wind forced up a mountain barrier | air rises and cools → condensation on the windward slope → heavy rain or snow → air descends the leeward side, warms and dries → **rain shadow** and a warm, dry **föhn wind** | West Coast of the South Island (wet) and Canterbury (rain shadow) |
| **Frontal** | warm, less dense air forced to rise over denser cold air along a front | gentle ascent at a warm front gives layered cloud and long steady rain; steep ascent at a cold front gives cumulonimbus and short heavy showers | mid-latitudes, including New Zealand in winter |
| **Convergence** | air streams meeting and rising | rising air cools → cloud → rain | the ITCZ near the equator |

*This loses marks:* a convectional-rainfall answer without a diagram is capped at 2 of 4 marks; an annotated diagram can earn all four.

**Forms of precipitation:** rain; drizzle (very fine drops from stratus); **sleet** (rain that freezes, or snow that half melts, as it falls); **hail** (ice pellets built up in layers as strong updrafts in a cumulonimbus carry them up and down through supercooled water); **snow** (ice crystals that clump into flakes, reaching the ground when it is near or below 0 °C).

**Dew, frost and fog.** By day the surface absorbs short-wave radiation from the Sun; day and night it gives off long-wave radiation. Cloud traps and returns long-wave radiation, keeping the surface warm. On a **clear, calm night** the radiation escapes to space, the ground cools rapidly, and the layer of air touching it cools to its dew point → water condenses on the grass as **dew**. If the temperature is below 0 °C the vapour freezes onto surfaces as **frost**. If the cooling extends some distance above the ground, condensation on dust particles in the air forms **fog** — a cloud at ground level.

### Air pressure and wind

**Air pressure** is the weight of the air pressing down on the Earth's surface, measured with a barometer in millibars.

*Causal chain of wind.* The Sun heats the surface unevenly → over warm surfaces air is heated, expands, becomes less dense and rises, leaving **low pressure** at the surface → over cold surfaces air cools, becomes denser and sinks, creating **high pressure** → air moves across the surface from high to low pressure to replace the rising air — this movement is **wind** → the bigger the pressure difference over a distance (the **pressure gradient**), the stronger the wind → rising and sinking air complete a **convection cell**.

| Influence | Effect on the wind |
|---|---|
| Pressure gradient | sets air moving from high to low; closely spaced isobars = strong wind, widely spaced = light wind |
| Coriolis effect | the Earth's rotation deflects moving air to the **left in the Southern Hemisphere** and to the right in the Northern; there is no deflection at the equator and the most at the poles |
| Friction | at the surface it slows the wind, so the wind blows almost parallel to the isobars but slightly across them towards low pressure — about 15–20° over the sea, much more over land |

**Circulation around pressure systems.** In the **Southern Hemisphere**, air spirals **clockwise into a low** (rising air, cloud, rain) and **anticlockwise out of a high** (sinking air, clear skies). The Northern Hemisphere is the reverse. Winds are calm near the centre of a high but can be strong near the centre of a low.

**Buys Ballot's law.** In the Southern Hemisphere, if you *look into the wind*, low pressure is on your **left** — equivalently, with your back to the wind, low pressure is on your right. (In the Northern Hemisphere, back to the wind, low is on the left.)

**Local winds.** A **sea breeze** blows from sea to land by day, because the land heats faster and air rises over it; a **land breeze** blows from land to sea at night, because the land cools faster. Valley breezes blow up-slope by day; cold air drains down valleys at night (katabatic wind). A **monsoon** is a seasonal reversal of wind caused by the same land–sea contrast on a continental scale, bringing a wet season to South and East Asia.

### Global atmospheric circulation: the tri-cellular model

Insolation is greatest at the equator, where the Sun is high in the sky and its rays pass through less atmosphere and are concentrated on a smaller area, and least at the poles. The atmosphere moves surplus heat from the equator towards the poles in three convection cells in each hemisphere.

| Latitude | Cell boundary | Surface pressure | Surface winds | Climate produced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0° | rising limb of the two Hadley cells — the **ITCZ** | equatorial **low** | trade winds converge | hot and wet all year: convectional rain, tropical rainforest |
| about 30° N and S | sinking limbs of the Hadley and Ferrel cells | subtropical **high** | trade winds blow towards the equator; westerlies blow towards the poles | dry, clear skies: most of the world's **hot deserts** |
| about 60° N and S | rising limbs of the Ferrel and Polar cells — the **polar front** | sub-polar **low** | westerlies meet polar easterlies | cool, changeable, **frontal rain**: temperate climates |
| 90° | sinking limb of the Polar cell | polar **high** | polar easterlies blow outwards | very cold and dry |

*Causal chain, the Hadley cell.* Intense insolation heats the equator → air rises, creating the equatorial low → it cools and condenses, giving heavy convectional rain → the now-dry air spreads polewards at high altitude, cools, becomes denser and sinks at about 30° → sinking air warms and its relative humidity falls, so clouds cannot form → subtropical high pressure and deserts → surface air flows back towards the equatorial low as the trade winds.

**Why the trade winds blow from the north-east between the equator and 30° N** (a 4-mark question): insolation is highest at the equator → air rises, creating low pressure → air sinks at about 30°, creating high pressure → surface air moves from high to low pressure → the Coriolis force deflects it to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, so it arrives from the north-east. *This loses marks:* without the Coriolis force the answer is capped at 3, because nothing explains the *direction*. South of the equator the same air is deflected left and arrives from the south-east.

The whole pattern **shifts with the overhead Sun** — north in the Northern Hemisphere summer, south in the Southern Hemisphere summer — which is why many places have a wet and a dry season. **Jet streams** are bands of strong west-to-east wind high in the atmosphere that steer weather systems.

### Why climate varies: climate zones

| Climate type (class) | Where | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical (equatorial) | around the equator | hot and humid all year, very wet; rainforest |
| Arid | about 15–30° N and S, continental interiors, west coasts with cold currents | very dry, hot days and cold nights; desert |
| Mediterranean | west sides of continents about 30–40° | hot dry summers, mild wet winters |
| Temperate | mid-latitudes, especially near the sea (New Zealand, the UK) | mild summers, cool winters, rain all year |
| Continental | interiors of large continents far from the sea | long, cold winters and short, hot summers; a large temperature range |
| Polar | high latitudes | long periods of extreme cold, little precipitation |

The **Köppen classification** groups the same pattern into five letters: A moist tropical, B dry, C humid mid-latitude, D continental, E cold.

| Factor | How it changes climate |
|---|---|
| Latitude | near the equator the Sun is high, its energy concentrated and the days roughly equal length all year, so it is hot; towards the poles the rays are oblique, spread over a larger area and pass through more atmosphere, so it is colder |
| Altitude | air thins with height and holds less heat; temperature falls on average by about 6.5 °C per 1000 m (the class booklet rounds this to about 1 °C per 100 m) |
| Distance from the sea | water heats and cools more slowly than land, so coasts have cool summers, mild winters and a small range (maritime); continental interiors have hot summers, very cold winters and a large range |
| Ocean currents | warm currents raise temperature and supply moisture; cold currents cool the air and make it stable and dry (the Atacama and Namib deserts) |
| Prevailing winds | winds off the sea bring moisture and mild temperatures; winds off a continent are dry and more extreme |
| Relief | mountains force air to rise, wetting the windward side and leaving a rain shadow to leeward |
| Pressure belts | rising air (lows) gives cloud and rain; sinking air (highs) gives clear, dry weather |

### Reading and describing a climate graph

A climate graph shows **average monthly temperature as a line** (left axis, °C) and **average monthly precipitation as bars** (right axis, mm).

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describe the **overall shape**: is the temperature line steep or nearly flat; is rainfall even or seasonal? |
| 2 | Quote the **highest and lowest temperature and rainfall with their months and units** |
| 3 | **Temperature range** = highest − lowest |
| 4 | **Total annual rainfall** = add all twelve bars; **average monthly rainfall** = total ÷ 12 |
| 5 | Name the **wettest and driest season** — summer and winter, not spring and autumn; equatorial places have no seasons |
| 6 | Use the timing to find the **hemisphere**: warmest months June–August means Northern Hemisphere; December–February means Southern Hemisphere |
| 7 | Give the months, but do **not** give a month-by-month account |

**Worked example (equatorial — Manaus, Brazil).** Temperatures 27–28 °C every month, so the annual range is $28-27 = 1$ °C; rainfall in every month, lowest around the middle of the year (about 120 mm in June) and over 2000 mm in total. Described: *hot all year with a very small range; heavy rain throughout, with a slightly drier spell mid-year*. Explained: the Sun is overhead or nearly so all year, so insolation is constantly high; intense heating drives daily convection and cumulonimbus; the ITCZ's low pressure keeps air rising; evapotranspiration from the forest adds moisture.

| Graph shape | Climate | Example |
|---|---|---|
| High flat temperature line, tall bars every month | equatorial / tropical rainforest | Manaus, Borneo |
| Very hot summers, mild winters, almost no rain | hot desert | Sahara, Algeria, Mojave |
| Mild temperatures, small to moderate range, rain all year | temperate maritime | Auckland, the UK |
| Very large range with winter below 0 °C, summer rain peak | continental | Moscow, central Canada |
| Very cold all year, little precipitation | polar / tundra | Arctic stations |

*This loses marks:* describing a climate graph without quoting any figures, giving no units, or reading the temperature off the rainfall axis.

### Synoptic weather charts

A **synoptic chart** shows the weather over a large area at one time. **Isobars** join places of equal pressure, corrected to mean sea level so that altitude does not distort them, and are usually drawn every **4 mb**.

| Feature | Recognise it by | Weather |
|---|---|---|
| **Anticyclone (high, H)** | large area of widely spaced isobars, pressure rising towards the centre (a weak high about 1015 hPa, a strong one above about 1030 hPa) | sinking air, light winds, settled; summer: warm and sunny; winter: cold, clear nights with frost and fog; sometimes low grey cloud ("anticyclonic gloom") |
| **Depression (low, L)** | roughly circular, closely spaced isobars, pressure falling towards the centre (shallow above 1000 hPa, deep below 980 hPa); usually with fronts | rising air, strong winds, cloud and heavy rain |
| **Ridge** | a tongue of high pressure extending from a high | like a high: fine |
| **Trough** | an elongated extension of low pressure | like a low: cloud, showers |
| **Col** | an area of slack pressure between two highs and two lows | light variable winds; fog or thunderstorms |
| **Cold front** | line with **triangles**, blue | colder air replacing warmer air |
| **Warm front** | line with **semicircles**, red | warmer air replacing colder air |
| **Occluded front** | triangles and semicircles on the same side, purple | the cold front has caught up with the warm front |

The symbols point in the direction the front is moving. A deepening (intensifying) low has falling central pressure; a filling low has rising central pressure.

**Passage of a depression** (as it moves from west to east over a place):

| Stage | Sky and weather |
|---|---|
| Ahead of the warm front | high cirrus first, thickening to altostratus; pressure falling |
| Warm front | nimbostratus, long periods of steady rain or drizzle, wind strengthening |
| Warm sector | milder; a lull — clearer, or overcast with drizzle |
| Cold front | cumulonimbus, short heavy downpours, possibly thunder; temperature drops; in New Zealand the wind swings from north-west to south-west |
| Behind the cold front | cumulus and showers, then clearing, cooler and brighter |

**Air masses** are large bodies of air with uniform temperature and humidity, named by source: tropical (warm), polar (cold), maritime (moist), continental (dry). New Zealand mainly receives **maritime polar** and **maritime tropical** air. A **front** is the boundary between two air masses.

**Reading wind from a synoptic chart.** Find the isobars at the place → the wind blows almost along them, slightly towards lower pressure → clockwise round lows and anticlockwise round highs in the Southern Hemisphere → name the wind by the direction it comes *from* → the closer the isobars, the stronger the wind. The strongest winds are where the isobars are tightest, usually near a deep low. A **station model** on a plotted chart shows temperature, pressure, cloud cover (a shaded circle, in eighths or oktas), present weather, and wind direction and speed (a shaft pointing towards where the wind comes from, with feathers for speed).

### New Zealand's climate patterns

New Zealand lies between about **35° and 47° S**, a long, narrow, mountainous country about 1600 km long, surrounded by ocean. Its climate grades from warm subtropical in the far north to cool temperate in the far south, with severe alpine conditions in the mountains.

| Influence | Pattern produced |
|---|---|
| **Seasonal shift of the cells** | in **summer** the pressure belts move south and New Zealand sits under the sinking air where the Hadley and Ferrel cells meet, so it gets many **anticyclones** and settled weather; in **winter** they move north and New Zealand sits nearer the meeting of the Ferrel and Polar cells, so cold southern air collides with warm northern air and **fronts and frontal rain** are frequent |
| **Prevailing westerlies and relief** | most wind arrives from the west and south-west; the mountain chain blocks it, so air is funnelled through gaps such as **Cook Strait** and **Foveaux Strait**, making Wellington very windy |
| **Orographic effect** | air forced over the ranges gives a **wet west** and a **dry east**; the effect is strongest in the South Island because the Southern Alps are higher and more continuous. Milford Sound receives about 6700 mm a year, while Central Otago (Alexandra) gets under 400 mm. The hot, dry **nor'wester** across the Canterbury Plains is a föhn wind; droughts are common in the rain-shadow east |
| **Sunshine** | cloud from forced convection keeps the west dull; the sheltered east and north are the sunniest — Nelson, Marlborough, Hawke's Bay, Gisborne and the Bay of Plenty top the national averages, with over 2400 hours a year at Nelson and Blenheim |
| **Latitude** | the north is warmer than the south; mean annual temperature falls southwards along the 1600 km length |
| **Altitude** | about three-quarters of the country is hill country or mountains, so mean temperature falls sharply inland and upward; the map of mean annual temperature closely follows the relief map |
| **The ocean** | water heats and cools slowly, so the whole country has a small seasonal range; the range increases inland, and Central Otago has the most continental climate, with hot summers and frosty winters |
| **El Niño and La Niña** | during El Niño New Zealand gets more south-westerlies — cooler, with drought in the east; during La Niña more north-easterlies — warmer and wetter in the north and east |

*This loses marks:* "the west is wetter because of the mountains". The marks need the mechanism — prevailing westerly → forced to rise → cools to dew point → condensation → rain on the windward side → descending air warms and dries → rain shadow in the east.

## Ecosystems

An **ecosystem** is a community of living organisms (plants, animals, decomposers) interacting with the non-living parts of their environment — climate, relief, soil, water and rock — as one system. A change in any one component changes the others.

| | Tropical rainforest | Hot desert |
|---|---|---|
| Location | in a belt within about 10° of the equator: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin and West Africa, South-East Asia (Borneo, Indonesia) | about 15–30° north and south, especially on the west sides of continents: Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Namib, Atacama, central Australia, Mojave |
| Cause of location | rising air at the equatorial low and the ITCZ | sinking air of the subtropical high; trade winds that have crossed large land areas; cold offshore currents; rain shadows |
| Temperature | about 26–28 °C every month; annual range of only 1–2 °C | very high by day (often over 40 °C in summer), near freezing on winter nights; large daily range |
| Rainfall | heavy and in every month, usually over 2000 mm a year, convectional, falling in afternoon thunderstorms | under 250 mm a year and very unreliable |
| Humidity and sunshine | humidity very high; daily cloud cuts sunshine hours | humidity very low; clear skies, very high sunshine |
| Vegetation | dense, layered: emergents up to about 50 m, a continuous canopy at about 30 m, under-canopy, shrub layer, dark forest floor; buttress roots, drip-tip leaves, lianas, epiphytes | sparse, drought-adapted: succulents storing water, very long or wide roots, small, waxy or spiny leaves, seeds that lie dormant until rain |
| Soil | latosol: deep, red, leached and nutrient-poor | thin, sandy or stony, little organic matter, salts drawn to the surface |
| Wildlife | the highest biodiversity on land; most species live in the canopy | few species, highly specialised: nocturnal, burrowing, able to go without drinking |

**Why the rainforest has a small annual temperature range:** the Sun is overhead or at a high angle all year, so insolation is constantly high and concentrated; there is no cold season; the daily cloud cover limits extremes. **Why hot deserts have a large daily range:** no cloud, so insolation is intense by day, and with no cloud blanket the heat radiates straight back to space at night. **Why hot deserts are drier than rainforests:** sinking air in the subtropical high warms and cannot form cloud; the trade winds have crossed land and lost their moisture; cold offshore currents cool the air so it holds little moisture; some lie in rain shadows.

*Causal chain, why rainforest soils are poor despite the lush vegetation.* High temperature and constant moisture make decomposition of leaf litter very rapid → nutrients are released and immediately taken up by shallow tree roots → almost all nutrients are stored in the living biomass, not the soil → heavy rain leaches what is left downwards → clear the trees and the nutrient store goes with them, so cleared land loses fertility within a few seasons.

**Interactions in the Amazon** (the named rainforest): constant heat and rain → rapid plant growth and competition for light → tall emergents, a dense canopy, plants adapted to shed water and climb → canopy shade keeps the floor dark and humid → huge variety of animals adapted to particular layers (canopy monkeys, birds and insects; jaguars on the floor) → rapid decomposition by fungi and insects recycles nutrients straight back to the vegetation → transpiration from the trees returns moisture to the air, feeding the next day's rain.

| Deforestation: cause | Deforestation: effect |
|---|---|
| cattle ranching — the biggest single cause in the Amazon | loss of habitat and biodiversity, species extinction, lost medicines |
| commercial farming such as soya | soil erosion and gullying once roots and canopy are gone |
| logging for hardwood | rivers silted, water quality falls, more flooding |
| mining (e.g. iron ore) | less interception and transpiration, so local rainfall falls and runoff rises |
| roads and settlement, such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway | carbon stored in the trees released, adding to global warming; less CO₂ absorbed |
| hydroelectric reservoirs | indigenous peoples lose land, livelihood and culture; conflict and disease |
| subsistence and shifting cultivation under population pressure | (benefits) jobs, export earnings, roads, power and land for farmers |

Evaluate deforestation by weighing short-term national economic gain against long-term local and global losses, and by who gains (ranchers, mining companies, the government) and who loses (indigenous groups, future generations, the global climate).

**Hot desert and drought — the Sahel.** The class case study is the **Sahel**, the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, which is turning to desert through **desertification**: over-cultivation to feed a fast-growing population, cutting trees for fuel and building, overgrazing and poor soil management, and climate change. The effects are food shortages, migration in search of food and water, the loss of traditions (Maasai herders further south in Kenya face the same pressures), and deaths from malnutrition — especially of young children — dehydration and water-borne disease. The **Great Green Wall** project plants a belt of trees across Africa to halt the advance of the desert, because tree roots hold soil and leaf cover reduces evaporation.

## Coasts

| Wave type | Constructive | Destructive |
|---|---|---|
| Height and length | low, long wavelength | high, steep, short wavelength |
| Frequency | few per minute (about 6–8) | many per minute (about 10–14) |
| Action | strong swash, weak backwash — builds the beach | weak swash, strong backwash — removes beach material |

| Coastal erosion process | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Hydraulic action | breaking waves force air and water into cracks; the compressed air expands as the wave retreats, weakening and breaking the rock |
| Abrasion (corrasion) | waves throw sand, pebbles and boulders at the cliff — a sandpaper action |
| Attrition | rocks carried by the waves knock into each other and become smaller and rounder |
| Corrosion (solution) | weak acids in sea water dissolve minerals, especially in limestone and chalk |

**Wave refraction** bends wave crests as they approach an irregular coast: waves slow in the shallow water off a headland, so their energy converges on the headland and diverges in the bay — which is why headlands are eroded and bays accumulate beaches. **Longshore drift** moves sediment along the coast where waves approach at an angle: the swash carries material up the beach at the angle of the prevailing wind, the backwash drags it straight back down under gravity, and the net result is a zigzag movement along the shore. Wind then moves dry sand inland by **saltation**, and vegetation such as marram or pīngao traps it (**sand trapping**).

| Landform | Type | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Headland and bay | erosional | bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast; differential erosion cuts the soft rock back into bays while the resistant rock stands out as headlands |
| Cliff and wave-cut platform | erosional | hydraulic action and abrasion cut a wave-cut notch at high-tide level → the overhang above is undercut and collapses → the cliff retreats inland, leaving a gently sloping rock platform exposed at low tide |
| Cave, arch, stack, stump | erosional | waves exploit a joint or fault in a headland → it widens into a cave → caves on either side meet or one cuts through to form an arch → the arch roof collapses, leaving an isolated stack → the stack is undercut and collapses to a stump |
| Beach | depositional | constructive waves deposit sand and shingle in sheltered bays; the swash pushes material up the beach, shingle collects at the back |
| Spit | depositional | longshore drift carries sediment past a change in the direction of the coast or across a river mouth → it builds out into open water above sea level → a second wave direction curves the end round (a recurved tip) → salt marsh forms in the sheltered water behind |
| Tombolo | depositional | a spit or bar grows until it joins an island to the mainland — Mount Maunganui stands on one |
| Sand dunes | depositional | onshore wind blows dry sand from the beach by saltation → it is trapped by driftwood or plants → the dune grows and is colonised by plants such as marram or pīngao → successive ridges stabilise inland |

**Sea-level change** also shapes coasts: a fall leaves raised beaches above the present shore; a rise drowns river valleys and glacial troughs (fiords). The sediment deposited at the coast and on the sea floor is the *recycling* stage of the geomorphic cycle — it is buried and lithified into new sedimentary rock.

| Coastal opportunity | Coastal hazard |
|---|---|
| ports, harbours, trade and fishing | cliff erosion and loss of land and buildings |
| tourism, recreation, attractive housing | storm surge and coastal flooding |
| flat land for settlement and industry | sea-level rise from global warming |
| sand, ironsand and aggregate extraction | tsunami on tectonic coasts |
| wildlife habitats, estuaries and dunes | landslides on unstable cliffs, especially after heavy rain |

| Management | Type | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sea wall | hard | a concrete wall reflects or absorbs wave energy | strong, long-lasting, doubles as a promenade | very expensive; reflected energy scours the beach in front |
| Revetment | hard | sloping concrete or timber barrier that breaks waves but lets some water through | cheaper than a wall | needs maintenance, ugly, restricts access |
| Rip-rap (rock armour) | hard | large boulders at the cliff foot absorb and dissipate wave energy | cheaper, effective | rock often imported; can be moved by big storms |
| Gabions | hard | wire cages of rocks absorb wave energy | cheap, easy to build | short-lived, ugly |
| Groynes | hard | timber or rock barriers across the beach trap sediment moving by longshore drift | build a wider beach, which absorbs wave energy | starve the coast downdrift of sediment |
| Breakwater | hard | offshore barrier makes waves break before they reach the coast | protects harbours | expensive |
| Beach nourishment | soft | sand added to replace losses | natural look; the beach itself absorbs energy | must be repeated |
| Dune planting and fencing | soft | vegetation traps sand and stabilises dunes | cheap, creates habitat | needs people kept off the dunes |
| Cliff drainage and planting | soft | reduces water in the cliff so it is less likely to slump | cheap | does not stop wave attack at the foot |
| Managed retreat | soft | lets the sea erode or flood low-value land | cheap, creates salt marsh that absorbs energy | land and homes lost; compensation disputes |

*This loses marks:* a management answer that names "sea wall" without saying *how* it reduces erosion. The second mark is for the mechanism.

**Named examples.** **Muriwai**, on Auckland's west coast, shows erosional landforms in a single view — cliffs, a wave-cut platform, a stack (Motutara Island) and caves — and in Cyclone Gabrielle (February 2023) a landslide there killed two volunteer firefighters. **Mangawhai Heads** in Northland is the depositional example: a sand spit sheltering an estuary, home to the critically endangered fairy tern, which was breached by a storm in 1978 and later rebuilt by the community; a future breach would expose the estuary and the township behind it.

**Coastal fieldwork.** Measure the beach profile with a clinometer and ranging poles along transects, sample sediment size and shape at intervals up the beach, time a float to measure longshore drift, compare sediment height on either side of groynes, and use a questionnaire on how people use and value the coast.

## Common exam errors

- **Describing when asked to explain.** A list of named landforms is not an explanation of the process that made them.
- **Confusing weathering with erosion**, **weather with climate**, or **intensity with magnitude**.
- **Quoting a figure without its unit** — discharge in m³/s, rainfall in mm, pressure in millibars, temperature in °C.
- **Giving a trend with no data** from the hydrograph, climate graph or synoptic chart in front of you.
- **Getting the hemisphere wrong** on wind circulation — New Zealand is *clockwise round lows*.
- **Naming a wind by where it is going.** A northerly comes *from* the north.
- **Forgetting the Coriolis effect** when explaining the direction of the trade winds or the westerlies.
- **Labelling instead of annotating.** A diagram earns marks when the labels say *what the process is doing*, with arrows showing direction of movement.
- **Explaining a river landform without saying where in the course it forms**, or treating meanders as a feature of the upper course.
- **Claiming a management scheme "works"** without naming the criterion, the place and the date.
