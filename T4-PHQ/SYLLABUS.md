# Physics — Pre-Q Syllabus

**Final exam (Term 4):** 3 hours, no formula sheet.
Paper 1 — 1 hour, 40 multiple-choice questions, 1 mark each, answer grid. Paper 2 cannot be started until the hour has elapsed.
Paper 2 — 2 hours, 10 short-answer sections of 15 marks. Q1–8 theory, Q9–10 practical skills. Full marks need the equation, the substitution, and a final answer with a unit and appropriate significant figures.

Covers everything studied from the start of Term 1, including practical data analysis, graph plotting and experiment design. Unit 5.4 Digital electronics was removed from the syllabus in 2022. Elastic/inelastic collision labels and beta-plus emission are out of scope.

## 1. Measurement, units and vectors

**1.1 Units and measurement**
- State that every measured quantity needs a unit.
- Identify the 7 SI base quantities: length (m), mass (kg), time (s), current (A), temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), luminous intensity (cd).
- List derived SI units for speed, velocity, acceleration, pressure, energy, power, potential difference, charge and momentum.
- Use standard form in calculations (e.g. $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s) and convert between unit prefixes.
- Measure an angle with a protractor; find the sine of an angle; construct a normal at 90° to a surface.
- Plot a scatter graph, add a best-fit line, interpret its features and calculate its gradient.
- Describe the use of rulers, measuring cylinders, analogue and digital timers.
- Obtain and calculate an average from repeated measurements (useful for small distances, short times, pendulum period).
- Understand the terms accuracy and precision, and how each can be improved when measuring.
- Measure small distances with a vernier calliper (±0.1 mm) and a micrometer screw gauge (±0.01 mm).
- Measure p.d. with a voltmeter in parallel, and current with an ammeter in series.

**1.2 Scalars and vectors**
- State that vectors have magnitude and direction; scalars have magnitude only.
- Distinguish scalars (distance, speed, time) from vectors (displacement, velocity, momentum) with examples.
- Add two vectors with a head-to-tail scale diagram and read off the resultant's magnitude and direction.
- Find the resultant of two or more forces acting along the same line, or at an angle in 2-D.
- Calculate the resultant of two perpendicular vectors with Pythagoras ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$) and basic trigonometry.

## 2. Motion

**2.1 Motion**
- Define speed as rate of change of distance; find it from the gradient of a distance–time graph using a large triangle.
- Use average speed = total distance / total time.
- Plot and interpret distance–time and speed–time graphs.
- Recognise from a speed–time graph: at rest, constant speed, changing speed.
- Find distance travelled from the area under a speed–time graph.
- Find acceleration from the gradient of a speed–time graph; recognise constant and non-constant acceleration, and deceleration.
- Distinguish speed (scalar) from velocity (vector).
- Define and calculate acceleration as change of velocity / time taken.
- State that free-fall acceleration near Earth is constant; use $g = 9.81$ m/s².
- Describe falling bodies with and without air resistance, in words and graphs, including terminal velocity as weight equal in magnitude to drag.

**2.2 SUVAT equations**
- Know the meaning of $s$, $u$, $v$, $a$, $t$.
- Recall and use, for constant or zero acceleration:
  - $v = u + at$
  - $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$
  - $s = \tfrac{1}{2}(u+v)t$
  - $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$
- Use $s = ut$ when acceleration is zero; use $g = 9.81$ m/s² for free fall.
- Recognise $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ as the area under a velocity–time graph, and acceleration as its gradient.

## 3. Forces and momentum

**2.3 Mass and weight**
- Recall mass in kilograms as a measure of a body's resistance to a change in its state of motion (inertia); it is a scalar.
- Recall weight as the gravitational force in newtons on a mass in a gravitational field, acting vertically downwards; it is a vector.
- Recall and use $W = mg$.
- Compare masses using a balance (only valid in a gravitational field).
- State that all objects fall with the same acceleration in a vacuum, and describe an experiment showing it (guinea-and-feather).

**2.5 Forces**
- Recognise that a force can change a body's size, shape, speed, direction or velocity.
- Plot and interpret extension–load graphs and describe the procedure.
- State Hooke's law: extension is proportional to force up to the limit of proportionality; recall and use $F = kx$; recognise the significance of the limit of proportionality.
- Recognise that zero resultant force means at rest or constant velocity in a straight line.
- Recognise friction as the force between surfaces that impedes motion and causes heating; recognise air resistance (drag) as a form of friction.
- Recall and use $F = ma$ including direction.
- Recall Newton's three laws; describe an action–reaction pair as equal in size, opposite in direction, acting on different objects and of the same type.
- Describe circular motion due to a perpendicular force and use $F = mv^2/r$.
- Define the moment of a force as force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action; give everyday examples; know that increasing force or distance increases the moment.
- Calculate clockwise and anticlockwise moments and apply the principle of moments to a beam balanced about a single pivot.
- State that a system is in equilibrium when there is no resultant force and no resultant moment; describe an experiment with vertical forces showing no net moment.
- Describe an experiment to find the centre of mass of a plane lamina with a plumb-line.
- Describe how centre-of-mass position affects stability; use the terms stable, unstable and neutral equilibrium.

**2.6 Momentum**
- Recall and use $p = mv$.
- Recall and use impulse = change in momentum, $Ft = mv - mu$; understand resultant force as rate of change of momentum, $F = \Delta p / t$.
- State that total momentum in an isolated system is constant, and that an isolated system has no external forces acting.
- Apply conservation of momentum to one-dimensional problems.

## 4. Density, energy and pressure

**2.4 Density**
- Recall and use $\rho = m/V$.
- Describe experiments to find the density of a liquid and of a regular solid (cube, cuboid, sphere, triangular prism).
- Determine the density of an irregular solid by displacement, using a measuring cylinder and a eureka can.
- Predict floating from density data, knowing water is 1 g/cm³.
- Convert units between g/cm³ and kg/m³, J and kJ.

**2.7 Energy, work and power**
- Identify changes in kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear and internal energy.
- Recognise energy transfer by forces (mechanical working), electrical currents, heating and waves.
- Apply conservation of energy, including multi-stage examples.
- Draw and read a Sankey diagram with arrow width proportional to energy.
- Recall and use $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h$.
- Explain dissipation: energy spreads out among objects and surroundings as thermal energy.
- Describe how useful energy is obtained from fuels, water (waves, tides, hydroelectric), geothermal, nuclear fission, solar and wind; state advantages and disadvantages in terms of renewability, cost, reliability, scale and environmental impact.
- State that the Sun is the source of all energy resources except geothermal, nuclear and tidal, and that it releases energy by nuclear fusion.
- Recall and use efficiency = (useful output / total input) × 100%, for both energy and power.
- State that work done on a system = energy transferred; recall and use $W = Fd = \Delta E$; know that a perpendicular force changes direction but not speed.
- Relate power to work done and time; recall and use $P = \Delta E / t$.

**2.8 Pressure**
- Recall and use $P = F/A$ in N/m² (Pa), for a normal force on an area.
- Describe a simple mercury barometer and its use for atmospheric pressure.
- Use a manometer and express pressure as a height of fluid.
- Recall and use $P = \rho g h$ for pressure beneath a liquid surface.

## 5. Thermal physics

**3.1 Kinetic molecular model of matter**
- Distinguish solids, liquids and gases by shape, volume and compressibility; draw particle arrangements; describe arrangement, separation and motion of molecules.
- Interpret gas temperature in terms of molecular motion; recognise the Kelvin scale as related to molecular kinetic energy; describe 0 K as the temperature at which molecular motion stops.
- Convert Celsius to Kelvin with $T/\mathrm{K} = T/^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273$.
- Explain gas pressure as the change of momentum of particles striking the walls creating a force over an area.
- Describe Brownian motion in a suspension as evidence for the kinetic model, caused by random molecular bombardment; recall that massive particles can be moved by light, fast molecules.
- Recognise that molecular collisions lose no kinetic energy.
- Describe evaporation as escape of faster-moving molecules from a liquid surface, relate it to cooling, and recall the effect of temperature, surface area and draught.
- Describe qualitatively the pressure effect of changing temperature at constant volume (Gay-Lussac) and volume at constant temperature (Boyle).
- Recall and use $pV = \text{constant}$, $P/T = \text{constant}$, $V/T = \text{constant}$ and $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$ with Kelvin temperatures; relate the laws to their graphs; recognise when the combined equation is unreliable (a real gas near liquefying).

**3.2 Thermal properties and temperature**
- Describe thermal expansion of solids, liquids and gases at constant pressure; explain the relative magnitudes in terms of molecular motion and arrangement; give applications and consequences.
- Explain how any temperature-dependent physical property can measure temperature; recognise the need for fixed points.
- Describe the structure and action of a liquid-in-glass thermometer, and how its structure changes sensitivity, range and linearity.
- Describe a thermocouple and its use for high and rapidly varying temperatures.
- Relate a temperature rise to increased internal energy; give a molecular account.
- Identify heat as energy transferred from a hotter to a colder body.
- State thermal capacity $C = mc$; define specific heat capacity as the energy to raise unit mass by unit temperature; describe an experiment to measure it; recall and use $Q = mc\Delta T$, and know it does not apply during a change of state.
- Apply conservation of energy to heat transfer: energy given out by the hot body = energy taken in by the cold body.
- Describe melting and boiling as energy input without temperature change; state the meaning of melting and boiling point; describe condensation and solidification molecularly; distinguish boiling from evaporation.
- Use latent heat of fusion and of vaporisation with a molecular interpretation; define specific latent heat; describe experiments for ice and steam; recall and use $Q = ml$.

**3.3 Thermal processes**
- Describe experiments demonstrating good and bad thermal conductors; explain conduction by lattice vibration and free-electron transfer.
- Recognise convection as thermal transfer in fluids; describe experiments; explain convection currents via reduced density of heated fluid.
- Identify infrared as part of the electromagnetic spectrum; recognise that radiation needs no medium.
- Describe the effect of surface colour and texture on emission, absorption and reflection; describe Leslie's cube; know that emission also depends on surface temperature and area.
- Identify everyday applications and consequences of conduction, convection and radiation.

## 6. Waves, light and sound

**4.1 General wave properties**
- Demonstrate that waves transfer energy without transferring matter.
- Describe wave motion using ropes, springs and water waves; use the term wavefront.
- Define wave speed, frequency, period, wavelength and amplitude; recall and use $v = f\lambda$ and $f = 1/T$.
- Distinguish transverse from longitudinal waves with examples.
- Describe reflection at a plane surface, refraction due to a change of speed, and diffraction through a narrow gap; describe using water waves; recognise interference (superposition) of waves.
- Describe how wavelength and gap size affect diffraction through a gap, and how wavelength affects diffraction at an edge.

**4.2 Light**
- Describe image formation by a plane mirror and its characteristics; recall that the image is virtual; recall and use angle of incidence = angle of reflection; perform constructions and calculations.
- Describe refraction through a glass block; use angle of incidence $i$ and refraction $r$; describe passage through parallel-sided material.
- Give the meaning of critical angle; describe internal and total internal reflection with a semicircular or right-angled block.
- Recall and use $n = \sin i / \sin r$ and $n = 1/\sin c$; define refractive index via wave speeds.
- Describe the action of optical fibres in medicine and communications.
- Describe the action of a thin converging lens; use principal focus and focal length; draw ray diagrams for real and virtual images; describe images as enlarged / same size / diminished and upright / inverted / real / virtual; use a lens as a magnifying glass.
- Describe dispersion by a prism; state the seven spectral colours in order; recall that single-frequency light is monochromatic.
- Recall the primary colours red, green, blue and the secondary colours cyan, yellow, magenta; explain filters, coloured objects, and how screens mix primaries; describe the apparent colour of objects under coloured light.

**4.3 Electromagnetic spectrum**
- Describe the main features of the spectrum in order of wavelength.
- State that all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, $3.0\times10^8$ m/s, approximately the same in air.
- Describe typical properties and uses: radio waves (radio and TV), microwaves (satellite TV, phones), infrared (appliances, remote controls, intruder alarms), X-rays (medicine, security).
- Demonstrate awareness of safety: microwaves heating body cells, X-rays causing ionisation.

**4.4 Sound**
- Describe sound production by vibrating sources, and its longitudinal nature; describe compression and rarefaction.
- State the audible range as roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz; understand ultrasound as above that range and identify real-life uses of it.
- Show that sound needs a medium to travel through; state typical speeds in gases, liquids and solids (about 330–340 m/s in air).
- Describe an experiment to determine the speed of sound in air (echo method).
- Relate loudness to amplitude and pitch to frequency; describe echoes as reflected sound.
- Describe the basic structure of the ear: eardrum and bones detect pressure variations, the cochlea converts vibrations to impulses carried by the auditory nerve.

## 7. Electricity and magnetism

**5.1 Magnetism**
- Describe forces between magnets and between magnets and magnetic materials; recall that magnetic materials contain magnetic domains.
- Draw field-line patterns around a bar magnet and around two attracting or repelling magnets; explain that field lines cannot cross.
- Describe an experiment using iron filings and a plotting compass to show the pattern and direction.
- Explain magnetisation by stroking, by d.c. in a coil, and by hammering in a field; describe demagnetisation by hammering, heating and a.c. in a coil.
- Contrast soft iron with harder materials such as steel; distinguish permanent magnets from electromagnets.

**5.2 Electrical quantities**
- State that atoms contain positive and negative charges; that unlike charges attract and like charges repel; that charge is measured in coulombs.
- Describe experiments producing charge by friction and detecting it with a gold-leaf electroscope; explain charging by friction as transfer of electrons; give an account of charging by induction.
- State that an electron is negative and a proton positive, with equal magnitude of charge.
- Distinguish conductors (metals with free electrons) from insulators using a simple electron model.
- Describe an electric field as a region where a charge experiences a force; state that field direction is the force on a positive charge; draw patterns for a point charge, a charged sphere, and between parallel plates.
- State that current is the rate of flow of charge; recall and use $I = Q/t$; state that current in metals is a flow of electrons, opposite to conventional current; use analogue and digital ammeters.
- State that e.m.f. is measured in volts and is defined by the energy change inside the supply that drives charge round a complete circuit.
- State that p.d. across a component is measured in volts; recall that 1 V = 1 J/C; use a voltmeter in parallel.
- State that resistance = p.d. / current; recall and use $R = V/I$; describe an experiment to determine resistance; state Ohm's law.
- Relate resistance to wire length and cross-sectional area, and recall and use $R = \rho L/A$.
- Sketch and explain the I–V characteristics of an ohmic resistor and a filament lamp.
- Recall and use $R_T = R_1 + R_2$ in series and $R_T = \dfrac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ in parallel.
- Understand that circuits transfer energy from the supply to components and then to the surroundings; recall and use $P = IV$ and $E = IVt$.

**5.3 Electric circuits**
- Draw and interpret circuit diagrams with sources, switches, fixed and variable resistors, heaters, thermistors, LDRs, lamps, ammeters, voltmeters, galvanometers, magnetising coils, transformers, bells, fuses, relays and diodes.
- Understand that current is the same at every point in a series circuit, and that series resistances add.
- State that in a parallel circuit the source current is the sum of the branch currents and is larger than any branch current; that combined parallel resistance is less than either resistor; state the advantages of lamps in parallel.
- Calculate combined e.m.f. of sources in series; use the fact that series p.d.s sum to the supply p.d.
- Describe the action of a variable potential divider (potentiometer).
- Describe the action of thermistors and LDRs as input transducers, a relay in switching circuits, and a diode as a rectifier.
- Recognise circuits operating as light-sensitive switches and temperature-operated alarms using a relay.

**5.5 Dangers of electricity**
- State the hazards of damaged insulation, overheating cables and damp conditions.
- State the 3-pin plug wire colours: live brown, neutral blue, earth green/yellow.
- State that a fuse protects a circuit, not a person; explain the use of fuses and circuit breakers.
- Use appliance power rating to select a fuse rating or breaker setting.
- Explain the safety benefit of earthing a metal case.

**5.6 Electromagnetic effects**
- Show that a conductor moving across a field, or a changing field linking a conductor, induces an e.m.f.; describe an experiment demonstrating it.
- State the factors affecting induced e.m.f. (rate of change of field, number of turns); know that induced e.m.f. opposes the change causing it (Lenz's law); use Fleming's right-hand rule.
- Distinguish d.c. from a.c.; describe a rotating-coil generator with slip rings; sketch its voltage–time output and relate coil position to peaks and zeros.
- Describe the construction and operation of a transformer with a soft-iron core; use the terms step-up and step-down; recall and use $V_p/V_s = N_p/N_s$ and, for 100% efficiency, $I_pV_p = I_sV_s$.
- Describe high-voltage transmission and its advantages; explain lower cable losses at high voltage via $P = I^2R$.
- Describe the field pattern and direction due to current in a straight wire and in a solenoid; state that a field line's direction is the direction of the force on a N pole; state how field strength varies over the pattern; describe the effect of changing current magnitude and direction; describe applications including the relay.
- Describe an experiment showing a force on a current-carrying conductor in a field, including reversing the current and the field; state the relative directions of force, field and current; describe the corresponding force on a beam of charged particles.
- State that a current-carrying coil in a field experiences a turning effect, increased by more turns, more current or a stronger field; relate this to the d.c. motor and the split-ring commutator.

## 8. Atomic physics and space

**6.1 The nuclear atom**
- Describe models of the atom from hard sphere, through plum pudding, to the nuclear atom (Rutherford) and the Bohr shell refinement.
- Describe how α-particle scattering by thin gold foil gave evidence for replacing the plum-pudding model.
- State that most of the atom is empty space, the nucleus being about $10^5$ times smaller than the inner electron orbits.
- Describe the nucleus as protons and neutrons; state their charges.
- Use proton number $Z$, nucleon number $A$, the term nuclide and the notation $^{A}_{Z}X$.
- Explain isotopes as nuclides with the same $Z$ but different $A$.
- State the meaning of nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
- Balance nuclear equations conserving $A$ and $Z$.

**6.2 Natural radioactivity**
- Demonstrate understanding of background radiation; describe the detection of α, β⁻ and γ emissions.
- Discuss the random and spontaneous nature of radioactive emission.
- Identify α, β⁻ and γ by their nature, relative ionising effect (α most, γ least) and penetrating power (paper stops α, thin aluminium stops β, thick lead or rock stops γ); interpret ionising effects in terms of charge and mass.
- Describe deflection of radioactive particles in electric fields and in magnetic fields (Fleming's left-hand rule).
- Give and explain practical and industrial applications of α, β⁻ and γ.
- State the meaning of radioactive decay; state that α or β decay changes the element; balance decay equations in nuclide notation.
- Know that a Geiger–Müller tube with a counter detects emissions.
- State half-life as the time for the count rate or number of radioactive nuclei to halve; carry out half-life calculations from tables and decay curves, with or without background subtraction.
- Recall the effects of ionising radiation on living cells (death or mutation); describe safe handling, use and storage.
- Know that nuclear power stations rely on fission of a large nucleus such as uranium, and that the Sun releases energy by fusion of small nuclei such as hydrogen.

**7 Space**
- Define an orbit and describe an elliptical orbit; calculate average orbital speed from circumference and period.
- Recall that gravity causes orbital motion; explain that gravitational field strength at a planet's surface depends on its mass, and decreases with distance from the planet.
- State that a light year is a distance, about $9.5\times10^{15}$ m; recall that the Milky Way is one of billions of galaxies and is about 100 000 light-years across.
- Recall that the distance to a far galaxy can be found from the brightness of a supernova in it.
- Describe redshift as an increase in observed wavelength from receding stars and galaxies; explain it as evidence that the Universe is expanding, supporting the Big Bang theory; recall that recession speed follows from the wavelength change.
- Define the Hubble constant as recession speed divided by distance; recall and use $H_0 = v/d$; recall the current estimate $H_0 \approx 2.2\times10^{-18}$ s⁻¹.
- Know that $d/v = 1/H_0$ estimates the age of the Universe, evidence that all matter was once at a single point.

## 9. Practical skills

In the Term 4 final, Paper 2 Q9–10 test practical skills, including practical data analysis, graph plotting and experiment design. (The Term 1 and Term 2 exams also had an in-class alternative-to-practical assessment worth 10%; no weighting is stated for Term 4.)

- Plan an experiment: state the variable to change, the variable to measure, and the variables to keep constant.
- Choose suitable instruments and ranges, and justify the choice by the precision needed.
- Record readings in a table with a quantity, a unit and a consistent number of decimal places.
- Repeat readings and average them; identify and discard anomalous results with a reason.
- Plot a graph with labelled axes, units, a sensible scale and a best-fit line; calculate a gradient with a large triangle; interpret an intercept.
- Describe sources of error, distinguish random from systematic error, and suggest specific improvements.
- Quote a final answer to an appropriate number of significant figures with a unit.
