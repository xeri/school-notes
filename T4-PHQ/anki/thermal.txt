#deck:Physics::5 Thermal physics
Particle arrangement in a solid	Regular lattice, touching, vibrating about fixed positions
Particle arrangement in a liquid	Close together but disordered, sliding past each other
Particle arrangement in a gas	Far apart (~10 diameters), random fast motion, straight lines between collisions
Which state is easily compressible and why?	Gas — the particles are far apart with large gaps
What does temperature measure?	The average kinetic energy of the molecules
What is internal energy?	The total of molecular kinetic AND potential energy
Why is Kelvin the scientific temperature scale?	It is proportional to molecular kinetic energy
What happens at 0 K?	Molecular motion stops — absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature
Celsius to Kelvin conversion	\(T/\mathrm{K} = T/^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273\)
Convert 27 °C to kelvin	300 K
Is a change of 20 °C the same as a change of 20 K?	Yes — the interval is identical, only the zero differs
Explain gas pressure in terms of molecules	Molecules collide with the walls; each collision reverses their momentum, exerting a force spread over the wall area
What is Brownian motion?	The random jittering of smoke particles seen under a microscope
What does Brownian motion show?	That invisible, fast-moving air molecules are bombarding the particle unevenly — evidence for the kinetic model
Do molecular collisions lose kinetic energy?	No — molecules never slow down and stop, even after millions of collisions
Boyle's law	\(pV = \text{constant}\) at constant temperature
Gay-Lussac's law	\(P/T = \text{constant}\) at constant volume
Charles' law	\(V/T = \text{constant}\) at constant pressure
Combined gas law	\(\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}\)
What units must temperature be in for the gas laws?	Kelvin, always
Why does reducing volume raise pressure at constant temperature?	The same molecules hit the walls more often per second in a smaller space
Why does raising temperature raise pressure at constant volume?	Molecules move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often
Explain evaporation molecularly	The fastest molecules at the surface escape
Why does evaporation cool a liquid?	Losing the fastest molecules lowers the average kinetic energy of those left
Three factors that speed up evaporation	Higher temperature, larger surface area, a draught over the surface
Difference between evaporation and boiling	Evaporation is at the surface only at any temperature; boiling is throughout the liquid at a fixed point
Order of thermal expansion magnitude	gases ≫ liquids > solids
Why do gases expand most?	The forces between their molecules are weakest, so separation grows most freely
Three applications of thermal expansion	Bridge expansion gaps, bimetallic strip thermostats, liquid-in-glass thermometers
What are fixed points?	Reproducible reference temperatures used to define a scale — pure melting ice (0 °C) and boiling water (100 °C)
How does a liquid-in-glass thermometer work?	The liquid in the bulb expands much more than the glass, so it rises up the narrow capillary
How do you make a liquid-in-glass thermometer more sensitive?	A larger bulb or a narrower capillary
What does a narrower capillary cost you?	A smaller range
When is a thermocouple used instead?	For high temperatures and rapidly varying temperatures — it has a tiny junction and no liquid to boil
Definition of specific heat capacity	The energy needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 K
Specific heat capacity formula	\(Q = mc\Delta T\)
Specific heat capacity of water	4200 J/kg·K
Thermal capacity of a whole object	\(Q = mc\) per kelvin, in J/K
Main error in a specific heat capacity experiment	Heat loss to the surroundings, which makes the measured value too high — reduce it by lagging
Why does temperature stay constant during melting or boiling?	The energy input changes the molecular arrangement (breaks bonds) rather than increasing molecular speed
Specific latent heat formula	\(Q = ml\)
Definition of specific latent heat	The energy per kilogram needed to change state at constant temperature
Latent heat of fusion refers to which change?	Solid ↔ liquid
Latent heat of vaporisation refers to which change?	Liquid ↔ gas
Explain conduction in a non-metal	Hot molecules vibrate more and jostle their neighbours, passing energy along
Why do metals conduct much better?	They also have free (delocalised) electrons that travel through the lattice carrying kinetic energy
Which state is the worst conductor?	Gas — the molecules are far apart
Describe an experiment comparing conductors	Identical rods of copper, aluminium, glass and wood with pins waxed to the far ends, heated at one end; the pins fall in order of conductivity
Explain a convection current	Heating a fluid lowers its density, so it rises; denser cold fluid sinks to replace it, circulating
Does radiation need a medium?	No — it crosses a vacuum, which is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth
Which surface is the best emitter and absorber of radiation?	Dull black
Which surface is the best reflector of radiation?	Shiny silver
Two other factors affecting radiation emitted	Surface temperature and surface area
What does Leslie's cube demonstrate?	That dull black faces emit more infrared radiation than shiny ones, at the same temperature
How does a vacuum flask block all three transfers?	Vacuum stops conduction and convection, silvered walls reduce radiation, a stopper stops evaporation
