# Practical skills

Assessed as Paper 2 Q9–10 (15 marks each) and as the in-class alternative-to-practical assessment, which includes graph plotting.

## Planning an investigation

State the relationship being investigated, then name three things explicitly:

| | Example: how does pendulum length affect period? |
|---|---|
| **Independent variable** — what you change | length $l$, five values from 0.20 m to 1.00 m |
| **Dependent variable** — what you measure | period $T$ |
| **Control variables** — what you keep the same | mass of bob, amplitude (small), same pivot, no push on release |

Then say **how**, with apparatus and units:

> Measure the length from the pivot to the **centre of the bob** with a metre rule (±1 mm). Displace the bob by a small angle and release it without pushing. Time **20 complete oscillations** with a stopwatch and divide by 20 to get the period. Repeat each length three times and average.

Why 20 oscillations: a ±0.2 s reaction error is 1% of a 20 s total but 10% of a single 2 s swing. Repeats have a purpose — estimating variation and reducing random timing error. Say what the purpose is.

## Choosing apparatus

Match the instrument to the size of the quantity and the precision needed.

| Measuring | Use | Not |
|---|---|---|
| Wire diameter | micrometer (±0.01 mm) | metre rule |
| 30 cm length | metre rule (±1 mm) | micrometer |
| Volume of a pebble | displacement in a measuring cylinder | calliper |
| A 2 s interval | time 20 oscillations | time one |
| Current through a lamp | ammeter in series | voltmeter |

Justify the choice by the precision: "a micrometer, because the wire is about 0.3 mm across and a rule could not resolve that."

## Tables

- Put **quantity and unit** in each column heading: `Length / m`, `Time for 20 swings / s`, `Period / s`.
- Independent variable in the first column, in order.
- All values in a column to the **same number of decimal places**, set by the instrument's resolution.
- Do not invent precision the instrument cannot deliver — a metre rule gives 0.452 m, not 0.4523 m.
- Include a column for the mean of repeats, and for any processed quantity (e.g. $T^2$).

## Graphs

- Independent variable on the **horizontal** axis, unless the analysis needs otherwise (e.g. plotting $T^2$ against $l$ to get a straight line).
- Label both axes with quantity **and** unit.
- Choose a scale in easy steps (1, 2, 5, 10) that uses **more than half** the grid in both directions. Awkward scales like 3 per square lose marks and cause plotting errors.
- Plot points as small crosses or encircled dots.
- Draw a single best-fit straight line or smooth curve — **never dot-to-dot**, and never force the line through the origin unless the physics requires it.

**Gradient.** Pick two widely separated points **on the line** (not data points), draw a large triangle, and calculate

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

with units. State the units — a $T^2$-against-$l$ gradient is in s²/m.

**Intercept.** Read where the best-fit line crosses the axis. A non-zero intercept where the physics predicts zero usually indicates a **systematic** error.

## Anomalous results

An anomaly is a point clearly off the trend. The correct response is to **check and repeat that measurement**, and only then to exclude it, stating a reason — a misread scale, a mistimed start. Do not delete a point simply because it spoils the line, and do not quietly drop it: say in the answer that it was identified, repeated and excluded, and ignore it when drawing the best-fit line.

## Errors

| Type | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Random** | scatter either side of the line; repeats differ | repeat and average; measure larger quantities |
| **Systematic** | all readings shifted the same way; non-zero intercept | check and correct zero error; calibrate the instrument |
| **Zero error** | instrument reads non-zero with nothing loaded | note it and subtract it |
| **Parallax** | reading varies with eye position | view perpendicular to the scale |

Repeats reduce the effect of random error. They do **nothing** for a systematic error.

## Suggesting improvements

Vague suggestions score nothing. Be specific and say what it fixes:

- ✗ "be more accurate"
- ✓ "lag the calorimeter with felt to reduce heat loss to the surroundings, which made the measured specific heat capacity too high"
- ✗ "use better equipment"
- ✓ "use a micrometer rather than a rule for the wire diameter, since the rule can only resolve 1 mm on a 0.3 mm wire"
- ✓ "use light gates rather than a stopwatch to remove reaction-time error from the timing"

## Writing the answer

Full marks for a calculation need all four of:

1. the **equation** stated
2. the **substitution** shown with values
3. a **final answer** to an appropriate number of significant figures
4. the **unit**

Match the significant figures to the data: if the measurements have 3 s.f., quote the answer to 3 s.f., not to the eight digits on the calculator.

Conclusions must be supported by the data — quote the gradient or specific values — and must state the limits: the range tested, and the conditions held constant.
