# Atomic physics and space

## Models of the atom

| Model | Picture | Why it was replaced |
|---|---|---|
| Hard sphere (Dalton) | solid indivisible ball | discovery of the electron |
| Plum pudding (Thomson) | electrons embedded in a positive dough | α-scattering results |
| Nuclear (Rutherford) | tiny positive nucleus, electrons orbiting | needed to explain fixed spectra |
| Bohr refinement | electrons in specific shells | — |

**Rutherford's α-scattering experiment.** A thin gold foil was bombarded with α-particles and the scattering was recorded at many angles. Three results and what each shows:

1. Most α-particles went **straight through** — the atom is mostly empty space.
2. Some were **deflected** through small angles — there is a concentrated **positive** charge repelling them.
3. A very few **bounced back** through more than 90° — that positive charge is in a very small, very **massive** nucleus.

The plum-pudding model predicts only tiny deflections, so it had to go. The nucleus is roughly $10^5$ times smaller than the diameter of the inner electron orbits.

## Nucleus and nuclide notation

The nucleus contains **protons** (charge +1) and **neutrons** (charge 0). Electrons (charge −1) orbit outside.

$$^{A}_{Z}X$$

- $Z$ = **proton number** (atomic number) — identifies the element
- $A$ = **nucleon number** (atomic mass) = protons + neutrons
- neutrons = $A - Z$

A **nuclide** is a specific nucleus. **Isotopes** are nuclides of the same element — same $Z$, different $A$, so different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes: both $Z = 6$, with 6 and 8 neutrons.

**Nuclear fission** splits a large nucleus into smaller pieces. **Nuclear fusion** joins two small nuclei into a larger one.

**Balancing nuclear equations.** Total $A$ and total $Z$ must be the same on both sides.

$$^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} + ^{4}_{2}\alpha$$

Check: $238 = 234 + 4$ ✓ and $92 = 90 + 2$ ✓

## The three emissions

| | α (alpha) | β⁻ (beta) | γ (gamma) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | helium nucleus, 2p + 2n | fast electron | high-frequency EM wave |
| Notation | $^4_2\mathrm{He}$ | $^{\ \ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$ | $^0_0\gamma$ |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Mass | large | tiny | none |
| Ionising effect | **most** | medium | **least** |
| Penetration | stopped by paper / a few cm of air | stopped by a few mm of aluminium | needs thick lead or very thick rock |
| Deflected by fields? | yes, slightly (heavy) | yes, strongly and oppositely | no (uncharged) |

Ionising power tracks charge and mass: a big, double-charged, slow α-particle interacts strongly with every atom it passes, so it loses its energy quickly over a short range. A γ-ray has no charge, interacts rarely, and travels far.

In an **electric field** α and β deflect in opposite directions by electrostatic attraction to the oppositely charged plate. In a **magnetic field** they deflect according to Fleming's left-hand rule, remembering that β⁻ travelling one way is a conventional current the other way. γ is undeflected in both.

β⁺ (positron) emission is not in scope — all β here means β⁻.

## Radioactive decay

Decay is **random** and **spontaneous**: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or make one decay sooner. It is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemistry. But a large sample follows a precise statistical pattern.

In α or β decay the nucleus becomes that of a **different element**, because $Z$ changes.

$$^{14}_{\ 6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow\ ^{14}_{\ 7}\mathrm{N} + ^{\ \ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$$

A neutron has become a proton, emitting an electron: $A$ unchanged, $Z$ up by one.

**Background radiation** is the low level of radiation always present — from rocks (especially radon gas), cosmic rays, food, and medical and industrial sources. It must be **subtracted** from any measured count rate before analysing a sample.

**Detection.** A **Geiger–Müller tube** connected to a counter or scaler clicks once per ionising particle entering it. Photographic film, a cloud chamber and a spark counter also detect emissions.

### Applications

| Emission | Application | Why that one |
|---|---|---|
| α | smoke detector | ionises air in a short gap; smoke interrupts the current |
| β | thickness monitoring of paper or foil | passes through thin sheets, absorption varies with thickness |
| γ | sterilising medical equipment, cancer treatment, tracing pipe leaks | penetrates packaging and tissue |

## Half-life

**Half-life** is the time for the number of undecayed nuclei — or the count rate — to fall to **half** its value.

After 1 half-life, ½ remains; after 2, ¼; after 3, ⅛; after $n$, $(\tfrac12)^n$.

**Worked example.** A source has a corrected count rate of 800 counts/min. Four hours later it reads 50 counts/min. Find the half-life.

$800 \to 400 \to 200 \to 100 \to 50$ is **4 half-lives** in 4 hours, so the half-life is **1 hour**.

**Worked example with background.** The background is 20 counts/min. A sample reads 340 counts/min initially and 100 counts/min after 90 minutes.

Corrected: $340 - 20 = 320$ and $100 - 20 = 80$. That is a fall by a factor of 4, so 2 half-lives in 90 min — a half-life of **45 minutes**.

Forgetting to subtract the background is the single most common error in these questions.

From a decay curve, read the initial count rate, halve it, and read off the time at which the curve reaches that value.

## Safety

Ionising radiation can cause cell **death** (high dose) or cell **mutation** leading to cancer (lower dose). Safe practice:

- store sources in lead-lined containers in a locked cupboard
- handle with long tongs, never with bare hands
- point the source away from people
- keep exposure time short and distance large
- wear a film badge to monitor accumulated dose
- shield with lead or concrete for γ

## Nuclear power

Nuclear power stations rely on **fission**: a large nucleus such as uranium absorbs a neutron and splits into two roughly equal smaller nuclei, releasing energy and more neutrons, which can sustain a chain reaction.

The **Sun** releases energy by **fusion**: small nuclei such as hydrogen join to make a larger nucleus (helium), releasing energy. Fusion needs enormous temperature and pressure to overcome the repulsion between positive nuclei.

## Orbits and gravity

An **orbit** is the curved path of one body around another under gravity. Planetary orbits are **elliptical** — an oval with the Sun at one focus, so the orbital distance and speed vary slightly through the year. Many are nearly circular.

For a near-circular orbit, the average orbital speed is the circumference divided by the period:

$$v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

**Worked example.** A satellite orbits at $r = 7.0\times10^6$ m with a period of 100 minutes (6000 s):

$$v = \frac{2\pi(7.0\times10^6)}{6000} = 7.3\times10^3\ \text{m/s}$$

**Gravity** is the force responsible for orbital motion — it provides the centripetal force that keeps changing the body's direction.

Gravitational field strength:
- at a planet's **surface**, it depends on the planet's **mass** (and radius) — Jupiter's surface field is far stronger than the Moon's
- **around** a planet, it **decreases** as the distance from the planet increases

## Astronomical distances

A **light year** is a **distance** — not a time — equal to the distance light travels in one year, about $9.5\times10^{15}$ m.

The **Milky Way** is one of many billions of galaxies making up the Universe. Its diameter is about **100 000 light-years**.

The distance $d$ to a far galaxy can be found from the **brightness of a supernova** in it: supernovae of a given type have a known actual brightness, so the observed brightness reveals how far away they are.

## Redshift and the expanding Universe

**Redshift** is an increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation from a receding star or galaxy.

Light from distant galaxies is redshifted compared with the same light emitted on Earth. Almost every galaxy is redshifted, and the further away it is the greater the shift — evidence that the **Universe is expanding**, which supports the **Big Bang theory**. The recession speed $v$ can be calculated from the size of the wavelength change.

## Hubble's law

The **Hubble constant** $H_0$ is the ratio of a galaxy's recession speed to its distance from Earth:

$$H_0 = \frac{v}{d}$$

The current estimate is $H_0 \approx 2.2\times10^{-18}$ s⁻¹.

Rearranged, $\dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{1}{H_0}$ gives an **estimate of the age of the Universe** — the time for which galaxies have been separating at their present rates. That a single number works for galaxies in every direction is evidence that all the matter in the Universe was once at a single point.

$$\frac{1}{H_0} = \frac{1}{2.2\times10^{-18}} = 4.5\times10^{17}\ \text{s} \approx 14\ \text{billion years}$$
