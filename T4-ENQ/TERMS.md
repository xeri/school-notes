# Literary, poetic and film glossary

Precision earns marks on its own, and the conventions criterion rewards naming a device correctly before analysing it. Where two terms are routinely confused, they are defined next to each other with the distinction stated.

## Narrative and structure

**Plot** — the sequence of events as the text arranges them.
**Story** — the events in the order they actually happened. *A non-linear text has one story and a rearranged plot.*
**Exposition** — the delivery of the information a reader or viewer needs to orient themselves.
**In medias res** — opening in the middle of the action, withholding orientation deliberately.
**Climax** — the point of greatest tension, after which the outcome is determined.
**Denouement** — the unravelling and settling of consequences after the climax.
**Cyclical structure** — a text that closes by returning to its opening image or situation, so the repetition carries the change.
**Frame narrative** — a story told inside another story, which raises the question of who is telling it and why.
**Flashback** — a scene showing something that happened before the present action.
**Foreshadowing** — a detail planted early whose significance only becomes clear later. *A flashback looks backwards at an event; foreshadowing points forwards to one that has not yet occurred.*
**Theme** — an active exploration of an idea: a premise examined, an argument advanced, a conclusion reached.
**Motif** — a concrete image, object or phrase that recurs. *A theme is an argument the text makes; a motif is a repeated element through which it makes it.*
**Idea** — a static given about the world of the text, prior to any exploration of it.
**Symbol** — a concrete thing standing for an abstraction, its meaning invested by context rather than inherent.
**Allegory** — a narrative in which the whole surface story maps systematically onto a second meaning.
**Allusion** — a reference to another text, person, event or myth, which imports that source's associations.
**Juxtaposition** — the placing of two unlike things side by side so that each comments on the other.
**Antithesis** — a balanced grammatical contrast of two opposed ideas within a sentence or line.
**Characterisation** — the impression formed of a character's nature and underlying motivations, and the means by which the writer builds it.
**Foil** — a character whose contrasting qualities expose something about another.
**Protagonist** — the character whose pursuit drives the narrative.
**Antagonist** — the force or character opposing the protagonist. *An antagonist may never appear on the page or screen.*
**Antihero** — a protagonist who lacks conventional heroic virtues yet holds the narrative's sympathy.
**Superficial meaning** — what is explicitly stated; on the line.
**Implied meaning** — what is not stated and must be inferred; between the lines. *The understanding criterion escalates from the first to the second, so a paragraph that reaches only superficial meaning is capped before the analysis is read.*

## Poetic form and metre

**Stanza** — a grouped set of lines, functioning as a unit of argument.
**Couplet** — two consecutive lines, usually rhymed; often used for closure or epigram.
**Quatrain** — a four-line stanza.
**Sonnet** — a fourteen-line poem in iambic pentameter, with a turn in the argument.
**Volta** — the turn in a poem's argument, conventionally at the ninth line of a sonnet but available anywhere.
**Blank verse** — unrhymed iambic pentameter; the register of elevated speech in Shakespeare.
**Free verse** — verse without regular metre or rhyme, where line breaks still do deliberate work.
**Metre** — the repeating pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line.
**Foot** — the basic metrical unit; an iamb is unstressed-stressed, a trochee stressed-unstressed, an anapaest two unstressed then a stressed, a dactyl a stressed then two unstressed, a spondee two stressed.
**Iambic pentameter** — five iambs per line; the metre closest to English speech.
**Enjambment** — sense running over the line ending without a pause, creating momentum or a double reading of the final word.
**End-stopping** — a line that closes on its punctuation, creating finality or flatness.
**Caesura** — a distinct pause inside a line, which can split it into two opposed halves.
**Refrain** — a line or phrase repeated across stanzas, which means something different on each return.
**Dramatic monologue** — a poem spoken throughout by a single invented persona to an implied listener.
**Persona** — the constructed speaking voice of a poem, who is not the poet.
**Elegy** — a poem of lament for the dead.
**Ode** — a formal address to a subject, usually elevated in tone.
**Pastoral** — a mode idealising rural life and landscape.
**Conceit** — an extended, ingenious and often strained comparison sustained across a poem.

## Figurative language

**Simile** — a comparison of two things using *like* or *as*.
**Metaphor** — a comparison that identifies one thing *as* another, with no comparative word. *The simile admits the comparison is a comparison; the metaphor asserts an identity, and the stronger claim is what you analyse.*
**Extended metaphor** — a metaphor sustained and developed across several lines or paragraphs.
**Personification** — the attribution of human qualities to a non-living thing or an abstraction. *Only non-living things can be personified; a human character cannot be.*
**Pathetic fallacy** — landscape, weather or surroundings presented as reflecting human emotion.
**Metonymy** — naming something by an associated thing, as *the crown* for monarchy.
**Synecdoche** — naming a whole by one of its parts, as *hands* for workers.
**Hyperbole** — deliberate exaggeration for effect.
**Litotes** — deliberate understatement by negation, as *not unimpressive*.
**Oxymoron** — two contradictory terms placed together in one phrase.
**Paradox** — a statement that appears self-contradictory yet discloses a truth.
**Sensory imagery** — language appealing to sight, sound, touch, smell or taste.
**Synaesthesia** — the description of one sense in the vocabulary of another.
**Semantic field** — a cluster of words drawn from a single domain, such as commerce, disease or warfare.
**Connotation** — the associations a word carries beyond its literal sense.
**Denotation** — a word's literal, dictionary meaning.
**Register** — the level and variety of language chosen: formal, colloquial, archaic, technical, biblical.
**Euphemism** — a mild substitute for something harsh or taboo.
**Dysphemism** — a deliberately harsh substitute for something neutral.
**Anaphora** — the repetition of the same words at the beginning of successive clauses or lines.
**Epistrophe** — the repetition of the same words at the end of successive clauses or lines.
**Epizeuxis** — the immediate repetition of a word for pressure, as *"The handkerchief!"* three times over.
**Tricolon** — a group of three parallel elements.
**Asyndeton** — the omission of conjunctions from a list, which accelerates it.
**Polysyndeton** — the piling up of conjunctions, which slows a list and makes it feel endless.
**Chiasmus** — a reversal of grammatical structure in the second of two parallel phrases.
**Rhetorical question** — a question asked for effect, carrying an implied answer.

## Sound

**Alliteration** — the repetition of initial consonant sounds in nearby words.
**Assonance** — the repetition of vowel sounds in nearby words.
**Consonance** — the repetition of consonant sounds anywhere within nearby words, not only at the start. *Assonance repeats vowels; consonance repeats consonants — name which, and name the sound.*
**Sibilance** — the repetition of *s*, *sh* and *z* sounds, producing hush, whisper or menace.
**Plosives** — the abrupt consonants *p*, *b*, *t*, *d*, *k* and *g*, percussive and interruptive.
**Fricatives** — the continuous consonants *f*, *v* and *th*, breathy and sustained.
**Onomatopoeia** — a word whose sound imitates the thing it names.
**Full rhyme** — identical final stressed vowels and following consonants.
**Half rhyme** — approximate rhyme, raising an expectation of chime and then denying it.
**Internal rhyme** — rhyme occurring within a single line.
**Eye rhyme** — words that look as though they rhyme but do not sound alike.
**Rhyme scheme** — the pattern of line endings across a stanza, written as *abab*, *aabb* and so on.
**Cacophony** — a deliberately harsh, discordant arrangement of sounds.
**Euphony** — a deliberately smooth, pleasing arrangement of sounds.
**Rhythm** — the movement of stress through a line, whether or not it follows a regular metre.

## Drama

**Soliloquy** — a speech delivered by a character alone on stage, giving the audience their unguarded thought.
**Monologue** — an extended speech delivered to others on stage.
**Aside** — a short remark heard by the audience but not by the other characters present.
**Stichomythia** — rapid alternating single lines of dialogue, usually in conflict or interrogation.
**Shared line** — a single verse line completed by two speakers, which dramatises intimacy, interruption or takeover.
**Dramatic irony** — a situation in which the audience knows something a character does not.
**Verbal irony** — words that mean the opposite of what they say.
**Situational irony** — an outcome that reverses what the circumstances led us to expect.
**Tragedy** — a dramatic form in which a protagonist's fall follows from character and circumstance.
**Hamartia** — the flaw or error in the protagonist that makes the catastrophe possible.
**Peripeteia** — the reversal of fortune, the moment the action turns.
**Anagnorisis** — the protagonist's recognition of the truth, usually too late.
**Catharsis** — the purging of pity and fear said to be produced in the audience of a tragedy.
**Stagecraft** — the use of staging resources for meaning: entrances, levels, concealment, torches, props, the bed.
**Blocking** — the arrangement and movement of actors in the playing space.
**Prop** — an object handled on stage, which accumulates meaning through who holds it and who is denied it.
**Tone** — a speaker's attitude towards the subject, whether narrator, persona, character or author.
**Mood** — the emotional feeling of the world of the text, built from setting, events and interactions. *Tone belongs to the voice; mood belongs to the world. The same sombre world can be described in a flippant tone, and that gap is usually the point.*
**Atmosphere** — the prevailing feeling of a scene or setting; used interchangeably with mood.

## Film technique

**Mise-en-scène** — everything arranged in front of the camera: setting, props, costume, lighting, blocking and performance.
**Framing** — what is included in the image, and what is therefore excluded.
**Frame within a frame** — a subject composed through a doorway, window, mirror or blinds, implying surveillance or entrapment.
**Shot** — a single uninterrupted run of the camera.
**Long take** — an unusually extended single shot, whose duration itself becomes the content.
**Close-up** — a shot in which a face or object fills the frame, forcing intimacy and denying context.
**Long shot** — a shot showing the whole figure within its surroundings.
**High angle** — the camera above the subject, looking down, diminishing it.
**Low angle** — the camera below the subject, looking up, conferring stature or threat.
**Dutch angle** — a tilted horizon, signalling instability or wrongness.
**Tracking shot** — the camera physically travelling with or alongside the action.
**Pan** — a horizontal pivot of the camera from a fixed position.
**Zoom** — a change of focal length rather than of camera position, which flattens space.
**Shallow focus** — a narrow plane in sharp focus, isolating a subject from its surroundings.
**Deep focus** — foreground and background both sharp, keeping context and relationships legible.
**Rack focus** — a shift of focus within a shot, redirecting attention without a cut.
**High key lighting** — bright, even, low-contrast illumination with few shadows.
**Low key lighting** — predominantly dark, high-contrast illumination with deep shadow.
**Chiaroscuro** — extreme contrast of light and dark within a single image.
**Motivated lighting** — light that appears to originate from a source visible or implied within the scene.
**Practical** — a lamp or fire present in the shot that actually provides its light.
**Colour palette** — the recurring range of hues across a film or sequence.
**Grading** — the adjustment of colour after shooting, which determines what the palette suppresses.
**Cut** — a direct join between two shots.
**Continuity editing** — cutting designed to be spatially coherent and effectively invisible.
**180-degree rule** — the convention of keeping the camera on one side of an imaginary line between subjects, so screen direction stays consistent; crossing it disorients deliberately.
**Shot / reverse shot** — the alternating pattern that conventionally covers a conversation.
**Eyeline match** — a cut from a character looking to the thing they see.
**Match cut** — a cut joining two shots through visual, graphic or aural similarity, asserting an equivalence.
**Jump cut** — a cut within a continuous action that breaks its time, producing disruption.
**Cross-cutting** — alternation between two simultaneous actions, building tension and forcing comparison.
**Elliptical editing** — the omission of part of an action, requiring the viewer to infer it.
**Montage** — a compressed sequence of shots making an argument by accumulation rather than by scene.
**Pace** — the rate of cutting, measured by average shot length.
**Diegetic sound** — sound originating inside the world of the film, audible to the characters.
**Non-diegetic sound** — sound the characters cannot hear: score, voiceover, added effect. *The test is simply whether a character could hear it; source music playing on a radio is diegetic even though it is music.*
**Score** — music composed for the film, laid over the image.
**Leitmotif** — a musical phrase attached to a character or idea, which makes a connection on its return.
**Sound bridge** — sound carried across a cut, beginning under the previous scene or continuing into the next.
**Voiceover** — narration spoken over the image, raising the question of when and by whom.
**Silence** — the deliberate removal of sound or score, which only registers in a film that has established a soundscape.
**Restricted narration** — the viewer is limited to what one character knows, which generates suspense.
**Omniscient narration** — the viewer knows more than the characters, which generates dramatic irony.

## Essay and argument

**Thesis** — a single arguable sentence stating an informed position in answer to the question.
**Point sentence** — the opening claim of a body paragraph, which supports the thesis and gives a reason to believe it.
**Rationale** — the brief indication, in the introduction, of the sequence of points the essay will make.
**Hook** — an opening sentence stating a broad, universally applicable idea, which the next sentence narrows to the text.
**Summation** — the restatement of the thesis in a conclusion, now as something demonstrated.
**Discourse marker** — a word or phrase signalling the logical relationship between sentences or paragraphs.
**Concession** — the acknowledgement of a legitimate opposing point before qualifying or answering it.
**Qualification** — the narrowing of a claim to the conditions under which it holds.
**Author-intention verb** — a verb attributing purpose to the writer, such as *exposes*, *undercuts*, *satirises* or *interrogates*.
**Integration** — the placing of a quotation inside your own grammatical sentence rather than beside it.
**Paraphrase** — restating the text in other words. *Paraphrase is not analysis: swapping a writer's diction for synonyms explains nothing about why the original was chosen.*
**Comma splice** — two independent clauses joined by only a comma; the commonest expression error in timed essays.
**Assertion adverb** — *clearly*, *obviously*, *undoubtedly*, used as a substitute for argument.
