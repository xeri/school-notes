# Biblical and mythical allusions

Both Paper 1 texts, the prose and the poem, contain at least one biblical or mythical allusion, and the mark schedule gives it a criterion of its own (criterion 3, 5 of the 25 marks). To score it you must **identify** it accurately, **name the source**, **map** the parallel, and **analyse the effect**, ideally including where the text departs from the source. The method for writing the paragraph, and the two-signal test against overclaiming, are in [the unfamiliar texts notes](../notes/unfamiliar-texts.md), under *Biblical and mythical allusion*. This page is the reference library: the stories themselves, the images that give them away, and the past Pre-Q texts that used them.

**How the page is organised.** First a bare list of names, then one table for quick revision, then a full explanation of each allusion further down. Bible quotations are short phrases from the King James Version, which is the wording writers usually echo.

## Full explanations

Each entry gives the story, the key images and phrases, what writers use it for, how to spot it in an unseen text, and the past Pre-Q texts that used it. "Past Pre-Q texts" means the Paper 1 papers and school exercises in the course sources: the 2022 unfamiliar-texts exercise (*The Wild Laughter*), the 2024 paper (*eden*, "Ajar"), the 2025 paper (*Crime and Punishment*, "Ice Palace") and the school's Destructors model commentary. Where none used it, the entry says so. Do not assume a future paper repeats a past source; use these as models of how an allusion is built and analysed.

### Biblical: Old Testament

#### Creation (Genesis 1)

- **The story.** In the beginning the earth is *"without form, and void"* and dark. God creates over six days: light, sky, land and sea, plants, sun and moon, animals, and finally humans *"in his own image"*, and calls it *"very good"*. He rests on the seventh day.
- **Key images and phrases.** *Let there be light*; darkness and the deep; the void; the first day; naming things; seven days; clay or dust shaped into life.
- **What writers use it for.** To give creative or destructive acts cosmic weight. A character who makes order from nothing becomes godlike; a scene of chaos can echo the void before creation.
- **How to spot it.** Light appearing in darkness; something made out of nothing; a seven-part or day-by-day structure; characters working "with the seriousness of creators"; the word *beginning*.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** The school's Destructors model commentary reads *"light"* appearing as the boys work as a Genesis allusion: the boys become God and the house the void, so that destruction becomes a kind of creation. Also relevant to Othello: *"when I love thee not, / Chaos is come again"* (III.iii) invokes the pre-creation void.

#### Eden and the Fall (Genesis 2 to 3)

- **The story.** God places Adam and Eve in a garden with one prohibition: they must not eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The serpent, *"more subtil than any beast"*, tells Eve *"ye shall not surely die"* and that they will be *"as gods"*. She eats and gives to Adam; they know they are naked, sew *fig leaves* into aprons, and hide. God curses the serpent, sets pain on Eve and toil on Adam (*"dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return"*), and expels them, setting cherubim and a flaming sword at the garden's gate. The *apple* belongs to later tradition; Genesis says only *fruit*.
- **Key images and phrases.** Garden, wall, gate, tree, serpent, forbidden fruit or apple, nakedness, fig leaves, shame, expulsion, the knowledge of good and evil, paradise lost, *this other Eden*.
- **What writers use it for.** Innocence ending; temptation and transgression; the cost of knowledge; the origin of shame; exile from a protected world. Writers often alter it: a garden with no prohibition, a Fall with no sin.
- **How to spot it.** Look for several signals together: an enclosed garden or orchard, a wall or boundary, a forbidden thing, fruit, a tempter, shame about bodies, an angel, a banishment. One apple alone is not enough.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2024 prose, *eden* by Jim Crace: the angel Alum peers from a tree over the wall at *"forbidden ground"*, calls the world *"eden amplified"*, and an apple is thrown from the courtyard. 2025 poem, "Ice Palace" by Margaret Atwood: *"Another demi-paradise"* echoes Shakespeare's *Richard II*, where John of Gaunt calls England *"This other Eden, demi-paradise"*, and the closing prohibition (*"Do not look"*) repeats Eden's forbidden thing. The *"named and thus created"* also recalls Adam's naming in Genesis.

#### Cain and Abel (Genesis 4)

- **The story.** Adam and Eve's sons: Abel keeps sheep and Cain farms. God respects Abel's offering and not Cain's. Cain, angry, lures Abel into a field and kills him. God asks where Abel is; Cain answers *"Am I my brother's keeper?"* God says *"the voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground"*, curses Cain to be a fugitive and a vagabond, and puts *a mark upon Cain* so no one will kill him. Cain settles in the land of Nod, east of Eden. Before the killing, God warns Cain that sin *"lieth at the door"*.
- **Key images and phrases.** Two brothers; offerings; jealousy; a field; blood crying from the ground; *my brother's keeper*; *the mark of Cain*; a wanderer; the first murder.
- **What writers use it for.** Sibling hatred; violence within the family; guilt that marks a person; the outcast; the first sin after the Fall, so violence is shown as inherited.
- **How to spot it.** Two brothers (especially twins), a jealous rivalry over favour, an injury or killing between them, a mark or a wanderer, blood, a parent who failed to see.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** The 2022 unfamiliar-texts exercise, *The Wild Laughter* by Caoilinn Hughes: Father Shaughnessy confesses how, as a boy, he made his twin Jason swallow magnets, with the Devil *"perched on his shoulder"*. The school's model says Shaughnessy *"clearly alludes to Cain and Abel"*. The task asks what the **similarities and differences** are. Similarities: brothers, jealous rivalry, a deliberate injury, guilt carried for life, a devil urging evil (cf. sin at the door). Differences: the harm is slow and hidden, done with magnets and a ritual parody rather than in a field; the brother Jason started the injury first by making him swallow the shell, so Shaughnessy is both victim and aggressor; the passage never confirms that Jason dies; and the story is told to a second brother in a confessional, so the confession passes the burden on.

#### The Flood and Noah (Genesis 6 to 9)

- **The story.** God sees that the earth is *"filled with violence"* and resolves to destroy it. Noah, a righteous man, builds an ark of gopher wood and takes his family and two of every animal. It rains *forty days and forty nights*. When the waters fall, the ark rests on Ararat; Noah sends a raven and then a dove, which returns with an olive leaf. God sets a *rainbow* in the cloud as a covenant never to flood the earth again.
- **Key images and phrases.** Rain, flood, ark, pairs of animals, dove, olive branch, rainbow, forty days, waters rising and falling, *a second chance*.
- **What writers use it for.** Cleansing destruction; the survival of a few; covenant and a new beginning; sometimes ironic, as a flood that washes away nothing.
- **How to spot it.** Relentless rain, rising water, a boat or refuge, a bird returning with a leaf, a rainbow, pairs.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources; it is a standard allusion for storm and water imagery.

#### The Tower of Babel (Genesis 11)

- **The story.** The people of Shinar, speaking one language, say *"let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven"*. God sees their pride, confuses their speech so that they cannot understand each other, and scatters them across the earth. The city is named Babel.
- **Key images and phrases.** A tower that reaches the sky; bricks; one language; confusion; babble; scattering.
- **What writers use it for.** Hubris and overreach; the breakdown of communication; modern cities and technology as arrogance; many voices that cannot understand one another.
- **How to spot it.** A very tall structure, mixed languages or incomprehensible speech, a city, a punishment of scattering or division.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Lot's wife and Sodom (Genesis 19)

- **The story.** God destroys Sodom and Gomorrah with fire and brimstone. Angels lead Lot and his family out with the command not to look back. Lot's wife *"looked back from behind him, and she became a pillar of salt"*.
- **Key images and phrases.** A burning city; fleeing; a forbidden look; a pillar of salt; nostalgia.
- **What writers use it for.** The danger of looking back at a lost life; regret; curiosity punished; an echo of Orpheus.
- **How to spot it.** A command not to look; someone frozen or turned to stone or salt after looking back at a place being destroyed.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources. The repeated *"Do not look"* in "Ice Palace" belongs mainly to Bluebeard, but a forbidden backward or forward look is a shared pattern.

#### Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 22)

- **The story.** God tests Abraham: *"Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest"* and offer him as a burnt offering. Abraham takes wood and fire up a mountain; Isaac asks *"where is the lamb for a burnt offering?"* Abraham answers that God will provide. Abraham binds Isaac and raises the knife; an angel stops him, and a ram caught in a thicket is sacrificed instead.
- **Key images and phrases.** A father and son climbing a hill; wood and fire; a knife; binding; an angel's voice; a ram in a thicket; *the sacrifice of the young*.
- **What writers use it for.** Obedience to authority against love; the terror of faith; the old sending the young to die (Wilfred Owen's "The Parable of the Old Man and the Young" is the classic war-poem use); parents' power over children.
- **How to spot it.** A father leading a child somewhere to be harmed; a knife or altar; a last-minute substitution.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Jacob and Esau (Genesis 25 to 33)

- **The story.** Twin sons of Isaac and Rebekah; Esau is hairy and a hunter, Jacob smooth and quiet. Esau, famished, sells his birthright to Jacob for *"a mess of pottage"* (lentil stew). Later Rebekah helps Jacob deceive blind Isaac by wearing goatskins to pose as Esau; Isaac says *"The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau"* and gives the blessing to Jacob. Years later the brothers meet and are reconciled. Jacob also dreams of a ladder to heaven and wrestles an angel, taking the name Israel.
- **Key images and phrases.** Twins; birthright; pottage; blindness; a disguise; stolen blessing; goatskins.
- **What writers use it for.** Sibling rivalry and deceit; the younger supplanting the elder; family favouritism; inheritance; disguise and a stolen identity.
- **How to spot it.** Twins or brothers competing for a parent's favour; a trick played on an old or blind parent; a trade of a birthright for food.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources, but it pairs naturally with Cain and Abel when twins appear, as in *The Wild Laughter*.

#### Joseph (Genesis 37 to 50)

- **The story.** Jacob's favourite son Joseph is given a *coat of many colours* and dreams of ruling his brothers. They throw him into a pit and sell him to traders for twenty pieces of silver, then tell their father he is dead. In Egypt he is falsely accused by Potiphar's wife, jailed, and rises by interpreting Pharaoh's dreams of seven fat and seven lean cattle. When famine brings the brothers to Egypt he forgives them: *"ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good"*.
- **Key images and phrases.** The coat; the pit; dreams; silver; a famine; a brother who returns as a ruler.
- **What writers use it for.** Family jealousy and betrayal; the favoured child; reversal of fortune; forgiveness and providence.
- **How to spot it.** A favoured brother in a distinctive garment, a pit or well, a sale into slavery, dreams that foretell power.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Moses and the Exodus (Exodus)

- **The story.** Moses, saved as a baby in a basket of bulrushes, is called by God from a burning bush: *"I AM THAT I AM"*. He demands *"Let my people go"*; ten plagues strike Egypt; the Israelites eat the Passover meal and flee; the Red Sea parts for them and drowns Pharaoh's army. God gives manna in the desert and the Ten Commandments at Sinai; while Moses is on the mountain the people worship a golden calf. The people wander for forty years, and Moses dies in sight of the promised land.
- **Key images and phrases.** Burning bush; plagues; parting waters; wilderness; manna; tablets of stone; golden calf; promised land; *I am that I am*; forty years.
- **What writers use it for.** Liberation from slavery or oppression; a long, hard journey towards a promised place; leaders who do not live to arrive; corruption of faith through idols.
- **How to spot it.** Escape from a tyrant, crossing water, a desert journey, bread from nowhere, a leader on a mountain.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in Paper 1 sources. In *Othello*, Iago's *"I am not what I am"* (I.i) inverts God's *I am that I am*, which is how a mock-religious allusion works inside a play.

#### David and Goliath (1 Samuel 17)

- **The story.** The Philistine champion Goliath, *six cubits and a span* tall, challenges Israel for forty days. The shepherd boy David refuses King Saul's armour, takes a sling and *five smooth stones* from a brook, and strikes Goliath's forehead; he then cuts off the giant's head with his own sword.
- **Key images and phrases.** A giant; a boy with a sling; a stone; refusing armour; the underdog.
- **What writers use it for.** The weak overcoming the strong; ingenuity against brute force; courage and faith; small people against institutions.
- **How to spot it.** A huge opponent and a small, poorly armed one; a thrown stone.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Samson and Delilah (Judges 13 to 16)

- **The story.** Samson, a Nazirite whose hair has never been cut, has superhuman strength: he kills a lion, slaughters Philistines, and carries off the gates of Gaza. He loves Delilah. The Philistines bribe her to find the source of his strength; after three false answers she wears him down and he tells her about his hair. She cuts it while he sleeps, he is captured, blinded and set to grind at the mill. His hair regrows, and he pulls down the pillars of the Philistine temple, dying with his enemies.
- **Key images and phrases.** Long hair; a haircut; a seductress; blindness; grinding corn; pillars; the strongman brought low.
- **What writers use it for.** Strength undone by trust or desire; betrayal by a lover; the cost of revealing a secret; blindness (literal and moral). Milton's *Samson Agonistes* is the major literary retelling.
- **How to spot it.** A powerful person weakened through a lover's trickery; cutting of hair; blinding.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Job (The Book of Job)

- **The story.** Job is *"perfect and upright"*. In a wager with Satan, God allows Job's children, flocks and health to be taken, leaving him covered in boils. Job says *"the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord"*. His friends insist he must have sinned; he demands an answer. God replies from a whirlwind: *"Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?"* Job humbles himself, and his fortunes are restored.
- **Key images and phrases.** Boils; ashes; the whirlwind; *the patience of Job*; *Job's comforters* (friends who add to the pain); the wager.
- **What writers use it for.** Undeserved suffering; faith under strain; the silence or cruelty of God; a character who endures.
- **How to spot it.** A good person suffering disaster after disaster; friends who blame them; a questioning of divine justice.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Jonah (The Book of Jonah)

- **The story.** God tells the prophet Jonah to preach in Nineveh. Jonah flees by ship to Tarshish; a storm threatens the ship and the sailors throw him overboard. *"A great fish"* swallows him; he prays inside it for three days and nights and is vomited onto dry land. He then preaches, the city repents, and Jonah sulks under a gourd that withers, learning about God's mercy. (The text says *a great fish*, not a whale.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A storm at sea; thrown overboard; the belly of the fish; three days; running away; a *Jonah* (an unlucky person who brings bad luck to others).
- **What writers use it for.** Avoiding one's duty; being swallowed by something huge; a descent followed by rebirth; reluctance and hypocrisy.
- **How to spot it.** A character fleeing a task, a storm, being swallowed or enclosed in a dark belly-like space, then expelled.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Daniel (The Book of Daniel)

- **The story.** Daniel is a Jewish exile at the Babylonian court. Three friends, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, refuse to worship a golden statue and survive the fiery furnace (Daniel 3). Daniel interprets King Nebuchadnezzar's dreams; at Belshazzar's feast a hand writes on the wall *MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN* ("you are weighed and found wanting"), and the king dies that night (Daniel 5). Daniel prays despite a ban and is thrown into the lions' den; God shuts the lions' mouths (Daniel 6).
- **Key images and phrases.** Lions' den; fiery furnace; *the writing on the wall*; a feast; an interpreter of dreams.
- **What writers use it for.** Faith and integrity in a hostile place; survival of apparently certain death; a sign of coming doom.
- **How to spot it.** A man unharmed among beasts or flames; a warning appearing as writing; a king's feast interrupted.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

### Biblical: New Testament

#### The Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11 to 32)

- **The story.** A younger son demands his inheritance, goes abroad and wastes it in *"riotous living"*. Famine strikes and he ends up feeding pigs, longing for their husks. He decides *"I will arise and go to my father"*. The father sees him *"a great way off"*, runs to him, and gives him a robe, a ring and a *fatted calf* feast. The dutiful elder brother is angry; the father says *"this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found"*.
- **Key images and phrases.** A younger son leaving; wasted money; pigs; a long road home; a running father; robe, ring, fatted calf; an angry elder brother.
- **What writers use it for.** Waste and repentance; unearned forgiveness; homecoming; resentment between siblings; the family that takes a returnee back.
- **How to spot it.** A child who returns after wasting their inheritance or youth; a parent who forgives without conditions; a feast of welcome.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25 to 37)

- **The story.** Asked *"who is my neighbour?"*, Christ tells of a man beaten by robbers on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. A priest and a Levite see him and *"passed by on the other side"*. A Samaritan, a despised outsider, binds his wounds with oil and wine, takes him to an inn and pays for his care. Christ says *"Go, and do thou likewise."*
- **Key images and phrases.** A wounded traveller on a road; people who pass by; a stranger who stops; oil and wine; an inn.
- **What writers use it for.** Compassion that crosses social, religious or racial boundaries; criticism of respectable indifference; a "good Samaritan" as an unexpected helper.
- **How to spot it.** Someone beaten or ill at the roadside; a stranger of lower status who helps while others ignore.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The lost sheep (Luke 15:3 to 7, Matthew 18:12)

- **The story.** A shepherd with a hundred sheep leaves the *"ninety and nine"* to search for the one that is lost, and when he finds it he lays it on his shoulders and calls his friends to rejoice. Christ says there is more joy in heaven over one repentant sinner than over ninety-nine who need none. It sits beside the lost coin and the Prodigal Son in Luke 15.
- **Key images and phrases.** Shepherd, flock, a single sheep gone astray, carried on the shoulders, rejoicing; the Good Shepherd; *Psalm 23*.
- **What writers use it for.** Care for the one who strays; the worth of every individual; rescue; leaders as shepherds; innocence and vulnerability.
- **How to spot it.** A figure who looks after or searches for someone who has wandered; a flock; a stray; a rescued lamb.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The loaves and fishes (Mark 6, John 6)

- **The story.** A crowd of about five thousand follows Christ and has nothing to eat. A boy offers five barley loaves and two fish; Christ blesses and breaks them, and everyone eats until satisfied. Twelve baskets of fragments are left over.
- **Key images and phrases.** Five loaves and two fishes; breaking bread; a crowd on a hillside; leftovers; a miracle of plenty.
- **What writers use it for.** Plenty from scarcity; sharing; generosity; a meagre meal that sustains many; communion; ironically, a meal that does not stretch.
- **How to spot it.** Food multiplying; a small portion feeding a crowd; bread and fish together.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources. The "all-you-can-eat breakfast" of "Ice Palace" is a commercial counterfeit of abundance, but it is not a signal strong enough on its own.

#### The woman who anoints Christ's feet (Luke 7:36 to 50)

- **The story.** Christ dines at the house of Simon the Pharisee. *"A woman in the city, which was a sinner"* enters with an alabaster box of ointment, weeps over his feet, wipes them with her hair, kisses them and anoints them. Simon thinks that a true prophet would know what sort of woman she is. Christ replies that her sins, *"which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much"*, and tells her *"Thy faith hath saved thee; go in peace."*
- **Key images and phrases.** A sinful woman; tears; hair used as a towel; kissing feet; ointment; a self-righteous host; forgiveness through love.
- **What writers use it for.** Humility and repentance; the outcast closer to grace than the respectable; physical self-abasement as devotion. A writer may **reverse** it.
- **How to spot it.** Someone kneeling or bowing to kiss another's feet; a woman branded a sinner by society; tears; an onlooker who judges.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2025 prose, *Crime and Punishment* by Dostoevsky. Raskolnikov drops to the ground and kisses Sonia's foot, calling her *"a great sinner"* and saying *"I bowed down to all the suffering of humanity"*. The school's reading is that Dostoevsky **reverses** Luke 7: here the sinner's foot is kissed, and the man who kneels is the one in need of absolution. Sonia becomes a Christ-like figure whose suffering is redemptive, and Raskolnikov takes the place of the woman. The two signals that make it secure are the label *sinner* and the foot-kiss.

#### Mary Magdalene (Luke 8, John 20)

- **The story.** Mary of Magdala is a follower of Christ from whom *"seven devils"* were cast out (Luke 8:2). She stands at the cross and is the first to find the empty tomb. In John 20 she weeps outside the tomb, mistakes the risen Christ for the gardener, and he says to her *"Touch me not"* (the *noli me tangere* of Latin tradition). The Bible does not call her a prostitute or identify her with the woman of Luke 7; Pope Gregory I (591) merged the two, and that popular picture of the reformed prostitute is the one writers usually invoke.
- **Key images and phrases.** A woman weeping at a tomb; long hair; a jar of ointment; seven devils; a garden at dawn; *Touch me not*.
- **What writers use it for.** The reformed outcast; female devotion and witness; grief that turns into recognition.
- **How to spot it.** A fallen or stigmatised woman who is spiritually the strongest figure; grief at a grave; a garden at dawn.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** No past paper names her. She is the figure behind the popular memory of the Luke 7 woman, so she is useful background for Sonia in the 2025 *Crime and Punishment* extract.

#### Lazarus (John 11)

- **The story.** Mary and Martha send for Christ when their brother Lazarus is ill; he arrives after Lazarus has been dead four days. *"Jesus wept."* At the tomb he says *"I am the resurrection, and the life"*, tells them to remove the stone, and cries *"Lazarus, come forth."* Lazarus comes out still wrapped in graveclothes. (Do not confuse him with the beggar Lazarus in Luke 16, the poor man at the rich man's gate.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A tomb with a stone; four days; the stench of death; *Lazarus, come forth*; graveclothes; a man called back from the dead.
- **What writers use it for.** Revival of a person who seems spiritually or physically dead; hope for a lost soul; a miracle that turns despair around.
- **How to spot it.** Someone "dead" returning; calling a person out of a dark or enclosed place; a four-day gap.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2025 prose, *Crime and Punishment*. Beyond the extract (revision knowledge, not something to assume in an unseen paper), Sonia reads Raskolnikov the raising of Lazarus soon after this conversation, framing herself as the one who can bring him back to life. In the exam, use it only as one clause, and only if you know the novel.

#### The Last Supper (Matthew 26, John 13)

- **The story.** On the eve of his arrest, Christ shares a Passover meal with his twelve disciples. He breaks bread (*"Take, eat; this is my body"*) and gives wine (*"this is my blood of the new testament"*), and says *"one of you shall betray me"*. In John he washes his disciples' feet. Judas leaves to carry out the betrayal. The Church re-enacts the meal in the Eucharist.
- **Key images and phrases.** A long table; thirteen people; bread and wine; a betrayal announced; washing feet; a final meal.
- **What writers use it for.** Fellowship that hides treachery; sacrament and sacrifice; a final shared meal before a loss; parody when a holy rite is turned to something profane.
- **How to spot it.** A table with a betrayer among friends; bread broken and wine shared as a ritual; someone placing something on another's tongue; the Eucharist.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2022 *The Wild Laughter*: Shaughnessy places a magnet on his brother's tongue *"like the Eucharist"*, a parody of Communion in which the sacrament becomes an injury. See the Eucharist entry below.

#### Judas and the thirty pieces of silver (Matthew 26 to 27)

- **The story.** Judas Iscariot, one of the twelve, goes to the chief priests and asks what they will give him; they weigh out *"thirty pieces of silver"*. In the Garden of Gethsemane he greets Christ with a kiss as the signal to the soldiers. Seized by remorse, Judas throws the silver into the temple and hangs himself; the priests buy the *field of blood* with it.
- **Key images and phrases.** A kiss as a betrayal; thirty silver coins; Gethsemane; a hanging; blood money; the betrayer at the table.
- **What writers use it for.** Betrayal by an intimate; treachery bought; guilt that destroys the betrayer; a *Judas* as a synonym for traitor.
- **How to spot it.** A kiss, silver or coins, a friend who gives another up for money, a betrayal in a garden or at night.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources; "a kiss, silver, an intimate betrayal" is among the school's listed signals.

#### Pontius Pilate washing his hands (Matthew 27:24)

- **The story.** The Roman governor Pilate finds no fault in Christ but fears a riot. He *"took water, and washed his hands before the multitude, saying, I am innocent of the blood of this just person: see ye to it"*, and hands Christ over to be crucified. Elsewhere he asks *"What is truth?"* (John 18:38).
- **Key images and phrases.** A basin of water; washing hands before a crowd; *innocent of the blood*; an official who gives way; a bowl and a towel.
- **What writers use it for.** Evasion of moral responsibility; the bureaucrat who lets harm happen; guilt that cannot be washed away. Lady Macbeth's hand-washing is the closest Shakespeare parallel.
- **How to spot it.** Hand-washing as a gesture of disowning blame; someone with authority who says it is not their decision.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The Crucifixion (the four Gospels)

- **The story.** Christ is condemned, mocked by soldiers who dress him in a robe and press *a crown of thorns* on his head, and made to carry his cross to Golgotha, *the place of a skull*. He is nailed up between two thieves under a sign reading "King of the Jews". Soldiers gamble for his clothes, he is offered vinegar on a sponge, cries *"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"* and dies (in John, *"It is finished"*).
- **Key images and phrases.** Cross, nails, crown of thorns, vinegar, a spear in the side, a skull, the carrying of a burden, three crosses on a hill, darkness at noon.
- **What writers use it for.** Sacrifice; innocent suffering; martyrdom; scapegoating; a person carrying a heavy burden (*to bear one's cross*).
- **How to spot it.** Thorns; a heavy burden carried on the back up a hill; a figure with arms stretched out; nails; a mocking crowd.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None named. The Gospel frame of the 2025 *Crime and Punishment* extract (Sonia as a redemptive sufferer) draws on the same story of suffering for others.

#### The Resurrection (the four Gospels)

- **The story.** Three days after the Crucifixion, women go to the tomb at dawn and find the stone rolled away. An angel says *"He is not here: for he is risen"* (Matthew 28:6). Christ appears to Mary Magdalene, to two disciples on the road to Emmaus, and to Thomas, who will only believe if he can touch the wounds.
- **Key images and phrases.** An empty tomb; a stone rolled away; the third day; dawn; Easter; doubting Thomas; a gardener who is more than he seems.
- **What writers use it for.** Rebirth; hope after death; the failure of death; renewal of a community. A text can also use a *failed* resurrection to show hope denied.
- **How to spot it.** An empty grave or space, a stone moved, dawn after a night of grief, "three days".
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources. Compare the Lazarus entry.

#### Revelation and the Apocalypse (Revelation)

- **The story.** John of Patmos records visions of the end of the world. The Lamb opens seven seals, and four horsemen ride out (conquest, war, famine and a *pale horse*, *"and his name that sat on him was Death"*). Trumpets sound, plagues fall, the Beast (whose number is 666) and the whore of Babylon rule, and a final battle follows. God makes *"a new heaven and a new earth"*, and the text names him *Alpha and Omega*. *Apocalypse* means "unveiling".
- **Key images and phrases.** Four horsemen; seven seals; trumpets; the Beast; 666; Babylon; Armageddon; the lake of fire; a new heaven and a new earth; *the end of days*.
- **What writers use it for.** Catastrophe on a world scale; judgement; prophecy of ruin; and, in the final chapters, renewal and a new order.
- **How to spot it.** Riders, seals, trumpets, a beast, scorched or ruined worlds, repeated sevens, and a language of final judgement.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

### Other religious allusions the school's texts use

#### Lucifer and the Devil (Isaiah 14, Revelation 12, Genesis 3)

- **The story.** Tradition reads Isaiah 14:12, *"How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!"*, together with Revelation 12 as the story of an angel who rebelled out of pride and was cast out of heaven. He becomes Satan, the tempter who takes the serpent's form in Eden, and later the Devil of popular imagination. Milton's *Paradise Lost* makes him a tragic rebel.
- **Key images and phrases.** A fallen angel; pride; *the Devil on one's shoulder*; a tempter at the ear; hell; temptation presented as an opportunity; horns and fire in popular versions.
- **What writers use it for.** Pride and rebellion; evil as outside influence or as a presence inside; an excuse for wrongdoing (*the Devil made me do it*).
- **How to spot it.** A character who blames the Devil; an adviser whispering at the ear; a fall from grace; the name Lucifer.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2022 *The Wild Laughter*: Shaughnessy says the Devil was *"perched on his shoulder"* and later *"crept off his shoulder and climbed onto mine"*; *"Lucifer couldn't believe his luck."* This both externalises the boys' malice and shows Shaughnessy using the Devil to avoid blame. Iago's *"Hell and night"* soliloquy (*Othello*, I.iii) is an example from Paper 3.

#### The Eucharist, the Trinity and Catholic penance (Wild Laughter)

- **The story.** In the Eucharist (Communion), Catholics believe the consecrated bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ (transubstantiation). The Trinity is the doctrine that God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit. A Catholic confesses sins to a priest in a confessional box, is given a penance such as saying *Hail Marys* (a prayer to Mary, the mother of Jesus) or fasting, and ends with the Act of Contrition, which is the prayer of sorrow.
- **Key images and phrases.** A wafer or bread placed on the tongue; kneeling; *One for God the Father, God the Son, the Holy Spirit*; the confessional grille and curtain; Hail Marys; thirty days' fasting.
- **What writers use it for.** Ritual and sacrament; guilt and absolution; sacred words twisted into something cruel, so the irony cuts deeper; the failure of the church's remedies.
- **How to spot it.** Priests, confessionals, prayers counted out, anything placed on a tongue, threefold repetition tied to Father, Son and Spirit.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2022 *The Wild Laughter*. Shaughnessy places each magnet on Jason's tongue *"like the Eucharist"* and counts them out as *"One for God the Father"*, *"the Son"*, *"the Holy Spirit"*: a parody that turns a sacrament of life into an injury, and shows a child mimicking priestly authority. The school's notes on irony add that the narrator comes to confess his hatred but must absorb the priest's own story instead, and ends with *"One Hail Mary"* and a thirty-day fast as a penance. The Hail Mary and fasting are associated with forgiveness in Catholic tradition, but they cannot lift the burden, so the closing penance reads as inadequate and ironic.

### Mythical: Greek and Roman

#### Prometheus

- **The story.** The Titan Prometheus, whose name means "forethought", steals fire from the gods (hidden in a fennel stalk) and gives it to humans. Zeus punishes him by chaining him to a rock where an eagle eats his liver each day and it grows back each night. In Hesiod's version, Zeus also punishes humanity by sending Pandora. Heracles eventually frees him. In some versions Prometheus shapes humans from clay.
- **Key images and phrases.** Stolen fire; a chained figure; an eagle and a liver; fire as knowledge, technology, or life; *the modern Prometheus* (the subtitle of *Frankenstein*).
- **What writers use it for.** Defiant benefaction; the price of knowledge or progress; creators who overreach; rebellion against authority.
- **How to spot it.** Fire given or stolen; a rock; a creator whose creation rebels; punishment for helping others.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in Paper 1 sources. In *Othello* V.ii, Othello says *"I know not where is that Promethean heat / That can thy light relume"*: he knows that he cannot restore Desdemona's life once he has put out her light.

#### Pandora

- **The story.** In Hesiod, Zeus punishes humanity for Prometheus's theft of fire by sending the first woman, Pandora, made by the gods and given to Epimetheus. She brings a sealed jar (*pithos*; the "box" is a later mistranslation). Despite the warning not to open it, she lifts the lid and *evils* (sickness, toil, death) fly out. Only *hope* remains inside the jar.
- **Key images and phrases.** A jar or box; a lid; a warning not to look; troubles flying out; hope left inside; *a can of worms* in modern idiom; *Pandora's box*.
- **What writers use it for.** Curiosity and its consequences; blame placed on women; the origin of suffering; ambiguous hope (is hope a blessing or the last evil?); a quarrel in which the unsaid is let loose.
- **How to spot it.** A closed container, a prohibition, something released, a woman blamed, hope mentioned at the end.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2024 poem, "Ajar" by A. E. Stallings: Pandora is named outright, *"Pandora was a bride; the gods gave her a jar"*. A marital quarrel is retold through the myth, beginning with the broken washing-machine door. The key divergence is the last line: *"the woes were words, and the only thing left was quiet"*, so what stays in the jar is silence, not hope. 2025 poem, "Ice Palace": the *"one forbidden door"* is Bluebeard first, but one of the course's own notes describes the poem as a Bluebeard and Pandora fusion, since both stories turn on a forbidden opening.

#### Icarus

- **The story.** Daedalus, the inventor, builds wings of feathers and wax so that he and his son Icarus can escape from Crete. He warns Icarus not to fly too low (the sea wets the feathers) or too high (the sun melts the wax). Icarus, thrilled by flight, soars too high; the wax melts and he falls into the sea, which is named after him. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* VIII.)
- **Key images and phrases.** Wings, wax, the sun, feathers, a boy falling into the sea, a father looking up, a warning ignored. Auden's "Musée des Beaux Arts" and Brueghel's painting show the fall going unnoticed.
- **What writers use it for.** Hubris and ambition; a fall after a rise; youthful recklessness; a parent's failure to protect; an unnoticed death.
- **How to spot it.** Flight, a sun that destroys, wax or melting, a falling figure, a father-and-son pair.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources, though it is on the school's signal list.

#### Narcissus and Echo

- **The story.** Narcissus, a beautiful youth, scorns everyone who loves him, including the nymph Echo, who has been cursed to repeat only the last words spoken to her. He sees his reflection in a pool, falls in love with it, cannot leave, and wastes away; a flower grows where he dies. Echo fades until only her voice is left. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* III.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A pool, a reflection, a mirror, a voice that repeats, a flower, self-love, wasting away.
- **What writers use it for.** Vanity and self-absorption; the trap of the image; love that cannot reach another person; a voice that has no words of its own.
- **How to spot it.** Mirrors and reflections, a character who stares at themselves, echoes, a pale flower.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Orpheus and Eurydice

- **The story.** Orpheus, the finest musician, loses his bride Eurydice to a snakebite. He descends to the Underworld and his music so moves Hades and Persephone that they let Eurydice return, on one condition: he must not look back until they reach the upper world. At the threshold he looks back, and she is lost for ever. (Virgil, *Georgics* IV; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* X.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A lyre; a descent; a snakebite; a backward glance; a shade slipping away; *"do not look back"*.
- **What writers use it for.** Grief; love's limits; the power and failure of art against death; the doubt that costs everything.
- **How to spot it.** A descent to a dark place to rescue someone; a prohibition on looking; music that moves the dead.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources. The school lists *"a backward glance that loses her"* as a signal.

#### Sisyphus

- **The story.** Sisyphus, a king of Corinth, cheats death twice and is punished in Tartarus by having to roll a huge boulder up a hill. Each time he nears the top it rolls back, so he must begin again for ever. Albert Camus uses him in *The Myth of Sisyphus*, ending *"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."*
- **Key images and phrases.** A boulder; a hill; repeated effort; a task never completed; *a Sisyphean task*.
- **What writers use it for.** Futile, repeated labour; the absurdity of routine; defiant endurance.
- **How to spot it.** Endless, pointless tasks; pushing a heavy object uphill; labour whose results vanish.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Tantalus

- **The story.** Tantalus, a king who offended the gods, is punished in the Underworld by standing in water up to his chin beneath fruit-laden branches. When he bends to drink, the water drains away; when he reaches for fruit, the wind lifts it beyond his hand. His name gives the word *tantalise*.
- **Key images and phrases.** Water just out of reach; fruit that retreats; thirst and hunger; *tantalising*.
- **What writers use it for.** Desire structurally frustrated; temptation visible but unreachable; punishment through longing.
- **How to spot it.** The word *tantalise* or *tantalising*; fruit or water just beyond a character's grasp.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None named. In the 2024 *eden* extract, Alum finds it *"tantalizing to glimpse and whiff forbidden land"*; treat this as a hedged echo (the word and the situation of forbidden fruit) rather than a main allusion.

#### Achilles

- **The story.** Achilles is the greatest Greek warrior of the Trojan War, son of the sea-nymph Thetis. In later tradition she dips him in the river Styx to make him invulnerable, holding him by the heel. The *Iliad* is the story of his rage after Agamemnon takes his prize; his friend Patroclus dies in his place, he kills Hector and drags his body round the walls, and he is killed by an arrow in the heel. He chose a short, glorious life over a long, forgotten one.
- **Key images and phrases.** A heel; the river Styx; rage; a warrior sulking in his tent; Hector's body dragged behind a chariot; *Achilles' heel* (a fatal weakness).
- **What writers use it for.** A hidden vulnerability in a strong person; wrath; honour and fame against survival; grief for a friend.
- **How to spot it.** A single weak point, an arrow, a heel, an angry hero withdrawing from battle.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Odysseus and the Sirens

- **The story.** In Homer's *Odyssey*, the hero Odysseus takes ten years to get home from Troy to his wife Penelope. Warned by Circe that the Sirens' song lures sailors to their deaths on the rocks, he fills his crew's ears with wax and has himself tied to the mast so that he can hear it but not steer to it. He survives; he also passes Scylla and Charybdis, blinds the Cyclops, and returns disguised to kill Penelope's suitors.
- **Key images and phrases.** A ship; a mast; wax in ears; singing women on rocks; a seductive voice; *a siren call*; a long voyage; a waiting wife.
- **What writers use it for.** Temptation by a beautiful voice or promise; self-restraint; delayed homecoming; cunning; the long way home.
- **How to spot it.** A voice or song that draws characters towards danger; being tied or bound in order to resist; a long journey home.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources, though the *Odyssey* and the Sirens appear on the school's signal list.

#### Medusa

- **The story.** Medusa is one of the three Gorgons, a monster with snakes for hair whose gaze turns anyone who meets it to stone. Perseus, helped by Athena and Hermes, looks only at her reflection in his bronze shield, cuts off her head and carries it in a bag; Pegasus springs from her blood. In Ovid she was originally a beautiful woman who was assaulted by Poseidon in Athena's temple and then punished by Athena with snakes, which is the reading many modern writers (such as Carol Ann Duffy) take up.
- **Key images and phrases.** Snake hair; a stony gaze; a severed head; a mirrored shield; petrification.
- **What writers use it for.** A woman made monstrous; deadly looking; paralysis through fear; female anger; the gaze (who looks, and who is turned to stone).
- **How to spot it.** Snakes, stone, a lethal stare, a reflected image used to face a danger.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Midas

- **The story.** King Midas receives a wish from the god Dionysus and asks that everything he touches turn to gold. Food, drink and, in Hawthorne's retelling, his own daughter turn to gold; he begs to be released, and washes in the river Pactolus. (Midas also has donkey's ears in a separate myth.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A golden touch; food turned to metal; a greedy king; a washing in a river; *the Midas touch*.
- **What writers use it for.** Greed and its cost; a wish that destroys; the emptiness of wealth.
- **How to spot it.** Gold or gilding on everything; a desire granted with a terrible catch.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Persephone

- **The story.** Persephone (Proserpina to the Romans), daughter of Demeter, goddess of the harvest, is picking flowers when Hades, king of the Underworld, seizes her and carries her below. Demeter's grief makes the earth barren. Zeus orders her return, but because she has eaten pomegranate seeds in the Underworld she must spend part of each year there; her return each spring is the arrival of spring.
- **Key images and phrases.** Flowers picked in a meadow; a pomegranate; seeds; a mother searching; winter and spring; a girl taken underground.
- **What writers use it for.** Loss of innocence; the seasons; a mother's grief and a daughter's abduction; a life split between two worlds.
- **How to spot it.** Pomegranate imagery, a girl lost underground, a barren season, a return in spring.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Cupid and Psyche

- **The story.** In Apuleius's *The Golden Ass*, Psyche is so beautiful that Venus is jealous and sends her son Cupid to punish her; he falls in love with her instead. He takes her to a palace where invisible servants wait on her, and visits her only at night in the dark on condition that she never looks at him. Her jealous sisters persuade her to light a lamp and look; a drop of hot oil falls on him, he wakes and flees. Psyche undertakes Venus's impossible tasks and, last, carries a box from the Underworld that she is told not to open; she opens it from curiosity and falls into sleep. Cupid wakes her and she becomes immortal.
- **Key images and phrases.** A lamp and a drop of oil; an unseen lover; invisible servants; a palace; a forbidden look; a box not to be opened; tasks and trials.
- **What writers use it for.** Love that depends on not looking; curiosity as both a fault and a path to knowledge; trust; the soul (*psyche*) tested.
- **How to spot it.** Invisible attendants, a lover who must not be seen, a lamp that exposes.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None named. The *"Invisible hands"* and the prohibition on looking in "Ice Palace" fit this story, but the school's reading prefers Bluebeard and Beauty and the Beast as the main parallels, so use Cupid and Psyche only as a supporting clause.

#### The Trojan Horse

- **The story.** After ten years of siege the Greeks pretend to sail away, leaving a huge wooden horse. The Trojans, persuaded by the lying Sinon (and despite warnings from Laocoön, who says *"I fear the Greeks, even when they bring gifts"*, and from Cassandra), drag it into the city. That night Greek soldiers climb out and open the gates, and Troy burns. (Virgil, *Aeneid* II.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A wooden horse; a gift; soldiers hidden inside; gates opened at night; a ten-year siege; *a Trojan horse* in computing.
- **What writers use it for.** Deception; a gift that hides an attack; an enemy within; the ruin of a proud city by its own choice.
- **How to spot it.** A gift or offering that carries danger; something hollow with something hidden inside; a siege.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Oedipus

- **The story.** Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*. A prophecy says that Oedipus will kill his father and marry his mother. Abandoned as a baby and raised in Corinth, he flees what he thinks are his parents, kills a stranger at a crossroads (his real father, King Laius), solves the Sphinx's riddle and marries the widowed queen, Jocasta. When a plague strikes Thebes, he vows to find the killer of Laius, and learns that it is himself. Jocasta hangs herself and he blinds himself with her brooch pins.
- **Key images and phrases.** A crossroads; a riddle; a plague; blindness and sight; a prophecy that cannot be avoided; the swollen foot; the blind prophet Tiresias.
- **What writers use it for.** Fate against free will; unwitting guilt; the detective who finds himself guilty; sight and blindness as knowledge; a family secret.
- **How to spot it.** A man who investigates a crime that proves to be his own; blinding; irony around seeing and not seeing.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Cassandra

- **The story.** Cassandra, a princess of Troy, is given the gift of prophecy by Apollo. When she refuses his advances, he curses her so that no one will believe her. She warns of the wooden horse and of Troy's fall, and is ignored. She is taken as a prize by Agamemnon and killed with him on their return (Aeschylus, *Agamemnon*).
- **Key images and phrases.** A woman who foresees and is dismissed; a warning as the city burns; truth without credibility; *a Cassandra*.
- **What writers use it for.** The ignored truth-teller, often a woman; warnings about disaster that are not heeded; helplessness of foreknowledge.
- **How to spot it.** A prophet or whistle-blower whom nobody trusts; a disaster predicted and then unfolding.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### Hades and the Underworld

- **The story.** Hades is both the god of the dead and the name of his realm. The dead cross the river Styx (or Acheron) in the ferryman Charon's boat, paying with a coin placed in the mouth; Cerberus, a three-headed dog, guards the gate; the river Lethe brings forgetfulness. The good go to the Elysian Fields and the wicked to Tartarus. Heroes who make the descent (a *katabasis*) include Orpheus, Odysseus, Heracles and Aeneas; Persephone is its queen.
- **Key images and phrases.** A river, a ferryman, a coin, a three-headed dog, shades or ghosts, forgetfulness, a descent into darkness, asphodel.
- **What writers use it for.** Death; descent into the unconscious or into a personal hell; a crossing that cannot be undone; a journey to find a lost person.
- **How to spot it.** A boat across dark water, a guard dog, a staircase down, the dead speaking, forgetfulness.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None named, though Orpheus and Persephone, which the school does use as signals, are both parts of this story.

#### Theseus, the Minotaur and Ariadne

- **The story.** King Minos of Crete keeps a monster, the Minotaur (half man, half bull), in a labyrinth built by Daedalus, and demands young Athenians as tribute. Theseus volunteers to kill it. Ariadne, Minos's daughter, falls in love with him and gives him a ball of thread to unwind so that he can find his way out. He kills the Minotaur and escapes, but abandons Ariadne on Naxos.
- **Key images and phrases.** A maze; a thread; a monster at the centre; a bull; a hero abandoning the woman who helped him.
- **What writers use it for.** Being lost; the monster in the centre of a self or institution; guidance and betrayal; a clue followed back.
- **How to spot it.** Labyrinthine corridors, a thread, a horned beast in the middle, a journey that ends in the dark.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources. The school's advice is that a maze image alone is better analysed as frustrating enclosure than forced into this myth.

#### Pygmalion

- **The story.** Ovid's Pygmalion, a sculptor who despises real women, carves an ivory statue so beautiful that he falls in love with it. At the festival of Venus he prays for a wife like his statue; when he kisses it, it comes to life, and he marries her. (Bernard Shaw's *Pygmalion* reworks it as a story of a man remaking a woman.)
- **Key images and phrases.** A sculptor; a statue that warms into flesh; a creator who loves his creation; remaking a person.
- **What writers use it for.** The artist's control over what he makes; idealised, possessive love; a man reshaping a woman; art that turns real.
- **How to spot it.** A statue, doll or portrait that lives or is treated as living; one person reshaping another.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The Fates

- **The story.** The three Fates (the Moirai in Greek, the Parcae in Latin) are Clotho who spins the thread of life, Lachesis who measures it, and Atropos who cuts it. Even the gods cannot overrule them.
- **Key images and phrases.** Thread, spindle, shears, three old women spinning, a length of life.
- **What writers use it for.** Determinism; a life measured out; the inevitability of death.
- **How to spot it.** Three women with thread or weaving; a cut thread; the words *fate* or *destiny*.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

#### The phoenix

- **The story.** A legendary bird that lives for centuries, builds a nest of spices, burns itself, and is reborn from its own ashes. (A tradition from classical and Egyptian sources, taken over by early Christian writers as a symbol of resurrection.)
- **Key images and phrases.** Flame, ashes, a bird rising, rebirth, "rising from the ashes".
- **What writers use it for.** Renewal after destruction; cyclical rebirth; recovery of a city or person.
- **How to spot it.** A bird in fire; ashes producing new life.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** None in the sources.

### Fairy tales the school counts

#### Bluebeard

- **The story.** Charles Perrault's *Barbe bleue* (1697). A wealthy man with a blue beard, who has had several wives who all disappeared, marries a young woman. Before leaving on a journey he gives her the keys to every room, but forbids her to use *one small key* to a particular room. Curious, she opens it and finds the floor covered in blood and the bodies of his former wives. The key is stained with blood that cannot be washed off, so Bluebeard discovers her disobedience and prepares to kill her; her sister Anne watches from the tower (*"Sister Anne, do you see anyone coming?"*) until her brothers arrive and kill him.
- **Key images and phrases.** A key that will not come clean; a forbidden room (the last, the smallest); blood on the floor; hidden bodies; a husband's wealth; a wife's curiosity.
- **What writers use it for.** Curiosity punished; secret violence behind a rich or respectable surface; a woman trapped in a house; the gothic.
- **How to spot it.** One locked or forbidden door in a house of open ones, a prohibition addressed to a bride or guest, blood where it should be clean, bodies behind the door.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2025 poem, "Ice Palace" by Margaret Atwood. The signals are *"the one forbidden door"*, *"the last and smallest room"*, *"Red petals on the floor. / You hope they're petals"*, *"carnage"*, and the repeated *"Do not look"*. The **divergence** is what earns marks: the room is now marked *"Staff Only"*, so the hidden violence belongs to the economy that runs the hotel and not to one murderous husband, while the speaker, addressing the reader as *"dearest"*, takes the husband's part. The reader becomes the curious wife and the desire to know is the desire forbidden.

#### Beauty and the Beast

- **The story.** In Gabrielle-Suzanne de Villeneuve's tale (1740), shortened by Jeanne-Marie Leprince de Beaumont (1756), a merchant picks a rose in the Beast's garden for his daughter Beauty; the Beast demands his life or that of the daughter. Beauty goes to the Beast's enchanted palace, where unseen servants wait on her and he asks her every night to marry him. She goes home for a visit, overstays, and returns to find him dying; her love breaks the spell and he becomes a prince.
- **Key images and phrases.** A beast who longs to be loved; a rose; an enchanted palace; invisible servants (*"Invisible hands"*); a mirror; luxury and captivity; the kiss that transforms.
- **What writers use it for.** Love as transformation; captivity disguised as luxury; judging by appearances; a fearful being who proves tender.
- **How to spot it.** A grand house run by unseen hands; a feared master who longs for affection; a rose.
- **Past Pre-Q texts.** 2025 poem, "Ice Palace": the *"fearful beast who runs the show / and longs for kisses"* and the *"Invisible hands"* that bring food and turn on lights. The school's reading uses this as a supporting clause for the Bluebeard reading, not a separate paragraph.

## Using this page in the exam

- **Match on two signals, not one.** A single apple or mirror is only an image. Several linked details (a wall, a forbidden thing, fruit, a tempter) point to a source.
- **Name the story precisely**: the book and chapter if you know it (*Luke 7*, *Genesis 4*), the author of a myth (Ovid, Homer), and say what the original does in one clause.
- **Map before you analyse.** State which figure corresponds to which character, and which object to which object.
- **Aim for the divergence.** The highest marks go to what the writer changes: a Pandora whose jar holds silence, a Gospel scene with the roles reversed, a Bluebeard whose room is a staff door, a Cain who is also a victim.
- **Hedge when thin.** If there is only one signal, say the image "invites a reading through" the story and then analyse the image itself.
- **Keep it short.** The allusion paragraph is one of four or five body paragraphs; the school's exercise asks for about 100 words on it. Cover the source, the parallel, the divergence and the effect, with two or three quoted fragments.

For the technique side of the paragraph and the worked 2025 models, see [the unfamiliar texts notes](../notes/unfamiliar-texts.md). A small deck of allusion flashcards is in [the allusions deck](../anki/allusions.txt).
