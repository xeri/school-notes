# Film analysis

One hour, 25 marks, one essay on the film studied in class: ***Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy*** **(Tomas Alfredson, 2011)**. The 2026 details give a choice of four questions; every Paper 4 from 2021 to 2025 printed five. The questions are **not text-specific**. They are written so that any film studied anywhere in the year group can answer them. That single fact determines how you prepare: you memorise **sequences and techniques**, not essays, and you re-angle them onto whichever question appears.

The paper asks for "an appreciation for a range of filmic conventions used by the director to create effect and meaning". The literature-essay criteria apply, with criterion 3 covering **filmic** conventions: at 5 marks, extensive and relevant examples perceptively identified and analysed in detail, with sensitive appreciation of effect. Naming a technique with little analysis sits in the 1–2 band; explaining how it generates meaning earns 4 or 5.

## Shot types and framing

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme long shot** | Figure tiny or absent within a vast field | Subordinates the human to place, scale or system |
| **Long shot / wide shot** | Whole body visible with surrounding space | Situates a character in an environment; keeps the viewer at a distance |
| **Medium shot** | Waist up | The neutral, conversational register of film; a baseline the director departs from |
| **Close-up** | Face or object fills the frame | Forces intimacy or scrutiny; denies the viewer context |
| **Extreme close-up** | Part of a face, or a detail | Fragments the body; converts a person into a surface |
| **Two-shot** | Two figures in one frame | Whether they are in the same frame or cut apart is an argument about their relationship |
| **Over-the-shoulder shot** | Framed past one character onto another | Aligns the viewer with the listener |
| **Point-of-view shot** | The camera takes a character's eyeline | Collapses viewer and character; the strongest available alignment device |
| **Headroom and lead room** | Space above or in front of the subject | Cramped framing confines; excess space isolates |
| **Frame within a frame** | Subject shot through a doorway, window, mirror, blinds | Surveillance, entrapment, or a relationship mediated by something |
| **Deep space staging** | Significant action placed far back in the frame | Makes the viewer search the image rather than be directed to it |
| **Rule of thirds / centred composition** | Where the subject sits in the frame | Dead-centre symmetry reads as formal, institutional, or unnatural stillness |

## Camera movement and angle

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Pan / tilt** | Camera pivots horizontally or vertically from a fixed point | Reveals, or withholds by moving away |
| **Tracking / dolly shot** | Camera travels with or alongside the action | Attaches the viewer to a moving subject |
| **Steadicam / handheld** | Smooth mobile following, or deliberate instability | Immersion, or disorientation and documentary urgency |
| **Crane / aerial shot** | Camera rises or descends | Detachment; an authorial or god's-eye perspective |
| **Zoom** | Focal length changes, not the camera position | Flattens space; feels mechanical and observational rather than human |
| **Static / locked-off camera** | The camera refuses to move | Makes the viewer scan the frame unaided; often used for scrutiny or helplessness |
| **High angle** | Looking down on the subject | Diminishes, exposes, places the subject under something |
| **Low angle** | Looking up at the subject | Confers stature or threat |
| **Dutch angle** | The horizon tilted | Instability, wrongness, psychological disturbance |
| **Eye level** | Neutral height | Parity between viewer and subject — meaningful only when the film has established something else |

## Focus and depth of field

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Shallow focus** | One narrow plane sharp, the rest soft | Isolates a figure; makes the surroundings irrelevant or unknowable |
| **Deep focus** | Foreground and background both sharp | Keeps relationships and context simultaneously legible; lets the viewer choose |
| **Rack focus** | Focus shifts within a shot | Redirects attention without a cut, which implies a connection between the two planes |
| **Soft focus** | The whole image diffused | Memory, idealisation, unreliability |
| **Long lens / telephoto** | Compresses depth, flattens planes | Makes the camera feel distant and observational — the look of surveillance |
| **Wide-angle lens** | Exaggerates depth and nearness; distorts at the edges | Immersion, or caricature when used on a face |

## Lighting

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-point lighting** | Key, fill and back light | The invisible default; its absence is what you analyse |
| **High key** | Bright, even, low-contrast, few shadows | Clarity, safety, comedy, or an artificial cleanliness |
| **Low key** | Predominantly dark, high contrast, deep shadow | Threat, concealment, moral ambiguity |
| **Chiaroscuro** | Extreme contrast of light and dark within one image | Divides a face or a world into opposed halves |
| **Motivated lighting** | Light that appears to come from a source within the scene — a window, a lamp, a fire | Makes the world feel inhabited rather than lit; the source itself becomes meaningful |
| **Practical** | A lamp visible in the shot that actually lights it | Ties mood to a mundane object a character can switch off |
| **Backlighting / silhouette** | Light behind the subject | Removes identity; converts a person into a shape |
| **Underlighting** | Light from below | Unnatural, distorting, traditionally sinister |
| **Natural / available light** | No added sources | Documentary authenticity; often flat and unflattering by design |

## Colour

- **Palette** — the recurring range of hues. Name it in three adjectives and tie it to meaning: a desaturated palette drains glamour from a world; a restricted palette makes a single intrusion of colour an event.
- **Grading** — colour adjusted after shooting. The relevant question is what it suppresses: warmth, contrast, variety.
- **Motivated colour** — a colour attached to a character, place or period, which then carries meaning when it reappears elsewhere.
- **Colour temperature** — warm amber against cool blue-green, often used to separate two timelines, two spaces, or memory from the present.
- **Monochrome insert** — a sequence stripped of colour inside a colour film, marking a different order of reality.

## Mise-en-scène

Everything arranged in front of the camera.

| Element | What to analyse |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Whether a location constrains or liberates. Repeated returns to one space make it an argument. |
| **Set design and architecture** | Corridors, thresholds, glass, ceiling height, clutter or emptiness. Architecture is the most reliable visual statement of how power is organised. |
| **Props** | An object that recurs accumulates meaning. Note who touches it, who is denied it, and what it is at the end. |
| **Costume** | Uniformity against individuation. The one character dressed differently is always being marked. |
| **Make-up and physical transformation** | Ageing, injury, concealment; whether the film tracks a change in the body. |
| **Blocking** | Where actors are placed and how they move. Who stands, who sits, who is behind whom, who leaves the frame first. |
| **Staging and depth** | Whether the significant action is given to the foreground or hidden at the back. |
| **Performance** | Stillness against gesture; micro-expression; what an actor does with the hands and the eyes while someone else speaks. |

## Editing

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut** | A direct join between shots | The basic unit; the question is always what it joins and what it omits |
| **Continuity editing** | Cutting designed to be invisible and spatially coherent | Naturalises the world; departures from it register as disturbance |
| **180-degree rule** | The camera stays on one side of an imaginary line between subjects | Keeps screen direction consistent; crossing the line disorients deliberately |
| **Shot / reverse shot** | Alternating views of two people in conversation | The standard grammar of dialogue. A **withheld reverse shot** denies the answering view and is always meaningful |
| **Eyeline match** | A character looks, then we see what they see | Grants the character's perspective authority |
| **Match cut** | A cut joining two shots by visual, aural or graphic similarity | Asserts an equivalence between two things the plot keeps separate |
| **Cross-cutting / parallel editing** | Alternating between two simultaneous actions | Builds tension and forces comparison between the two strands |
| **Jump cut** | A cut within a continuous action, breaking time | Disruption, compulsion, a mind that cannot proceed smoothly |
| **Elliptical editing** | Part of an action is omitted | Makes the viewer infer; denies the satisfaction of witnessing |
| **Montage** | A rapid or lyrical sequence compressing time, process or theme | Argument by accumulation rather than by scene |
| **Cross-dissolve / fade** | Gradual transitions | Passage of time; a softening that a cut would deny |
| **Pace and shot length** | Average shot length across a sequence | Long takes make the viewer wait; fast cutting removes the time to judge |
| **Long take** | A single unbroken shot of unusual length | Refuses to intervene; the duration itself becomes the content |

## Sound

| Term | Definition | What it typically does |
|---|---|---|
| **Diegetic sound** | Sound originating inside the world of the film, audible to the characters | Grounds the scene; foregrounding it in place of music makes a world feel observed rather than dramatised |
| **Non-diegetic sound** | Sound the characters cannot hear — score, voiceover, added effects | Directs the viewer's feeling; the most interventionist tool a director has |
| **Score** | Composed music | Analyse instrumentation and whether motifs resolve or circle |
| **Leitmotif** | A musical phrase attached to a character or idea | Its return makes a connection the dialogue does not state |
| **Source music** | Music playing within the scene — radio, band, record | Can be ironic against the image, which is the commonest use |
| **Silence** | The deliberate absence of sound or score | Only works in a film that has established a soundscape to remove |
| **Sound bridge** | Sound from the next scene begins under the last, or carries over | Stitches timelines; implies causation across a cut |
| **Voiceover** | Narration over the image | Raises the question of who is speaking, when, and whether they can be trusted |
| **Asynchronous sound** | Sound and image deliberately mismatched | Dislocation; the gap between what is seen and what is known |
| **Ambient sound / room tone** | The background noise of a space | Makes a location specific; its removal makes a space abstract |
| **Dialogue and volume** | Overlapping speech, muttering, shouting | Who is audible is a question about who has power |

## Narrative structure and point of view

| Term | What to analyse |
|---|---|
| **Linear / non-linear structure** | If the film reorders events, ask what the reordering withholds and when it chooses to supply it |
| **Flashback and flashforward** | What triggers the return, and whether the same memory is shown more than once with more revealed each time |
| **Framing narrative** | A story told inside another story — who is telling it, and to whom |
| **Restricted narration** | The viewer knows only what one character knows. Generates identification and suspense |
| **Omniscient narration** | The viewer knows more than the characters. Generates dramatic irony |
| **Unreliable narration** | The film gives information it later contradicts |
| **In medias res** | Opening mid-action, withholding orientation |
| **Exposition** | How necessary information is delivered, and how much is refused |
| **Climax and resolution** | Whether the film resolves, or deliberately declines to |
| **Protagonist and antagonist** | An **absent antagonist** who is never shown is a structural choice with thematic consequences |

## From technique to meaning

The sentence shape that earns the marks:

> **name the technique → describe precisely what it does in the image or on the soundtrack → "and the effect on the viewer is to…" → connect that effect to the question**

Weak: *"The director uses a close-up to create tension."*
Better: *"The director holds a close-up on the listener rather than the speaker, so that the viewer receives the news through a reaction and is denied the speaker's face, and the effect is to make the scene about the cost of information rather than its content."*

Three rules:

1. **Say what the technique withholds, not only what it shows.** Film means by exclusion — what is outside the frame, cut away from, left unheard.
2. **Never attribute an effect the technique cannot produce.** A high angle does not reveal a character's childhood. Keep the claim proportionate to the device.
3. **Separate observation from interpretation.** State what is observable, then offer the reading, then — where the evidence allows — name a competing reading and say why yours is better supported. That is what sensitivity to effect means on the mark schedule.

## Building a paragraph about a sequence

| Move | What it contains |
|---|---|
| **Point** | A claim about the film that answers the question and can be argued with |
| **Context** | One sentence locating the sequence in the narrative. Not a plot summary |
| **Evidence** | Two or three precise observations from *different* categories — say, framing, cut, and sound |
| **Analysis** | The mechanism of each, and its effect on the viewer. This is the majority of the paragraph |
| **Explanation** | Why that effect supports the point you opened with |
| **Link** | How the paragraph advances the thesis and answers the question |

A hypothetical illustration of what "evidence from different categories" means in practice: *suppose* a film stages an argument between two colleagues by holding them in one static wide shot, cutting away only once — to a third person watching through a glass partition — and letting a typewriter continue under the dialogue. Three observations are now available from three categories: the refusal to cut into shot/reverse shot keeps the two antagonists in the same frame and denies the viewer a side to take; the single cut to the observer converts a private quarrel into surveillance; the unbroken diegetic clatter insists that the institution continues working through it. Those three can be argued into one claim. Three observations from one category cannot.

Note that the illustration above is invented to show the method. **Your evidence must come from the film you actually studied, described accurately.** Never report a shot you have not seen.

## The studied film: *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy*

| | |
|---|---|
| Title | *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy*, italicised (underlined in handwriting) |
| Director | Tomas Alfredson, a Swedish director; his previous film was *Let the Right One In* (2008) |
| Year | 2011, British |
| Source | John le Carré's 1974 novel; screenplay by Bridget O'Connor and Peter Straughan |
| Camera and music | Cinematography by Hoyte van Hoytema; score by Alberto Iglesias |
| Setting | The Cold War, 1973: London, with Budapest and Istanbul |
| Premise | George Smiley (Gary Oldman), forced into retirement from "the Circus" (British intelligence, MI6), is recalled in secret to find a Soviet mole at the top of the service |

**The suspects.** Control (John Hurt), the dying head of the Circus, gives the five senior men code names from the nursery rhyme and tapes their photographs to chess pieces. **Tinker** is Percy Alleline (Toby Jones). **Tailor** is Bill Haydon (Colin Firth), who is the mole. **Soldier** is Roy Bland (Ciarán Hinds), **Poorman** is Toby Esterhase (David Dencik), and **Beggarman** is Smiley himself.

**The others.** Peter Guillam (Benedict Cumberbatch) is Smiley's man inside the Circus. Jim Prideaux (Mark Strong) is the agent shot in Budapest, later a teacher at a boys' school and Haydon's closest friend. Ricki Tarr (Tom Hardy) is the field agent whose story starts the hunt. Connie Sachs (Kathy Burke) is the sacked researcher who remembers everything. Karla, the Soviet spymaster, is never seen. Smiley's wife Ann is barely seen.

**Two contexts.** The film is *set* in 1973, a declining Britain in the Cold War, and draws on the real case of Kim Philby, the charming establishment insider at MI6 who was exposed as a Soviet agent and defected in 1963. Le Carré had worked for British intelligence himself. It was *made* in 2011. Say which context you mean before you argue from it.

### Sequences to know

Learn each one as narrative context, three observable details from different categories, and what they mean. Describe only what you have seen on screen; if your memory of a detail is shaky, rewatch it or leave it out.

| Sequence | What happens | Techniques to analyse | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Budapest (the opening)** | Control sends Prideaux to meet a Hungarian contact who can name the mole. In a café the waiter is sweating and Prideaux glances up at a window. He realises it is a trap, walks away, and is shot | Slow, near-wordless build; the camera adopts Prideaux's watching eyes; small physical details (sweat, a face at a window) carry the information; sudden violence breaking the stillness | Surveillance is mutual and the watcher is watched. Information arrives as detail, not speech. The failure ends Control's and Smiley's careers |
| **Smiley in retirement** | Smiley is pushed out with Control. He barely speaks in the opening stretch of the film, swims alone in the open-air ponds on Hampstead Heath, and changes his glasses, which marks past from present | Performance of stillness; costume and props (the spectacles); static framing that makes the viewer watch him | The anti-Bond hero: patience, watching and listening are his weapons. The glasses tie the film's theme of seeing clearly to its non-linear structure |
| **The Circus** | Meetings of the top men in a windowless, soundproofed room padded in orange; Control's chess pieces with faces taped on | Production design as argument; the muted palette of browns, greys and mustard everywhere else; frames within frames of glass, doors and corridors | An institution sealed off so it can hear only itself, and so cannot detect the enemy inside it. Men reduced to pieces in a game |
| **Ricki Tarr's story** | Tarr is waiting in Smiley's house at night. In flashback he tells of Istanbul, where he fell in love with Irina, a Soviet agent who wanted to defect and knew of the mole | Low-key lighting that hides Tarr's face at first (the school's model paragraph); a mid-shot of Smiley sensing his visitor; flashback as a story within a story; the colloquial *"your lot"* and *"their lot"* | Tarr is the one character who acts on love, and love is punished. Both sides want him dead, so the audience's sympathy is engaged before his story begins |
| **Guillam steals the file** | Guillam takes the logbook for the night of Tarr's signal from the Circus archive, under the eyes of his colleagues. He then ends his relationship with his partner to protect himself | Suspense built through duration and near-silence; ambient sound; elliptical editing in the break-up, where the conversation itself is not shown | The secret world charges its costs to private life. Guillam must lie to the one person he does not want to lie to, which suits a *supporting character* or *relationship* question |
| **Smiley's Karla monologue** | Late at night Smiley tells Guillam about meeting Karla in Delhi in 1955. Karla said nothing and kept Smiley's lighter, a gift inscribed from Ann. Smiley speaks partly to an empty chair | A long, unbroken take; the empty chair standing in for the absent antagonist; no score | The film's great antagonist is an absence. Smiley's account of a fanatic hiding a secret doubt reads as a portrait of himself, and Ann is shown to be his weak point |
| **The Christmas party** | A recurring flashback to the Circus party. Father Christmas in a Lenin mask leads the room in the Soviet anthem; Smiley sees Ann in an embrace with another man. Its last return shows a look between Haydon and Prideaux | Fragmented, non-linear flashback, revealing more each time; warmer light for memory than for the present; diegetic singing as irony | The enemy's anthem sung with delight inside British intelligence: loyalties are not where the institution assumes. The betrayals are visible to anyone who looks |
| **The owl** | At the boys' school, an owl comes down the chimney and panics in the classroom; Prideaux kills it with his bare hands | Sudden violence puncturing a calm scene; the boys' shocked reaction shots | A trapped creature killed by a trained man's reflexes. It foreshadows Prideaux's killing of Haydon |
| **The trap and the ending** | Smiley forces the mole to a meeting at a safe house and waits in the dark; Haydon walks in. Haydon claims his choice was aesthetic as much as moral, because the West had grown ugly. Prideaux later kills him with a rifle. The film closes on a montage over Julio Iglesias's *"La Mer"*, and Smiley takes the head chair in the Circus room | Waiting in darkness, with sound doing the work; the closing montage; an ironic non-diegetic song, warm and romantic over loneliness and death; circular structure | Betrayal is personal before it is political. Prideaux's shot is grief, not duty. Smiley inherits Control's place, and the game goes on |

### Matching the archetypes to the film

| Question type | Strongest material |
|---|---|
| Conflict | The Cold War outside against betrayal inside the Circus; Smiley against the absent Karla; Prideaux and Haydon |
| Supporting character | Ricki Tarr (the catalyst), Peter Guillam (the cost), Connie Sachs (memory), Jim Prideaux |
| Relationship | Haydon and Prideaux; Smiley and Ann through Haydon; Tarr and Irina |
| Sound | Near-silence and ambient sound; the Soviet anthem at the party; *"La Mer"* at the end; Iglesias's sparse score |

| Setting and place | The Circus and its orange room; the Budapest café; Istanbul against grey London |
| Context | Set in 1973 against Philby and British decline; made in 2011 |
| Antihero, agency, active or passive | Smiley the passive watcher who wins; Haydon the charming traitor |
| Allusion | The nursery rhyme behind the code names |

## Answering a non-text-specific question

The questions recur in a small number of shapes. Each one opens with a quoted claim or a general statement about film, then asks you to *discuss*, *analyse* or *comment on the extent to which* it applies "with reference to the film you have studied".

| Year | The five questions |
|---|---|
| 2021 | Universal and timeless, or specific to a time and place · a transgression of boundaries · characters hold interest only when they transform · cinema challenges conventional values · visual symbols |
| 2022 | A subjective perspective · characters as representatives of a group · the search for a sense of purpose · freedom and/or constraint · place as almost another character |
| 2023 | The antihero · allusion · power and persuasion in a relationship · material wealth as motivation · sources of conflict |
| 2024 | Symbolism · setting · agency · active and passive characters · conflict furthering the ideas |
| 2025 | Sound · a supporting character · conflict driving narrative and character · cultural and/or historical context · a relationship evolving over time |

| Archetype | How often | Typical wording |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Every year | *Analyse how a supporting character is used to advance the central theme.* *Discuss how the director has depicted an antihero.* *Discuss the extent to which characters have agency.* |
| **Technique** | Every year | *Analyse how sound is used to enhance the visual storytelling.* *Discuss how symbolism is used to convey meaning.* *Discuss how the director has used allusion to enrich their work.* |
| **Conflict** | 2023, 2024, 2025 | *Analyse how conflict is used to drive narrative and develop characters.* |
| **Relationship** | 2023, 2025 | *Discuss how a relationship between characters evolves over time to reveal key themes.* |
| **Setting and context** | 2021, 2022, 2024, 2025 | *Discuss how setting is used to enrich the ideas.* *Discuss the ways the film reflects the cultural and/or historical context in which it was made or set.* |
| **Theme or value** | 2021–2023 | *Discuss how the director presents freedom and/or constraint.* *Explore how a transgression of boundaries contributed to meaning.* |

A character question and a technique question have appeared every year, and conflict in each of the last three.

**How to prepare.** Build **five or six sequences** from the table above that you know in forensic detail: ideally an opening, a confrontation, a sequence of concealment or surveillance, a revelation, and the ending. For each, record in your own words: the narrative context in one sentence, three observable details from three different categories, one effect per detail, and one thematic connection. Five or six well-known sequences recombine to answer every archetype above. Candidates who memorise essays are defeated by a question they did not predict; candidates who memorise sequences are not.

**In the room.** Underline the archetype and the focus noun. Choose the three or four sequences that bear on it most directly, in narrative order. Write the thesis as a claim about the **director's** method, not about the characters. Name the film and the director accurately in the introduction and use the present tense throughout.

**On context.** If the film is set in one period and made in another, you have two legitimate contexts. Say which you mean before you argue from it, and do not drift anachronistically into the viewer's present unless the question invites it.

## Mark-losing habits

| Habit | Fix |
|---|---|
| Retelling the plot | Every paragraph must open with a claim, not an event |
| Writing about characters as if they were real people | Attribute choices to the director: *the director frames*, *the editor withholds* |
| "The camera shows…" | Name the shot, the angle and the duration |
| Listing techniques without effects | Cut the list to three and analyse each |
| Three observations all from one category | Force one each from framing, editing and sound |
| Invented or half-remembered detail | If you cannot describe it accurately, use a sequence you can |
| Calling the film a book, or the director an author | A film has a **director**; its title is italicised |
| Ignoring the second half of the question | Underline *and/or*, *to what extent*, *one or more* and answer exactly what is asked |
| Answering the archetype you prepared rather than the one printed | Re-angle the material; do not reproduce a rehearsed essay |
