# Othello

One hour, 25 marks, one essay: **either** a general question on the whole play **or** a question on a printed passage. Closed book, so the quotation bank below has to be memorised. If you answer the general question, do not use evidence from the printed passage.

## The facts

| | |
|---|---|
| Playwright | William Shakespeare |
| Written | c. 1603–04 |
| First recorded performance | At court in Whitehall, 1 November 1604, by the King's Men. Jacobean: the reign of James I |
| Printed | A quarto in 1622; the First Folio in 1623 |
| Source | A novella by Cinthio, from *Gli Hecatommithi* (1565) |
| Genre | Domestic tragedy: the stakes are a marriage, not a kingdom |
| Setting | Act I in Venice; Acts II–V in Cyprus |

Venice is law, senate and public argument; Cyprus is a garrison island with no civil society. Marjorie Garber, the critic the school's course leans on, notes the *"geographical shift in the middle of the play"*: Venice stands for law and order, Cyprus for *"passion and confusion"*. Cyprus was also the island of Venus (Aphrodite), goddess of love. The geographical move is also the structural one: once the storm destroys the Turkish fleet, the external war ends and the internal war begins.

## Structure, act by act

| Act | Where | What happens | The turning point |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Venice | Iago and Roderigo wake Brabantio at night; Othello answers the accusation of witchcraft; the Senate sends him to Cyprus; Desdemona defends her marriage in open court | Brabantio's parting warning — the seed Iago will later water |
| II | Cyprus | A storm destroys the Turkish fleet; Iago arrives before Othello; he gets Cassio drunk, engineers a brawl, and has him dismissed; he advises Cassio to petition Desdemona | Cassio's dismissal, which gives Iago an innocent meeting to misread |
| III | Cyprus | The temptation scene; the demand for proof; the handkerchief is dropped and taken; Othello and Iago kneel and swear revenge; the handkerchief interrogation | **III.iii — the pivot of the play.** Othello transfers his trust from his wife to his ensign |
| IV | Cyprus | Othello's language collapses into prose; the epileptic fit; the eavesdropping trick; Bianca enters with the handkerchief; Othello strikes Desdemona publicly; the brothel scene; the Willow Song | The handkerchief appearing in Bianca's hand — the moment Othello takes as proof |
| V | Cyprus | Night and chaos again; Roderigo is killed, Cassio wounded; the murder in the marriage bed; Emilia exposes Iago and dies for it; Othello kills himself | Emilia's refusal to obey — the one thing Iago cannot control |

Three structural observations worth deploying:

- **The play begins and ends at night.** Act I scene i and Act V scene i are both night scenes of alarm in the street, and Othello enters the bedchamber in V.ii carrying a light. The torch is stagecraft with a thesis attached.
- **The marriage is interrupted twice** — by armed men on the wedding night, then by the brawl in Cyprus. The murder in the bed becomes the act that replaces consummation.
- **The state's competence is placed next to its blindness.** The Senate sees through the Turkish feint on Rhodes in the same scene in which it fails to see through Iago.

## The characters and what each is used for

| Character | Function in the argument |
|---|---|
| **Othello** | A general whose identity is built entirely on public regard and eloquence, and who therefore has no private self to fall back on. His confidence in Act I and his insecurity in Act III are one trait seen from two sides. Track his **language** as the index of his collapse: magnificent blank verse → shared and interrupted lines with Iago → fragmentary prose and animal imagery → recovered verse in the final speech, which is his attempt to reassemble a self before destroying it. |
| **Iago** | Ensign, the play's longest role. Speaks prose to Roderigo and verse alone in soliloquy — an actor changing register for each audience. Offers at least four motives and commits to none. He is also the audience's confidant, which implicates the audience in the murder it watches being assembled. |
| **Desdemona** | Not passive. She elopes, argues with her father, defends herself in open Senate and campaigns for Cassio with politically naïve persistence. Her father calls her *"A maiden never bold, / Of spirit so still and quiet"*, yet Othello's own account shows her giving him the *"hint"* to speak. Brabantio even sets the test himself (*"If she confess that she was half the wooer"*), and she passes it in open Senate. The gap between those accounts is the gap between how women are seen and what they do. Her name echoes the Greek for "ill-starred", and Othello calls her exactly that over her body: *"O ill-starred wench!"* |
| **Emilia** | The play's moral realist and, in the last act, its hero. She moves from complicity — she hands the handkerchief to Iago and, when Desdemona asks where it could be, answers *"I know not, madam"* (III.iv) — to open defiance, and dies for telling the truth. She also uses racist language after the murder; the play refuses to make anyone clean. |
| **Cassio** | Florentine, courteous, more scholar than soldier: Iago sneers at him as *"a great arithmetician"*. His promotion over Iago is the first grievance. His reputation speech is the play's clearest statement of the theme; his treatment of Bianca shows the same hierarchy of contempt working one rung lower. |
| **Bianca** | A courtesan genuinely in love and treated as disposable. Her name means "white", which detonates the play's colour symbolism. The third term in its study of women: Desdemona idealised, Emilia pragmatic, Bianca commodified — all three destroyed or degraded by one system. |
| **Roderigo** | Dupe and funder. The interesting answer to "victim or collaborator" is both. |
| **Brabantio** | Patriarchal possessiveness in a single repeated word: *my*. He dies of grief offstage, reported in the last scene. |

## Iago's motives

Coleridge's phrase for Iago's shifting justifications is *"the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity"*, written in his notes on the soliloquy that closes I.iii. The 2024 general question was built on it (*"How valid is this assessment of Iago's character?"*). The play gives you the grievances to test it with. Iago lists them, commits to none, and adds new ones as the play goes on:

| Motive | Where | The line |
|---|---|---|
| Passed over for promotion | I.i | *"Preferment goes by letter and affection, / And not by old gradation"* |
| Rumour that Othello slept with Emilia | I.iii; II.i | *"it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets / He has done my office"*; *"I do suspect the lusty Moor / Hath leaped into my seat"* |
| Suspicion of Cassio too | II.i | *"For I fear Cassio with my nightcap too"* |
| Desire for Desdemona | II.i | *"Now, I do love her too"* |
| Envy of Cassio's goodness | V.i | *"He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly"* |

He admits the Emilia rumour is unproven (*"I know not if't be true, / But I, for mere suspicion in that kind, / Will do as if for surety"*). The school's reading: there is no evidence for any of the sexual claims. The strongest essays **qualify** Coleridge. The motives are real but inadequate to the scale of the destruction, which is why they keep changing.

## Iago's techniques of manipulation

Not a list — a sequence. He uses each technique on a different victim, and the essay is stronger if you show the escalation.

| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Working unseen** | In the opening scene he speaks in darkness and cannot be identified. The first fact established about him is that he operates where he cannot be seen. |
| **Register-switching** | Prose and bawdy with Roderigo, soldierly plainness with Cassio, reluctant delicacy with Othello, and finally silence: *"From this time forth I never will speak word."* |
| **Insinuation rather than assertion** | Through most of III.iii he never states that Desdemona is unfaithful. He refuses to speak, and the refusal is the poison; Othello supplies the content. |
| **The echo** | He repeats Othello's own words back as questions, adding nothing, so that the implication appears to originate inside Othello's head. |
| **Borrowed authority** | He recycles Brabantio's warning about a daughter who deceived her father. The father's line becomes the ensign's weapon. |
| **Hypocritical moralising** | He warns against jealousy while manufacturing it, and praises good name to Othello after telling Cassio reputation is worthless. |
| **Fabricated evidence** | Asked for proof, he supplies a story — Cassio talking in his sleep. There is no dream. Othello demands ocular proof and accepts a narrative. |
| **Staging scenes** | He arranges entrances and exits and positions Othello to overhear a conversation about Bianca. He functions as a director with an audience of one. |
| **Pressure of time** | Every exchange demands an immediate response. Othello is never permitted to think slowly, which is the real mechanism of the tragedy. |
| **Soliloquy to the audience** | He explains his plan in advance, which converts the plot from mystery into dread and makes the audience a silent accomplice. |

## Dramatic irony

The engine of the whole play, and the convention most worth analysing because it is specific to drama.

- **"Honest Iago."** The word *honest* and its cognates appear around fifty times, almost always corrosively. Every use is a joke the audience is forced to share.
- **Advance disclosure.** Because Iago states his intentions in soliloquy, the audience watches a known catastrophe arrive on schedule. The suspense is not about outcome but about timing.
- **Cross-purpose dialogue.** In the handkerchief interrogation Desdemona keeps pleading for Cassio while Othello keeps demanding the handkerchief; each speech makes the other's case worse.
- **Emilia's guess.** She describes the villain exactly — an insinuating rogue who devised the slander to get an office — while standing next to her husband.
- **Complicity.** The audience, like Emilia for most of the play, sees and says nothing. In a play about a society governed by gossip, that silence is the point.

## Motifs and image patterns

| Pattern | Where it runs | The argument to make |
|---|---|---|
| **Animal imagery** | Iago introduces it in I.i with the ram and the ewe, the Barbary horse, the beast with two backs; Othello has adopted it by IV.i with *"goats and monkeys!"* | The clearest possible index of who is infecting whom. Track the transfer of a register from villain to hero. |
| **Poison and the ear** | *"I'll pour this pestilence into his ear"*; *"Dangerous conceits are in their natures poisons"*; *"the Moor already changes with my poison"* | Iago's imagery is medical because his method is infection, and the ear is the point of entry. The play is about what listening does. |
| **Devil and hell** | *"Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world's light"*; *"Divinity of hell!"*; Othello looking for cloven hooves | Iago is explicitly Satanic, which raises the question of whether the play needs him to have a motive at all. |
| **Light and dark, black and white** | Night scenes throughout; Othello entering with a taper; *"Put out the light, and then put out the light"*; *"whiter skin of hers than snow"* | The play sets up the conventional colour symbolism and then detonates it: the white Venetian is the devil, the Moor is noble, and the woman named White is a courtesan. |
| **The sea and the storm** | The tempest that destroys the Turks; Othello's oath comparing his vengeance to the Pontic sea, whose current never ebbs | The sea is the play's image for irreversibility. The storm resolves the war and then blows through the marriage. |
| **Money and trade** | *"Put money in thy purse"*, repeated to Roderigo; *"Who steals my purse steals trash"*; Brabantio's *my*; Bianca's transactional position | Love in this play keeps being described in the vocabulary of the market, which is also how reputation is treated. |
| **The handkerchief** | Dropped in III.iii, interrogated in III.iv, displayed by Bianca in IV.i | *"Spotted with strawberries"*: the text gives only the strawberries, but staging and criticism make the cloth white, suggesting the wedding sheets and so Desdemona's sexual honour made portable. It is *"a private love token"* that becomes *"a public spectacle"* (Garber). Othello gives two contradictory accounts of its origin, an Egyptian charmer's gift to his mother in III.iv and *"an antique token / My father gave my mother"* in V.ii, which proves the meaning is invested, not inherent. Its triviality is precisely the point. |
| **The willow** | Desdemona's song in IV.iii, learned from her mother's maid Barbary, who died singing it | The willow is the tree of forsaken lovers. The song gives her a lineage of abandoned women and makes her death feel foreordained. |
| **The fountain and the toads** | IV.ii | Othello imagines the *"fountain from the which my current runs"* turned into *"a cistern for foul toads / To knot and gender in"*: purity polluted into breeding filth, his imagination now working in Iago's register. |

## Themes, with an argument attached

A theme is not a topic. Each of these needs a claim.

- **Jealousy.** Not one emotion but a class of social disease. Distinguish Othello's jealousy (imposed, agonised, catastrophic) from Iago's (chronic, ungrounded, self-generating) and Bianca's (justified and dismissed). The play tests whether jealousy is an aberration or the ordinary condition of a possessive marriage.
- **Reputation.** Socially manufactured currency rather than a reflection of virtue. The paradox is structural: the man who tells Cassio that reputation is a false imposition tells Othello it is the immediate jewel of the soul. Iago knows exactly what reputation is worth — that is why he trades in it.
- **Race and outsiderness.** Othello is simultaneously the state's indispensable general and its permanent outsider; he is resented for his race and for being a *stranger* to a homogeneous Venice. The most painful moment is his adoption of Venetian racial vocabulary against himself. Two ironies to deploy: the villain is the white Venetian, and the woman named White is a whore.
- **Appearance and reality.** *"I am not what I am"* and *"Men should be what they seem"* are the play's two poles. Othello's fatal error is epistemological: he asks for ocular proof and accepts a story.
- **Manipulation and language.** Othello wins Desdemona with words and is destroyed by them. Iago's entire armoury is rhetorical — insinuation, the withheld statement, the echo, the anecdote, the invented dream.
- **Gender and marriage.** Three women, three social positions, three destructions. Emilia's speech arguing that wives have the same senses and appetites as husbands is the play's counter-argument, and it is given to a servant and immediately ignored.
- **Honesty.** The most ironised word in the play. Ask what a society can know when its only instrument for judging character is reputation reported by others.
- **Public duty and private life.** Othello's marriage is interrupted by war twice, and when he loses faith in Desdemona the first thing he mourns is his career. The play's deepest claim may be that he has no private self to lose.

## Dramatic conventions — the toolkit for criterion 3

Do not analyse a play as though it were a poem. These are the features that mark a drama essay.

| Convention | Where to use it |
|---|---|
| **Blank verse** | Unrhymed iambic pentameter; the register of nobility and control. Othello in Act I and in his final speech. |
| **Prose** | The register of the low, the comic, the intimate and the unravelling. Iago with Roderigo; Othello's collapse in IV.i. |
| **Shared / split lines** | Two speakers completing one pentameter. In III.iii Othello begins finishing Iago's lines — the takeover of his thought made audible. |
| **Rhyming couplet** | Closure, sententiousness, or an order inverted. Brabantio closing I.ii (*"Bondslaves and pagans shall our statesmen be"*); the Duke's and Brabantio's sententious couplets in I.iii; Iago's *"Hell and night"* couplet ending Act I; his rhymed "praise" of women in II.i. |
| **Soliloquy** | Iago's soliloquies give the audience poisoned intimacy and advance knowledge. |
| **Aside** | A remark the audience hears and the stage does not — Iago narrating Cassio and Desdemona in II.i. |
| **Stichomythia** | Rapid single-line exchange, as in the *"Honest, my lord?"* sequence. |
| **Epizeuxis** | Immediate repetition for pressure: *"Reputation, reputation, reputation!"*; *"O fool, fool, fool!"* |
| **Repetition across lines** | Othello's *"The handkerchief!"*, three times against Desdemona's pleas for Cassio in III.iv; *"It is the cause, it is the cause"* opening V.ii. |
| **Wordplay on one word** | *"Put out the light, and then put out the light"*: the same phrase means the candle, then Desdemona's life. The Clown's puns on *lie* (stay, tell a falsehood) in III.iv; Othello's *"Lie with her? Lie on her?"* in IV.i. |
| **Stagecraft: light** | Torches and tapers in the night scenes; Othello enters V.ii carrying a light and addresses the candle as *"thou flaming minister"*. |
| **Stagecraft: levels and concealment** | Brabantio "above", at a window, in I.i; Othello hidden to overhear in IV.i. |
| **Entrances and exits** | In III.iii Othello leaves and returns, and each return finds him further gone. |
| **Props** | The handkerchief, the taper, the bed. |
| **The bed** | Revealed at the climax; the marriage bed becomes the deathbed. |
| **Hamartia / peripeteia / anagnorisis** | The Greek-tragic frame: credulity as the flaw, the handkerchief in Bianca's hand as the reversal, *"O fool, fool, fool!"* as the recognition. |

If you have seen a production, you may name the director, company and year, describe one staging choice and infer the intention. That is high-value evidence for a drama essay and needs no quotation.

## Context and critics the course teaches

Use one of these in the hook or the explanation step, tied to a claim. Context should be contemporary to Shakespeare, never to the present day.

| Lens | The idea | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|
| **Jacobean London** | A growing trading port of about 200,000 people. Black men and women lived there, yet the slave trade had begun. Garber: Shakespeare's time was one of *"great historical changes and social anomalies"* | Othello as the indispensable outsider; Roderigo calls him *"an extravagant and wheeling stranger"* |
| **Light and dark** | Garber: European languages *"loaded the term blackness with negativity"*, privileging light as reason and goodness | The play inverts it: the white Venetian is the villain, *Bianca* ("the white one") a courtesan |
| **Machiavelli** | *The Prince* (written 1513) was read as praising cunning and deceit; "Machiavel" was a stage byword for a scheming villain | Iago as the Machiavellian manipulator and stage director |
| **Iago as devil or Judas** | The school reads him as a nihilist with no belief in a decent society | *"I look down towards his feet; but that's a fable"*; Garber calls him *"satanic"* |
| **Kahneman: fast and slow thinking** | System 1 is fast, emotional and prone to bias; System 2 is slow and logical | Iago keeps Othello in System 1 by denying him time to think |
| **Buss: evolutionary psychology** | Male sexual jealousy is tied to paternity uncertainty and can turn violent; some jealousy shows a partner is valued | Othello's and Iago's sexual jealousy; the cuckold's horns |
| **Dunbar's number** | We evolved in bands of about 150; much of our talk is gossip about reputation | Why reputation and rumour (*"it is thought abroad"*) govern Venice |
| **Garber on Othello** | *"Othello's problem in the opening acts of the play is fundamentally a refusal to acknowledge the private nature of his passions and his own person"* | Public duty against private life |
| **Garber on the kneeling** | III.iii's *"two kneeling soldiers"* is *"the parody of a marriage"* | *"I am your own for ever"* |
| **Garber on the audience** | Iago *"has made us (the audience) his unwitting and willing co-conspirators, presuming our silence"* | Dramatic irony as complicity |

Garber's quotations come from the school's slides (her book is *Shakespeare After All*, 2004). Quote them as Garber's and then qualify them; the school's essay guide rewards agreement with qualification above both wholesale agreement and wholesale disagreement.

## General question or passage question

They are different essays. Choose on which one you can evidence, not on which looks easier.

| | General question | Passage-based question |
|---|---|---|
| Typical wording | *"Examine the ways in which Shakespeare presents [abstract noun] in the play"*, or a quoted statement followed by *"How far do you agree?"*, *"How valid is this assessment?"* or *"With this comment in mind…"* | *"Comment on how, and with what effects, Shakespeare presents [character, relationship, state of mind] / creates [tension and suspense] at this point in the play"* |
| Scope of evidence | The whole play, drawn from memory. Every past paper forbids using evidence from the printed passage here | The extract first, with deliberate links out to the rest of the play, **with quotations** |
| Organising principle | **Chronological by act**, so the argument develops as the play does | **Sequential through the extract**, or by speaker |
| What the marker rewards | Range across all five acts, and a thesis that holds across them | Close reading of the extract *plus* demonstrated knowledge of the wider text |
| The characteristic failure | Four paragraphs all drawn from Act III | Analysing the extract as though the play around it did not exist |

**General question — four-paragraph shape**

1. **Act I: establishment.** Venice, order, the theme introduced, Iago's first move.
2. **Acts II–III: escalation.** Cyprus, the temptation scene, the theme weaponised.
3. **Acts III–IV: collapse.** The handkerchief, the language, the fit.
4. **Act V: consequence.** The murder and the reckoning; what the play finally concludes about the theme.

Worked example — *"Examine the ways in which Shakespeare presents reputation in the play."*

> *Thesis:* Shakespeare presents reputation not as a reflection of virtue but as a socially manufactured currency, and by showing that it can be counterfeited by anyone willing to lie, he exposes the fragility of a society that governs itself by regard rather than by evidence.
>
> P1 — Reputation as inheritance under attack: Brabantio's name and the animal slanders in I.i; Othello's reputation as the state's most valuable asset in I.iii, which is also why it is worth destroying.
> P2 — Reputation as identity: Cassio's *"immortal part of myself"* set directly against Iago's *"idle and most false imposition"*, and then against Iago's own *"immediate jewel of their souls"*. The reversal proves that for Iago reputation is not a value but a lever.
> P3 — Reputation as a weapon against women: from *"She did deceive her father"* to *"made to write 'whore' upon"*. Desdemona is destroyed by a name she cannot bring herself to say.
> P4 — Reputation at the end: *"Speak of me as I am"* — the final speech as narrative self-management, delivered by a man killing himself while telling the story of killing a Turk.

**Passage question — four-paragraph shape**

1. **Structure and pacing of the extract** — line lengths, interruptions, shared lines, the rhythm of question and evasion, where the extract turns.
2. **One speaker's language** — diction, imagery, register, verse or prose.
3. **The other speaker's language in contrast**, and the interaction between them.
4. **What the audience knows that the characters do not**, and how the extract points forward and back in the play.

Four rules for the passage answer: contextualise in one or two sentences without retelling the plot; make links beyond the extract **with quotations from elsewhere**; use at least two language features and three to five short quotations per paragraph; and do not use passage material at all if you answer the general question.

The general question comes in two shapes: *examine the ways Shakespeare presents an abstract noun*, or a quoted judgement on a character or idea that you must test. Nouns used on the sister plays and therefore live: guilt, violence, honour, identity, deception, men and masculinity, the supernatural. Prepare **jealousy, deception, the presentation of women, Othello as hero or victim, and Iago's motives** in full, and be ready to re-angle the reputation and imagery plans onto anything else.

Likely extracts, on precedent: dense two- or three-hander scenes from every act. Expect I.iii (the Senate), III.iii in any of its parts, III.iv (the handkerchief), IV.ii (the brothel scene and its aftermath), IV.iii (the willow scene) and V.ii (the murder).
