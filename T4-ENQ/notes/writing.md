# Writing and argument

On Papers 3 and 4, three of the five criteria (response to the question, organisation and development, and expression) are decided by how you write: 15 of the 25 marks. On Paper 1, organisation and expression are 10 of the 25. This is the only topic examined in all three papers.

## Unpack the question first

Every question has three parts. Separate them on the paper before you read anything else.

| Part | Example from *"Examine the ways in which Shakespeare presents reputation in the play"* |
|---|---|
| **Command word** | *Examine the ways* — analyse method, not content |
| **Focus noun** | *reputation* — every paragraph must serve this word |
| **Limits** | *in the play* — the whole play, not one act; and, if you answer the general question, not the printed passage |

Then ask two further questions. **Does it contain "and/or"?** If so, choose, and say in the thesis which you have chosen. **Does it contain a quoted critical comment?** If so, you must engage with it — agree, qualify or dispute it explicitly, rather than ignoring it and writing the essay you prepared.

## Command words

| Command | What it actually demands |
|---|---|
| **Analyse** | Break the method into parts and explain how each produces its effect. The weight is on mechanism, not judgement. |
| **Discuss** | Present a reasoned case with more than one side acknowledged, then reach a position. A discussion with no counter-case is an assertion. |
| **Evaluate** | Judge, with criteria. State what would count as success or failure before you decide whether it is achieved. |
| **Explore** | Follow an idea through the text, including where it complicates itself. Permits — and rewards — ambiguity and development. |
| **Examine the ways** | Identify the methods and analyse the effect of each. "Ways" is plural: at least three distinct methods. |
| **To what extent / comment on the extent** | A question of degree. The answer is never simply yes or no; it is *largely, because X, but not in respect of Y*. The concession is the mark. |
| **How does the writer** | Method only. The subject of your sentences should be the writer, the playwright or the director, not the characters. |
| **Comment on how, and with what effects** | Two obligations: the method, and its effect on the reader or viewer. An answer that names methods without effects answers half the question. |
| **Compare** | Sustained two-way comparison inside each paragraph, not one text and then the other. Use *whereas*, *by contrast*, *similarly*. |
| **With this comment in mind / How far do you agree / How valid is this assessment** | The quoted comment is a position to test. Say how far it holds, and where it does not. All three wordings have been set on Paper 3. |

## From question to thesis

A thesis is a claim someone could disagree with. A topic is not.

| Not a thesis | Why | A thesis |
|---|---|---|
| *This essay will discuss reputation in Othello.* | Announces, claims nothing | *Shakespeare presents reputation as a manufactured currency rather than a reflection of virtue, and in showing that anyone willing to lie can counterfeit it he exposes a society that governs itself by regard rather than by evidence.* |
| *The writer uses a lot of imagery.* | Names a device, makes no argument | *The writer's imagery of enclosure converts the speaker's grief into a problem of architecture, so that the loss becomes something she lives inside rather than something she feels.* |
| *The director shows that war is bad.* | Unfalsifiable and generic | *The director demotes the external conflict in favour of conflicts inside the institution, arguing that the damage was done by the people who shared an office rather than by the enemy.* |

Build it in three steps:

1. Write the focus noun.
2. Ask what the text finally **claims** about it. The structural turn — a volta, a reversal, the final act — usually supplies the answer.
3. Phrase it with an intention verb so the sentence attributes agency to the writer: *exposes, qualifies, undercuts, interrogates, elevates, legitimises, satirises, refuses, domesticates, complicates*.

Place the thesis as the **last sentence of the introduction**, and open it with a discourse marker — *Ultimately*, *Thus*, *Therefore*.

## The introduction — four moves, four sentences

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | In the school's definition, an opening statement that introduces a broad, universally applicable idea about a facet of human experience. For a studied text, relevant context or a critic's view also works. Not a dictionary definition, not praise of the text, not a sweeping claim about all of history, not a biography of the author. |
| **Transition** | Narrows that claim to this text. **Name the title and the writer, playwright or director accurately here.** Repeating a small phrase from the hook stitches the two sentences together and reads as control. |
| **Rationale** | A brief indication of the sequence of points to come, in the order they will come. Write it at the level of character, event, relationship or setting. Do not list the devices you will analyse, and avoid *firstly, secondly*. |
| **Thesis** | The arguable claim, answering the question, in one sentence. |

For a passage-based question, add one further move: one or two sentences placing the extract in the plot. Do not retell the story. No evidence or analysis is needed in the introduction at all.

Do not open by identifying audience and purpose. It earns nothing.

## The body paragraph

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| **Point** | A statement of informed opinion that supports the thesis and gives a reason to believe it. Its substance should already have been signalled in the rationale. Open with a discourse marker. |
| **Evidence** | A direct quotation, contextualised and integrated, that directly illustrates the point. |
| **Analysis** | An examination of the evidence that moves beyond its superficial meaning and shows how literary or filmic conventions imply further meaning. |
| **Explanation** | Exactly how the evidence illustrates or proves the point sentence, at the level of theme. |
| **Link** | How the point and analysis address the question and develop the thesis. Reuse a key word from the question. |

Evidence and analysis **repeat** inside a paragraph: aim for at least two language features from different categories, and three to five short quotations. The school's shorthand is **P.E.E.: Point, Evidence, Explore**, with point and evidence carrying 25% of the weight and exploration 75%. If your paragraph is mostly quotation and summary, the structure itself has capped your mark.

**The colon trick.** When a point sentence leans on a vague abstract noun or adjective, add a colon and say exactly what you mean: *Othello is presented as insecure: he believes, at some level, that an outsider can never be good enough for a Venetian wife.*

A template for the link sentence:

> Since [the analysis] reveals [this aspect of the point], it follows that [the thesis, in whole or in part].

Every paragraph must pass through three levels: **language → literary or filmic element (character, structure, setting) → theme.** Skipping the middle level produces the "how?" failure, where a device is wired directly to a universal truth. Stopping at the middle level produces the "so what?" failure, where the analysis ends at the character and never reaches the argument.

## Embedding quotations

The school's rule is **SICK**: every quotation should be **S**elective (only the words you will analyse), **I**ntegrated (inside your own sentence), **C**ontextualised (who says it, when and why) and given with **K**nowledge of purpose (signal why you are using it).

Three legal methods of integration. Never let a quotation stand as its own sentence.

| Method | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue tag** | Name, verb, comma, then the quotation beginning with a capital | As Iago claims, "I am not what I am." |
| **Colon** | An independent clause, a colon, the quotation | Iago announces his own duplicity: "I am not what I am." |
| **Flow on** | The quotation sits inside your syntax — no tag, no comma, no capital | Iago's declaration that he is "not what I am" collapses being into seeming. |

- Use **ellipses** for what you have cut and **square brackets** for a word, pronoun or tense you have altered.
- Contextualise by speaker, moment and circumstance — who said it, when, why — not by page number.
- Signal before the quotation what you intend to draw from it, so the marker knows the quotation was chosen rather than found.
- Not every quotation needs full analysis. **Citing** short phrases while analysing two or three in depth is how strong answers reach four or five quotations in a paragraph.

## The shortest sufficient quotation

Keep the word that, if removed, makes the quotation meaningless. Cut everything else.

| Too long | Sufficient |
|---|---|
| "Good name in man and woman, dear my lord, / Is the immediate jewel of their souls" | "the immediate jewel of their souls" |
| "O, beware, my lord, of jealousy! / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on" | "the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on" |

Long quotations cost time, fill the page without earning credit, and signal that you have not decided which word matters. Four or five words is usually enough.

## Write about choices, not plot

Make the writer the subject of your sentences.

| Plot summary | Analysis of choice |
|---|---|
| Othello then demands proof from Iago, and Iago makes up a dream about Cassio. | Shakespeare answers Othello's demand for *"ocular proof"* with a fabricated anecdote, so that the general's criterion for truth is satisfied by exactly the thing it was meant to exclude. |
| The poem ends with the speaker alone in the house. | The poet withholds the expected return until the final line and then supplies an absence instead, so that the structure enacts the disappointment it describes. |

A useful test on every sentence: **could this sentence appear in a plot summary?** If yes, rewrite it or delete it. Another: **does the sentence name a choice?** Selection, placement, omission, repetition, contrast and sequence are all choices, and all available even when no obvious device is present.

## Counter-argument

Concession is not weakness; on *to what extent* and *discuss* questions it is where the marks are.

- **Concede and qualify.** *It is true that the speaker's grief appears to resolve in the final stanza; the half rhyme, however, withholds the closure the sense asserts.*
- **Name a competing reading and adjudicate.** *The gesture may equally register habit rather than fear; the surrounding paragraph's repeated conditionals make the second reading harder to sustain.*
- **Argue against the premise.** If a question asserts something the text does not support (that characters interest us only when they transform, for instance), say so, and build the essay on the better claim. Examiners reward engagement with the premise, not obedience to it.

**Quoted critics.** Paper 3 general questions often quote a critic, such as Coleridge on Iago's *"motiveless malignity"*. The school's essay guide gives three options: wholesale agreement, wholesale disagreement, or **agreement with qualifications**. The third scores best. Test the claim against all the characters and every stage of the play, and ask whether its language is too strong or too weak.

Concession markers to use deliberately: *Although*, *While it is true that*, *Even though*, *It may well be that… however*, *Regardless of the fact that*, *Nonetheless*, *Be that as it may*.

## Conclusions

Two moves, and nothing else.

1. **Summation**: a brief restatement of the thesis as something you have now demonstrated, in different words from the introduction, reusing key words of the question.
2. **Contemporary connect**: how the text, and the broad idea it yields, is relevant to the reader in a moral or philosophical way.

Rules: introduce no new evidence; repeat no sentence verbatim; keep it to three or four sentences; and write it even if a body paragraph is unfinished. The conclusion is the last thing the marker reads before deciding the grade.

Any historical context you use should be contemporary **to the writer**: a Jacobean audience for a Jacobean play. Anachronistic comparisons to current events are a named fault; the connection is to a moral or philosophical idea, not to the news. For a film set in one period and made in another, you have two legitimate contexts; say which one you mean.

## Register and sentence variety

- **No contractions**, no slang, no colloquialism, no informal *you*, no abbreviations such as *etc.* or *e.g.*
- **No comma splices.** Two independent clauses need a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction. This is the single most common expression error.
- **Present tense for the text, past tense for history.** Iago *manipulates*; the play *was first performed* in 1604.
- **Titles italicised** (underlined in handwriting). A play has a playwright, a film has a director, a novel has a novelist. Never "the author of the film", never "the book".
- **Avoid vague praise** — *strong imagery, effective scene, powerfully written, interesting, important*. These are unsubstantiated and read as filler.
- **Do not let emphasis words replace argument.** *Clearly, undoubtedly* and *without question* are on the school's list of emphasis markers, but only after the evidence has earned them. Never use them in place of analysis.
- **Never write "this quote shows".** The school's advice is that you need never write the words *quote* or *theme* at all. Name the device and describe its mechanism: *A simile compares… and the effect is to highlight…*; or use a precise verb such as *conveys, connotes, intimates, evinces, exposes, elucidates* or *undercuts*.
- **Vary sentence length on purpose.** A long accumulating sentence followed by a short declarative gives the short one force. Three long sentences in a row lose the reader; three short ones sound like notes.
- **Only non-living things can be personified.** A human character cannot be personified.
- **Spell names and titles correctly.** Drill them: *Othello*, *Desdemona*, *Iago*, *Emilia*, *Cassio*, *Brabantio*, *Roderigo*, *Bianca*, *Cyprus*. A misspelt title costs expression marks on sight.

Discourse markers, chosen for the relationship you actually mean:

| Relationship | Markers |
|---|---|
| Addition | Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Equally, Likewise, Indeed |
| Contrast | However, By contrast, Nevertheless, Conversely, Yet, On the other hand |
| Concession | Although, While it is true, Even so, Granted that, Admittedly |
| Cause and effect | Consequently, Therefore, Thus, Accordingly, Hence, As a result, Since |
| Illustration | For instance, As illustrated, Notably, In the case of |
| Emphasis | Above all, Significantly, Crucially, In truth |
| Conclusion | Ultimately, In summation, On the whole, To conclude, As discussed |

The school's full lists of discourse markers, analysis phrases and author-intention verbs are in [TERMS.md](../TERMS.md).

## Timing a 25-mark hour

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read all the questions. Choose. Annotate the command word, the focus noun and the limits. |
| 5–12 | Plan: four or five point sentences, with two or three pieces of evidence noted under each. Draft the thesis in the margin. |
| 12–52 | Write. Roughly eight minutes per paragraph. |
| 52–57 | Conclusion — summation and widening. Never rush it. |
| 57–60 | Proofread for the four killers: comma splices, contractions, tense slippage, and misspelt names or titles. |

Seven minutes of planning is not time lost. An essay with a thesis and an ordered rationale cannot drift, and drift is what the response-to-question and organisation criteria penalise. Aim for **about 650 words**, the school's target for the hour. Treat **four body paragraphs** as the working target and three as the minimum; the school's exemplar commentary uses four in 644 words. The literature-essay schedule ties its top expression bands to essays of over 600 words.

If you run out of time mid-paragraph, stop and write the conclusion. A complete argument with a thin fourth paragraph outscores an incomplete one.

## The most common mark-losing habits

| Habit | Why it costs | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Summarising the text | Caps the understanding criterion at the superficial band | Every paragraph opens with a claim, not an event |
| No thesis, or a thesis that is a topic | Penalised under response to the question and organisation | Write the arguable sentence before the first paragraph |
| Listing devices | Identification without analysis earns the bottom of the conventions band | Three features analysed beat eight named |
| Long quotation, one line of analysis | Inverts the weighting the marker applies | Shorten the quotation, lengthen the exploration |
| "This quote shows…" | Banned phrasing, and it invites the omission of analysis | Name the device and its mechanism |
| Comma splices and contractions | Direct expression marks | Proofread the last three minutes for these two faults only |
| Answering the question you prepared | Excellent expression cannot reach full marks on irrelevant material | Re-angle prepared material onto the printed focus noun |
| Ignoring half the question | Loses the second obligation entirely | Underline *and/or*, *to what extent*, *and with what effects* |
| Using the printed passage in a general answer | Explicitly prohibited on every past Paper 3 | Answer from memory; keep the extract closed |
| Anachronistic conclusions | A named fault | Widen to the writer's own moment |
| No conclusion | Loses the final impression and the organisation marks | Fix the conclusion at minute 52 regardless of what is unfinished |
| Writing about characters as real people | Misses the method the question asks for | Make the writer or director the subject of the sentence |
