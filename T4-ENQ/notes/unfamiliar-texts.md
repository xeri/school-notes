# Unfamiliar texts

One hour, 25 marks, one commentary on **either** the printed prose extract **or** the printed poem. You cannot revise the content, so the method has to be automatic.

## Choosing, in three minutes

| Choose the poem if | Choose the prose if |
|---|---|
| A formal pattern is immediately visible — stanza shape, rhyme scheme, refrain, a clear volta | There is a clear tonal shift, or a strong and distinctive narrative voice |
| You can hear the sound patterning | Narrative distance or free indirect discourse is doing obvious work |
| It is short enough to cover whole | There is a semantic field you can name in one phrase |

**The tiebreaker is the allusion.** Choose the text whose biblical or mythical reference you can actually name and use. Once you have chosen, do not reconsider.

## The hour

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | Annotate the question. Circle the focus noun. If it offers "and/or", pick one and say which. |
| 1–6 | Read both texts for sense. Choose. |
| 6–9 | Second read of your chosen text, with a pen. Mark the allusion, the turn, repeated words, odd syntax. |
| 9–13 | Plan four or five point sentences. Each names a *category* of technique and makes a *claim about meaning*. |
| 13–53 | Write. Roughly eight minutes per paragraph. |
| 53–57 | Conclusion: the thesis as demonstrated, then one widening sentence. |
| 57–60 | Proofread for comma splices, contractions, tense slippage, and the spelling of the author's name and title. |

## What to annotate

Annotate **to the question**, not comprehensively. Everything you mark must be capable of serving the focus noun.

- The **allusion** — box it.
- The **turn** — a volta, a change of tense or person, a paragraph or stanza beginning *Yet*, *But*, *And then*, a move from external description to internal reflection. Mark it with a horizontal line across the text.
- **Repeated words and repeated sounds** — link them with arrows.
- **Anything anomalous**: a one-line stanza among quatrains, a fragment among full sentences, a sudden monosyllable, white space, a dash.
- **The first and last lines**. Openings and closings are always load-bearing and always worth a sentence.

## Speaker, situation, shift

Before any analysis, answer three questions in the margin:

1. **Who speaks?** A persona, a named narrator, an omniscient third person, a limited third person? Is the speaker the subject of the text or an observer of it?
2. **What is the situation?** Where, when, to whom, and what has just happened. A poem addressed to an absent person behaves differently from one addressed to the reader.
3. **Where does it change?** The shift is the single most rewarded observation in this paper. Examiners praise tracking of tonal change more than any other feature.

If you cannot name the situation, say what is uncertain about it and analyse the uncertainty — withheld information is a technique, not an obstacle.

## Structure and form

| Term | What to say about it |
|---|---|
| **Stanza** | A unit of argument. Equal stanzas suggest control; an irregular one isolates its content. |
| **Line** | The poet's unit of attention. What word is given the end of the line, and what is given the beginning? |
| **Enjambment** | Sense runs over the line break without pause — creates momentum, hesitation, or a double reading of the last word before the break. |
| **End-stopping** | A line closes on punctuation — creates finality, flatness, or the rhythm of statement. |
| **Caesura** | A pause inside the line — interrupts, hesitates, or splits a line into two opposed halves. |
| **Volta** | The structural turn in a sonnet or any poem — the point where the argument changes direction. |
| **Refrain** | Repetition across stanzas — accumulates meaning, since the same words mean something different the second time. |
| **Sentence length** | A long accumulating sentence followed by a three-word sentence makes the short one land. Name the contrast, not just the length. |
| **Paragraphing (prose)** | Where the extract breaks is where the writer wants a gap. A one-sentence paragraph is emphasis. |
| **Cyclical structure** | The close returns to the opening — suggests inescapability or a change in what the same image now means. |
| **In medias res** | Opening mid-action, forcing inference and withholding orientation. |

Say **where** in the text something sits: "placed at the volta", "withheld until the final line", "buried mid-paragraph". Position is evidence.

## Diction and register

- **Connotation, not definition.** *Thin* and *slender* denote the same thing and imply opposite judgements.
- **Semantic field** — a cluster of words from one domain (commerce, disease, warfare, religion, farming). Quote three of them together and name the field; this is the fastest route to an argument about the writer's attitude.
- **Register** — formal, colloquial, archaic, technical, legal, biblical, childlike. A shift in register mid-text is a shift in the speaker's relationship to the subject.
- **Word class** matters. Abstract nouns generalise; concrete nouns anchor. Dynamic verbs give agency; stative verbs remove it. The passive voice deletes the agent — ask who has been deleted.
- **Modality** — *must*, *might*, *should*, *cannot*. Modal verbs are the grammar of certainty, and certainty is usually the subject.
- **Intensifiers and diminutives** — *merely*, *only*, *just*, *a little* — shrink things; ask what is being made small and by whom.

Illustrating with a sentence of my own: *"She managed the garden, as she managed everything, with a pair of scissors."* The verb *managed*, repeated across two unlike objects, imports the vocabulary of administration into domestic life, and the concrete *pair of scissors* makes the abstraction physical and faintly violent.

## Imagery

| Device | Precise definition | What to analyse |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | Comparison using *like* or *as* | Why the comparison was chosen — what the vehicle brings that the subject lacks |
| **Metaphor** | Direct identification of one thing with another | The claim being made by the identification, which a simile would have softened |
| **Extended metaphor** | A metaphor sustained across lines or paragraphs | Where it is introduced, where it is abandoned, and whether it holds |
| **Personification** | A non-living thing given human qualities | What relationship it creates between speaker and world. Humans cannot be personified. |
| **Pathetic fallacy** | Landscape or weather reflecting human feeling | Whether the text endorses the projection or exposes it |
| **Symbol** | A concrete thing standing for an abstraction | How the text *invests* the object — symbols are made by context, not by nature |
| **Motif** | A recurring image or idea | What changes about it between appearances |
| **Sensory imagery** | Appeals to sight, sound, touch, smell, taste | Which sense dominates, and what is conspicuously absent |
| **Synaesthesia** | One sense described in another's terms | The confusion of perception it dramatises |

## Sound

| Device | Definition | Typical effect to argue for |
|---|---|---|
| **Metre** | The repeating rhythmic pattern of stresses | Regularity enacts control or ritual; a broken foot enacts the thing that breaks it |
| **Iambic pentameter** | Five unstressed-stressed pairs per line | The rhythm closest to speech; departures from it are the point |
| **Trochaic / dactylic rhythm** | Falling rhythms, stress first | Insistence, chant, nursery rhyme, incantation |
| **Full rhyme** | Matching final stressed vowels and following consonants | Closure, inevitability, neatness that may be ironic |
| **Half rhyme** | Approximate rhyme | Expectation raised and denied — unease, incompleteness |
| **Internal rhyme** | Rhyme within a line | Binds two words into a claim |
| **Alliteration** | Repeated initial consonants | Name the consonant type: plosives (*p, b, t, d, k*) are abrupt and percussive; fricatives (*f, v, th*) are breathy and continuous |
| **Assonance** | Repeated vowel sounds | Long vowels slow the line; short vowels quicken it |
| **Consonance** | Repeated consonant sounds anywhere in the word | Hardness, texture, a knitting-together of unlike words |
| **Sibilance** | Repeated *s*, *sh*, *z* sounds | Hush, whisper, menace, the noise of air or water |
| **Onomatopoeia** | A word imitating its sound | Collapses the gap between language and event |

Never list sounds. Name the consonant class, say what noise it makes, then say what that noise does: *"The repeated plosive 'b' and 'd' sounds reproduce the knock of the gate against its post, and the effect is to make the sound of the place more present to the speaker than the view beyond it."*

## Tone and tonal shift

**Tone** is the speaker's attitude to the subject. **Mood** is the atmosphere of the world of the text. They are different and the distinction is marked.

Use a precise tone word, not a strong one: *wry, clinical, elegiac, resigned, nostalgic, indignant, detached, reverent, flippant, foreboding, matter-of-fact, embittered*.

A tonal shift is built, not announced. Look for:

- a change in sentence length or in punctuation density
- a change of tense or person
- the arrival of a word from a different register
- the first question, or the first imperative
- the point where a concrete description becomes an abstract claim

Devote one paragraph explicitly to the shift: where it happens, what signals it, and what the change in attitude reveals.

## Biblical and mythical allusion

Both printed texts will contain at least one, and it is worth 5 of the 25 marks. Hunt for it deliberately on the second read.

**Biblical signals**

| In the text | Allusion | What it imports |
|---|---|---|
| A garden, a wall, forbidden ground, a tree, fruit, nakedness | Eden and the Fall | Innocence lost; knowledge as transgression; exile; the origin of shame |
| A serpent, a whispered temptation | The serpent in Genesis | Persuasion as evil; deceit entering through language |
| Two brothers, jealousy, a killing | Cain and Abel | The first violence; the mark of the outcast |
| Rain, a flood, an ark, a dove | Noah | Cleansing destruction; covenant; beginning again |
| A tower, hubris, confused speech | Babel | Overreach; the fragmentation of understanding |
| A father told to sacrifice his son | Abraham and Isaac | Obedience; the terrible demands of faith |
| Wilderness, forty years, a promised land | Exodus | Deliverance deferred; long suffering before arrival |
| Suffering without cause | Job | Undeserved affliction; faith tested to breaking |
| A kiss, silver, an intimate betrayal | Judas | Treachery bought; betrayal from inside |
| A cross, three days, a stone rolled away | Crucifixion and Resurrection | Sacrifice; redemption; rebirth |
| A son returning, a father forgiving | The Prodigal Son | Waste, return, unearned grace |
| A stranger who stops to help | The Good Samaritan | Compassion crossing a social boundary |
| Light separated from darkness | Genesis 1 | Creation; order made out of chaos |

**Classical and mythical signals**

| In the text | Allusion | What it imports |
|---|---|---|
| A jar or box not to be opened; a forbidden door | Pandora | Curiosity, blame, released suffering — and hope left at the bottom |
| Wings, wax, flying too high, the sea | Icarus and Daedalus | Hubris; the cost of ambition; a father's failed protection |
| A maze, a monster, a thread | Theseus, the Minotaur, Ariadne | Being lost; guidance; the monster at the centre |
| A long voyage home, delays, a waiting spouse | Odysseus and Penelope | Homecoming deferred; endurance; identity across time |
| A backward glance that loses her | Orpheus and Eurydice | Grief; the fatal look back; art set against death |
| A statue that comes to life | Pygmalion | Making the beloved; the artist's possessiveness |
| A youth in love with his reflection; a voice that repeats | Narcissus and Echo | Self-absorption; a self with no independent speech |
| A descent, a river, a ferryman | Hades, Persephone, Charon | Death; seasonal return; a crossing that cannot be undone |
| Stolen fire, endless punishment | Prometheus | Defiant benefaction; suffering for knowledge |
| A rock rolled uphill for ever | Sisyphus | Futile, repeated labour |
| Fruit or water always out of reach | Tantalus | Desire structurally frustrated |
| Prophecy nobody believes | Cassandra | Truth without credibility |
| Three women spinning, measuring, cutting | The Fates | Determinism; a life measured out |
| A wooden horse, a ten-year siege | Troy | Deception; the long ruin of pride |
| Ashes and a bird | The phoenix | Cyclical renewal |

**Writing the allusion paragraph** — four moves, in its own paragraph, usually third or fourth.

1. **Identify.** Quote the signal words and name the story explicitly.
2. **Explain the source** in one clause. Assume the marker knows it; show that you do.
3. **Map** the parallel: which figure or object corresponds to which element of the story.
4. **Analyse the divergence.** The strongest marks come from where the text *departs* from the source — a Pandora figure who opens nothing, an Eden with no prohibition, a resurrection that fails.

**How not to overclaim.** Do not force an allusion onto an ordinary image. A test: can you point to **two independent signals**? One apple is a fruit; an apple plus a wall plus a prohibition is Eden. If you have only one signal, write about the image as an image — *"the maze image suggests frustrating enclosure"* is a complete and creditable analysis without naming Theseus. Where the reference is possible but thin, say so in a clause — *"the orchard invites, without insisting on, a reading through Eden"* — and then analyse the invitation. Hedged accuracy scores; confident invention does not.

## From naming a device to earning the mark

The invariant shape of a creditable analytical sentence:

> **name the device → describe its literal mechanism → "and the effect is to…" → state the implied meaning**

Weak: *"The writer uses a simile which shows the father is lonely."*
Strong: *"The simile compares the father to a word detached from its sentence, and the effect is to present his isolation as a failure of grammar rather than of feeling — he has not been abandoned so much as rendered unreadable."*

The middle step is the one weak commentaries skip, and it is the one the conventions criterion exists to reward. Identification alone earns one or two marks out of five; explaining how the convention generates meaning earns four or five.

## Quoting

- Quote **four or five words**, not sentences. Keep only the words that would make the quotation meaningless if removed.
- Use ellipses for what you cut and square brackets for what you alter.
- Integrate grammatically — the quotation must sit inside your own sentence, never stand alone as its own.
- Signal before the quotation what you are about to draw from it.
- Analyse the **wording**, not the content. Replacing *"the sky was blue"* with *"the heavens were turquoise"* is paraphrase wearing the costume of analysis.

## Building the thesis from the focus question

The thesis is a single sentence, the last of the introduction, and it must contain the focus noun.

1. Write the question's focus noun down.
2. Ask: **what does the text finally claim about it?** Not "it presents the speaker's state of mind" — *what* state, and what does the presentation argue?
3. The **turn** usually supplies the answer. If the text moves from assurance to doubt, that movement is your thesis.
4. Phrase it with an intention verb: the author *exposes*, *qualifies*, *undercuts*, *elevates*, *interrogates*, *domesticates*, *refuses*.

Then order the paragraphs so the argument develops. A reliable sequence: **structure → imagery → diction and sound → allusion → the close.**

## A worked demonstration

These four lines are my own, written for the exercise. Suppose the focus question were: *Comment on how, and with what effects, the author presents the speaker's attitude to what has been lost.*

> The orchard gate stood open all that summer,
> and we walked under branches we had planted,
> counting the apples that we would not eat.
> By August, the wasps had got there first.

**Structure.** The first three lines run on in a single accumulating sentence, enjambed twice, so the syntax keeps extending as the summer does. The fourth line is a separate, end-stopped sentence of nine monosyllables and one disyllable, and it arrives without a conjunction. The turn is therefore structural before it is semantic: the poem stops accumulating and simply reports. Placing the loss in an unlinked final line presents it as something that did not need to be argued for, only noticed.

**Diction.** The verbs divide between the two halves. *Stood*, *walked*, *counting* are stative or habitual, and they belong to a continuous, unfinished summer; *had got* is perfective and belongs to a completed event the speaker did not witness. The shift in tense is the shift in attitude — the speaker moves from inhabiting the time to looking back at it. *Counting* is the pivotal word: a verb of husbandry used on fruit already conceded, since the relative clause *"that we would not eat"* withdraws the harvest in the same breath that counts it.

**Imagery and sound.** *Branches we had planted* compresses labour and inheritance into three words, and the possessive *we had* makes the loss self-inflicted rather than accidental. The sibilance of *stood*, *summer* and *walked under* gives the first three lines a continuous, breathy movement; the plosives of *By August*, *got* and *first* break it. Naming the consonant classes is what makes this an argument rather than an observation: the soft sounds belong to duration, the hard sounds to interruption.

**Allusion.** *Orchard*, *gate*, *apples* and a *summer* that ends are four independent signals, which is enough to name **Eden and the Fall** without overclaiming. The lines import the pattern of a garden enjoyed and then lost. But the divergence is the interesting part: there is no prohibition, no serpent and no transgression. Nothing is forbidden and nobody disobeys; the fruit is lost to wasps and to August. The effect is to domesticate the Fall — to suggest that expulsion needs no sin, and that the ordinary operation of a season is sufficient to end a paradise. That makes the speaker's attitude one of resignation rather than guilt.

**Thesis assembled from the above.** *Through a structure that accumulates and then abruptly reports, and through an Edenic pattern stripped of its prohibition, the poet presents loss as seasonal rather than culpable, and the speaker's attitude as a resignation that requires no one to blame.*

Note that each paragraph passes through all three levels: **language → form or speaker → meaning.** A paragraph that stops at the first level has described; one that stops at the second has explained; only the third answers the question.

## Mark-losing habits

| Habit | Fix |
|---|---|
| Summarising the text | Replace every "then the speaker says" with a claim about effect |
| Listing devices | Three devices analysed beat eight named |
| One-sentence analysis after a long quotation | Shorten the quotation, lengthen the analysis |
| Opening with audience and purpose | Open with a universal claim that narrows to the text |
| "This quote shows" | Name the device and its mechanism instead |
| Forcing an allusion | Require two independent signals, or analyse the image on its own terms |
| Ignoring the second half of the text | Plan one paragraph that is explicitly about the close |
| Running out of time before the conclusion | Fix the conclusion at minute 53 whatever is unfinished |
