# Authors and context

Context earns marks only when it explains a **choice in the text**. Every fact below is followed by a line on how to use it. Use context **from the time the work was written or set**. Write in the past tense about history and the present tense about the text. ## Shakespeare and *Othello*

| Fact | How to use it in an essay |
|---|---|
| **Written c. 1603–04**, early in the reign of **James I** (from 1603). A **Jacobean** play | Call the original audience *Jacobean*, not Elizabethan. Their values on race, marriage and obedience are the ones the play tests |
| **First recorded performance:** 1 November 1604, at court in the Banqueting House at **Whitehall**, by the **King's Men** (Shakespeare's company, under the king's patronage) | The first audience included the king. James had written a poem on the Battle of **Lepanto** (1591) against the Turks, so the Turkish war in Act I would have struck a royal nerve |
| **Printed** in a quarto (1622) and the First Folio (1623); the two texts differ in places | Explains why editions vary in small words (*"the hollow hell"* / *"thy hollow cell"*; *"base Judean"* / *"base Indian"*). Quote one version and do not worry about the other |
| **Moors in Elizabethan and Jacobean England.** "Moor" could mean a North African Muslim or, more loosely, any dark-skinned African. A Moroccan ambassador visited Elizabeth I's court in 1600. Elizabeth's government issued orders in 1596 and 1601 for *"blackamoors"* to be removed from England. Black men and women lived in London, yet the slave trade had begun (Garber) | Othello is both admired and resented. The play's prejudice (*"thick-lips"*, *"sooty bosom"*) belongs to Venice and to the audience's own world, which makes Othello's *"Haply, for I am black"* painful |
| **Venice as a mercenary republic.** Rich, trading and governed by law and the Senate, Venice hired foreign generals to command its armies, partly so that no Venetian noble could seize power | Explains Othello's position: **indispensable and an outsider at once**. The Senate needs him in I.iii and recalls him the moment it can in IV.i |
| **Venice's reputation.** English writers saw Venice as a city of wealth, sophistication and sexual freedom, famous for its courtesans | Iago exploits this: *"In Venice they do let God see the pranks / They dare not show their husbands"* (III.iii). Othello, an outsider, cannot judge whether Iago is telling the truth about Venetian women |
| **The Ottoman wars and Cyprus.** Cyprus was a Venetian possession. The Ottoman Turks invaded in 1570 and took it in 1571; the Christian victory at Lepanto that year did not win it back | The play's victory over the Turks is a fantasy the first audience knew was temporary. The *"general enemy Ottoman"* is beaten by a storm, and the real enemy turns out to be inside the camp. *"Are we turned Turks?"* (II.iii) shows the fear of becoming the enemy |
| **Source: Cinthio's *Gli Hecatommithi*** (1565), a collection of Italian stories. In it only Disdemona is named; the Ensign lusts after her and, when she rejects him, turns his love to hate; he steals the handkerchief himself; he and the Moor beat her to death and fake an accident; the Moor is later killed by her relatives. The tale warns Italian women not to marry foreigners | **What Shakespeare changed:** he compressed months into days (hence double time); invented **Roderigo** and the **Senate scene** with Brabantio; made **Emilia** take the handkerchief and later speak out; gave Iago **many** motives instead of one; and made Othello **noble, eloquent and self-judging**, killing himself rather than being killed. Each change moves the blame from the outsider to Iago and to Venice |
| **Tragedy conventions (Aristotle).** **Hamartia**: the hero's flaw or error. **Peripeteia**: the reversal. **Anagnorisis**: the recognition. **Catharsis**: the audience's release of pity and fear | Hamartia = credulity, or insecurity; peripeteia = the handkerchief in Bianca's hand (IV.i); anagnorisis = *"O fool, fool, fool!"* (V.ii); catharsis = the final speech. A **domestic** tragedy: the stakes are a marriage, not a kingdom |
| **Double time.** Short time (about two nights and a day in Cyprus) runs alongside long time (references implying weeks); the critic John Wilson named it in 1849–50 | The speed denies Othello time to think (Kahneman's fast thinking); the long-time hints make the affair seem just possible. Details in [Othello key scenes](othello-scenes.md) |
| **Machiavelli's *The Prince*** (1513) was read in England as praising cunning; the stage "Machiavel" was a stock villain who confided in the audience | Iago as the Machiavel: soliloquies, plots, and the audience made his accomplice |

### Critics

Quote a critic, then **qualify** the view. The school ranks agreement with qualification above outright agreement or disagreement.

| Critic | Position in one line | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Samuel Taylor Coleridge** (Romantic poet, lecture notes c. 1810s) | Iago's soliloquy shows *"the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity"*: the reasons he gives are not his real reasons | Iago's motives (the 2024 question). Qualify: the motives are real but inadequate |
| **Marjorie Garber** (*Shakespeare After All*, 2004), the critic the school's slides use | Venice is law and order, Cyprus *"passion and confusion"*; the kneeling is *"the parody of a marriage"*; Iago makes the audience his *"co-conspirators"*; *"Othello kills Othello"* | Setting, III.iii, dramatic irony, the ending, race |
| **A. C. Bradley** (*Shakespearean Tragedy*, 1904) | Othello is a noble, romantic hero who is not naturally jealous; he is destroyed by a villain whose skill would have deceived anyone | Othello as victim; hero or victim |
| **F. R. Leavis** (essay "Diabolic Intellect and the Noble Hero", 1937) | Against Bradley: Othello falls because of his own pride and self-dramatising; Iago only triggers what is already in him | Othello as responsible; state of mind |
| **T. S. Eliot** ("Shakespeare and the Stoicism of Seneca", 1927) | In the final speech Othello is *"cheering himself up"*: he looks away from what he has done and dresses himself in a noble story | V.ii; reputation; hero or victim |
| **Thomas Rymer** (*A Short View of Tragedy*, 1693) | Mocked the play as *"the Tragedy of the Handkerchief"*: too much rests on a trifle | Symbolism. Quote him to **disagree**: the triviality is the point (*"Trifles light as air"*) |

## John le Carré and the novel

| Fact | How to use it in an essay |
|---|---|
| **John le Carré** was the pen name of **David Cornwell** (1931–2020). He worked for British intelligence, **MI5** and then **MI6**, in the late 1950s and early 1960s, including under diplomatic cover in West Germany | The film's world of files, procedure and quiet betrayal comes from someone who knew it. Its realism is the opposite of James Bond |
| His spy career ended in 1964, after the success of *The Spy Who Came in from the Cold* (1963). He later said his cover had been among those betrayed to the Soviets by **Kim Philby** | Betrayal from inside is personal for le Carré; the film inherits his bitterness about the Establishment |
| **Kim Philby** was a senior MI6 officer and Soviet agent, one of the **Cambridge Five**, recruited at Cambridge University in the 1930s (with Guy Burgess, Donald Maclean, Anthony Blunt and John Cairncross). Burgess and Maclean fled to Moscow in 1951; Philby defected in **1963** | **Bill Haydon is a Philby figure:** charming, upper-class, trusted because he *is* the Establishment. Use this for context, allusion and conventional-values questions |
| **The novel**, *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy*, was published in **1974** and set in the early 1970s. It is the first of the "Karla trilogy" | Note the adaptation: a 1974 novel squeezed into a 127-minute film, which is why the film withholds and compresses |
| **The BBC television series** (**1979**) starred **Alec Guinness** as Smiley, in seven episodes | The film had to do in two hours what television did in seven; Alfredson cut explanation and trusted images and silence |
| **Britain in 1973:** a declining power after the loss of empire, dependent on the United States, with strikes and economic trouble at home | Witchcraft exists to buy the Americans' respect; Connie's nostalgia for *"a real war"* (*check against the film*) and Haydon's *"ugly"* West express decline |

## Tomas Alfredson and the film

| Fact | How to use it in an essay |
|---|---|
| **Tomas Alfredson** is a **Swedish** director. His previous film was ***Let the Right One In*** (2008), a cold, quiet Swedish vampire story about a lonely boy; *Tinker Tailor* was his first English-language film | An outsider's view of the British Establishment. Both films share stillness, cold light, and lonely boys (Oskar, Bill Roach) |
| **Cinematographer: Hoyte van Hoytema**, who also shot *Let the Right One In* (and later *Interstellar* and *Oppenheimer*) | Credit the look to "Alfredson and his cinematographer": long lenses, frames within frames, smoky, faded colour |
| **Composer: Alberto Iglesias**, a Spanish composer known for his work with Pedro Almodóvar; the score was nominated for an Academy Award | Sparse, minor-key music that circles rather than resolves, so the puzzle is heard as well as seen. Do not confuse him with **Julio Iglesias**, the singer of *"La Mer"* |
| **Screenplay:** Bridget O'Connor and Peter Straughan, who won the BAFTA for adapted screenplay; O'Connor died before release and the film is dedicated to her. **Production designer:** Maria Djurkovic, who designed the orange soundproofed room | Name the production designer when discussing setting and the Circus room |
| **Style: a muted 1970s palette** of browns, greys and mustard, as if faded by cigarette smoke | Gloom and decline; warmer colour is kept for memory (the party) and for love (Istanbul) |
| **Style: long lenses** that compress space and make the camera feel distant, like a watcher | Surveillance: the viewer spies on the spies |
| **Style: silence** and diegetic sound instead of score | Suspense from what is withheld; the few music cues (the anthem, *"La Mer"*) carry irony |
| **Style: framing through glass**, doors, windows and corridors | Characters are always watched and always separated |
| **Released 2011** (premiered at the Venice Film Festival). Gary Oldman was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Actor | The **made** context: a film about institutions lying to themselves, made for a post-2008 audience. Use one hedged sentence at most, and say which context (set or made) you mean |
