# Othello quote bank

Paper 3 is closed book, so every quotation has to come from memory. This page arranges them **by theme and act**, **by character**, and as a **top 25**, then gives an **essay plan** for each likely question. The story and the scenes are in [Othello key scenes](othello-scenes.md). The background notes and the techniques are in [Othello](othello.md), and the terms are in [TERMS.md](../TERMS.md).

**Wording.** Quotations follow the Folger text, which is the text the school printed on the 2025 papers. Older editions spell some words differently (*pass'd*, *damn'd*, *to-night*). Either spelling is accurate. Where the editions differ in wording, it is noted.

**How to learn a line:** say the quotation, then its **act.scene**, **speaker**, **technique** and **effect**. Cover the table and recall all five.

## (a) Theme × act matrix

Each theme runs in play order, so a general essay can go act by act.

### 1. Jealousy and proof

| Quote | Where | Speaker | Technique | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *"it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets / He has done my office"* | I.iii | Iago | Rumour as motive; euphemism | Jealousy begins in **Iago**, not Othello, and it rests on gossip alone |
| *"the thought whereof / Doth, like a poisonous mineral, gnaw my inwards"* | II.i | Iago | Simile; poison | Jealousy eats the body from inside, and Iago passes his disease on |
| *"O, beware, my lord, of jealousy! / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on"* | III.iii | Iago | Personification; hypocrisy | The warning **plants** what it warns against; jealousy devours itself |
| *"I'll see before I doubt; when I doubt, prove"* | III.iii | Othello | Logical sequence of clauses | He believes he is rational, which is ironic, since he never gets real proof |
| *"Give me the ocular proof"* | III.iii | Othello | Imperative; legal diction | He demands to **see** and then accepts a **story** |
| *"Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ"* | III.iii | Iago | Simile; religious register | Predicts the handkerchief: any trifle becomes proof to a jealous mind |
| *"They are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they're jealous"* | III.iv | Emilia | Circular repetition | Jealousy needs no cause, so it is self-generated |
| *"Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, / Perplexed in the extreme"* | V.ii | Othello | Passive verb *being wrought* | He shifts the blame to Iago. Test whether the play supports *"not easily jealous"* |

### 7. Deception, appearance and reality

| Quote | Where | Speaker | Technique | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *"I am not what I am"* | I.i | Iago | Inverted biblical allusion (God's *"I am that I am"*, Exodus 3:14) | The whole play in five words, and a mock-divine boast |
| *"By Janus"* | I.ii | Iago | Mythical allusion: the two-faced Roman god | He swears by his own nature, a joke only the audience gets |
| *"'Tis a pageant / To keep us in false gaze"* | I.iii | First Senator | Theatre metaphor | Venice sees through the Turks' trick but not through Iago's |
| *"I am not merry, but I do beguile / The thing I am by seeming otherwise"* | II.i | Desdemona | Aside-like confession | Even the innocent perform, which shows that seeming is everywhere |
| *"When devils will the blackest sins put on, / They do suggest at first with heavenly shows"* | II.iii | Iago | Soliloquy; devil imagery | Evil disguised as good: his method, explained to us |
| *"Men should be what they seem"* | III.iii | Iago | Dramatic irony | Spoken by the one man who is not |
| *"O well-painted passion!"* | IV.i | Othello | Exclamation; acting metaphor | He now reads real grief as acting, so Iago's lie has inverted his sight |
| *"I look down towards his feet; but that's a fable"* | V.ii | Othello | Allusion to the devil's cloven hoof | The truth comes too late; Iago looks like any man |

## (d) Essay plans

Each plan is a thesis, then three or four paragraphs **in act order**, each with its method and quotations. For a **general** question, never use the printed passage. For a **passage** question, analyse the extract first and link out with quotations from the rest of the play.

### Jealousy

*"Examine the ways Shakespeare presents jealousy in the play."*

**Thesis:** Shakespeare presents jealousy not as Othello's private flaw but as a contagion that begins in Iago, spreads by insinuation, and needs no cause, so the play is less about a jealous husband than about how easily jealousy is manufactured.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Act I–II: Iago's jealousy comes first | The disease starts in the villain, and rests on rumour. Method: soliloquy | *"it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets / He has done my office"* (I.iii); *"like a poisonous mineral, gnaw my inwards"* (II.i); *"For I fear Cassio with my nightcap too"* (II.i) |
| 2. III.iii: jealousy planted | The warning plants the thing it warns against. Method: echo, hypocrisy, borrowed authority | *"the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on"*; *"She did deceive her father, marrying you"*; *"Give me the ocular proof"* |
| 3. III.iv: jealousy without cause | Emilia names jealousy as self-generating; Bianca's justified jealousy is dismissed. Method: repetition, parallel subplot | *"They are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they're jealous"*; *"This is some token from a newer friend"* |
| 4. IV–V: jealousy turns to murder, then excuse | Jealousy wrecks his language, then he rewrites it. Method: prose collapse, final speech | *"I will chop her into messes! Cuckold me!"* (IV.i); *"not easily jealous, but being wrought, / Perplexed in the extreme"* (V.ii) |

### Deception

*"Examine the ways Shakespeare presents deception in the play."*

**Thesis:** Deception in *Othello* works because the deceived are honest: Iago turns trust, courtesy and love into instruments, and the play's final irony is that Othello deceives himself into calling murder justice.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Act I: a deceiver announces himself | Iago tells the audience what he is; Venice reads the Turks' deception but not his. Method: dramatic irony | *"I am not what I am"* (I.i); *"By Janus"* (I.ii); *"'Tis a pageant / To keep us in false gaze"* (I.iii) |
| 2. Act II: virtue made the trap | Seeming good is his method. Method: soliloquy, devil imagery | *"They do suggest at first with heavenly shows"*; *"out of her own goodness make the net"* (II.iii) |
| 3. Acts III–IV: staged evidence | A handkerchief, an invented dream and an overheard conversation. Method: props, staging | *"Trifles light as air"* (III.iii); *"Nay, this was but his dream"* (III.iii); *"Look how he laughs already!"* (IV.i) |
| 4. Act V: the deception unravelled, and self-deception | Emilia's truth ends Iago's lies; Othello's *"honorable murderer"* is a final deception of himself. Method: reversal, irony | *"You told a lie, an odious, damnèd lie!"*; *"An honorable murderer, if you will"* (V.ii) |

### Women

*"Examine the ways Shakespeare presents women in the play."* (Also: relationships between men and women.)

**Thesis:** Shakespeare presents three women in three social positions (wife, servant and courtesan) and shows each judged by men's words rather than her own actions, while giving the play's clearest moral argument to the woman with least power.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Act I: Desdemona's agency against her father's image | She chooses and argues in public. Method: contrast of speeches | *"A maiden never bold"* (Brabantio); *"I do perceive here a divided duty"*; *"My downright violence and storm of fortunes"* (I.iii) |
| 2. Acts II–III: men's language about women | Misogyny as wit, then as possession. Method: rhymed "praise", possessive pronouns | *"wildcats in your kitchens"* (II.i); *"O curse of marriage, / That we can call these delicate creatures ours"* (III.iii) |
| 3. III.iv–IV.ii: women consumed | Emilia, Bianca and Desdemona each degraded. Method: appetite and book metaphors | *"They are all but stomachs"*; *"I must be circumstanced"* (III.iv); *"Made to write 'whore' upon?"* (IV.ii) |
| 4. IV.iii–V.ii: the counter-argument | Emilia argues equality, then disobeys and dies. Desdemona's last lie is either submission or love. Method: dramatic contrast | *"Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them"* (IV.iii); *"'Tis proper I obey him, but not now"*; *"Nobody. I myself"* (V.ii) |

### Othello: hero or victim (villain or victim)

*"Othello is either a malevolent villain or the victim of terrible circumstances." How far do you agree?* (2022)

**Thesis:** The either/or is false. Othello is a victim of Iago and of Venice's prejudice, yet he chooses murder and calls it justice. Shakespeare needs both truths for the tragedy to work.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Act I: the noble hero | Calm, eloquent, trusted by the state, so he is not a natural villain. Method: blank verse | *"Keep up your bright swords"* (I.ii); *"She loved me for the dangers I had passed"* (I.iii) |
| 2. III.iii: victim | Manipulated, and vulnerable because Venice has taught him he is an outsider. Method: echo, shared lines | *"Haply, for I am black"*; *"Thou hast set me on the rack"* (III.iii) |
| 3. IV.i–V.ii: agent of violence | He chooses the method and relishes the *"justice"*. Method: prose collapse, then ritual verse | *"Good, good! The justice of it pleases"* (IV.i); *"Yet she must die, else she'll betray more men"* (V.ii) |
| 4. V.ii: self-judgement | Recognition and suicide: he judges himself as a villain, but tells the story as a victim. Critics divide (Bradley against Eliot; see [context](context.md)) | *"O fool, fool, fool!"*; *"Like the base Judean, threw a pearl away"*; *"loved not wisely, but too well"* |

### Iago's motives (motiveless malignity)

*Coleridge called Iago's soliloquies "the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity". How valid is this assessment?* (2024)

**Thesis:** Coleridge is right that Iago's motives cannot explain the scale of his destruction, but *motiveless* goes too far: envy, class resentment and sexual jealousy are real. They are fuel rather than cause, and the constant underneath them is pleasure in power.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. I.i: a real grievance | Passed over for a "theorist". This **qualifies** Coleridge | *"Preferment goes by letter and affection"*; *"a great arithmetician"* (I.i) |
| 2. I.iii–II.i: motive-hunting | Motives multiply and contradict, and he admits they are unproven. This **supports** Coleridge. Method: soliloquy | *"I know not if 't be true, / But I, for mere suspicion in that kind, / Will do as if for surety"* (I.iii); *"Now, I do love her too"*; *"For I fear Cassio with my nightcap too"* (II.i) |
| 3. II.iii–IV.i: malignity as pleasure | He delights in the pain itself. Method: devil and poison imagery | *"Divinity of hell!"* (II.iii); *"Not poppy nor mandragora"* (III.iii); *"Work on, / My medicine, work!"* (IV.i) |
| 4. V.i–V.ii: envy, then silence | The nearest true motive is envy of goodness, and he ends by refusing any explanation, which completes Coleridge's point | *"He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly"* (V.i); *"Demand me nothing"* (V.ii) |

### Imagery and/or symbolism

*"Examine the ways that imagery and/or symbolism is used throughout the play."* (2025 Term 2)

**Thesis:** Shakespeare uses imagery to track infection: the animal, hellish and poisonous language Iago introduces passes into Othello's mouth, while the handkerchief and the light turn love into evidence and life into something that can be put out.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Animal imagery transfers | From Iago in I.i to Othello in IV.i, the clearest sign of who infects whom | *"an old black ram"* (I.i); *"as prime as goats, as hot as monkeys"* (III.iii); *"Goats and monkeys!"* (IV.i) |
| 2. Poison and hell | Iago's method is infection through the ear; his evil is presented as Satanic | *"I'll pour this pestilence into his ear"* (II.iii); *"The Moor already changes with my poison"*; *"Arise, black vengeance, from the hollow hell!"* (III.iii) |
| 3. The handkerchief | A love token becomes proof; its two origin stories show that its meaning is invested | *"This was her first remembrance from the Moor"* (III.iii); *"there's magic in the web of it"* (III.iv); *"an antique token / My father gave my mother"* (V.ii) |
| 4. Light, dark and the willow | Light is life that cannot be relit; the willow song makes the death feel foreordained | *"Put out the light"*; *"Promethean heat"* (V.ii); Desdemona's *"song of willow"* (IV.iii) |

### Language used to exploit and/or demean

*"Examine the ways language is used by characters to exploit and/or demean others in the play."* (2026 Term 2)

**Thesis:** In *Othello* language is the only weapon that matters. Iago exploits by speaking less than he means and demeans by speaking more, and when Othello adopts his vocabulary the hero becomes the abuser, until Emilia's plain speech breaks the spell.

| Paragraph | Point | Quotes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. I.i–I.iii: language to demean | Animal and racial slander shouted from the dark; Brabantio's *thing* | *"an old black ram"*; *"making the beast with two backs"* (I.i); *"such a thing as thou"* (I.ii) |
| 2. I.iii–II.iii: language to exploit | Repetition and flattery drain Roderigo; false comfort sends Cassio to Desdemona | *"Put money in thy purse"* (I.iii); *"Our general's wife is now the general"* (II.iii) |
| 3. III.iii: insinuation | The withheld statement and the echo; he exploits Othello's trust in his *"honesty"* | *"Think, my lord?"*; *"these stops of thine fright me the more"*; *"Men should be what they seem"* (III.iii) |
| 4. IV.ii–V.ii: the victim demeans, then truth answers | Othello's *"whore"* language; Emilia's insults turned on Iago; Iago's final silence | *"Impudent strumpet!"*; *"Was this fair paper"* (IV.ii); *"I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak"*; *"Demand me nothing"* (V.ii) |

### Othello's state of mind in V.ii (passage: *"It is the cause"*)

*"Comment on how, and with what effects, Shakespeare presents Othello's state of mind at this moment in the play."* (2022)

**Thesis:** Othello enters believing himself calm and just, but the speech shows a mind divided against itself: he dresses murder as ritual sacrifice while his love keeps breaking through, and his recovered verse is a performance of control rather than proof of it.

| Paragraph | Point | Evidence from the passage | Link beyond it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Murder as ritual justice | Repetition and religious diction make it a sacrifice, not a crime | *"It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul"*; *"Let me not name it to you, you chaste stars"*; *"Yet she must die, else she'll betray more men"* | He once vowed *"I'll see before I doubt"* (III.iii); he never saw |
| 2. Light and the rose | Images of irreversibility show that he knows what he is doing | *"Put out the light, and then put out the light"*; *"Promethean heat"*; *"When I have plucked the rose, / I cannot give it vital growth again"* | *"Chaos is come again"* (III.iii) |
| 3. Love breaking through | Kisses and paradox; tenderness that is now perverse | *"Be thus when thou art dead, and I will kill thee / And love thee after"*; *"This sorrow's heavenly; / It strikes where it doth love"* | *"But yet the pity of it, Iago!"* (IV.i) |
| 4. The dialogue: control slips | Short lines, questions, the inarticulate *"Hum!"*; she reads his body | *"Have you prayed tonight, Desdemona?"*; *"I would not kill thy soul"*; *"why gnaw you so your nether lip?"* | Verse recovered after the prose of IV.i; then *"O fool, fool, fool!"* |

### The Othello–Desdemona relationship in III.iv (passage: the handkerchief)

*"Comment on how, and with what effects, Shakespeare presents the relationship between Othello and Desdemona at this point in the play."* (2023)

**Thesis:** The marriage that began in I.iii as an exchange of stories ends here in a failure to communicate: Othello turns the handkerchief into a myth and a test, Desdemona answers with a small lie and a different subject, and each speech makes the other's case worse.

| Paragraph | Point | Evidence from the passage | Link beyond it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Love turned into a test | His exotic tale makes the token sacred and its loss *"perdition"* | *"That handkerchief / Did an Egyptian to my mother give"*; *"there's magic in the web of it"*; *"dyed in mummy"* | I.iii: he won her with tales; V.ii gives a different origin |
| 2. Fear and the small lie | Desdemona is frightened into a lie that seals her fate | *"Then would to God that I had never seen 't!"*; *"It is not lost. But what an if it were?"* | Emilia's *"I know not, madam"* earlier in the scene: the truth is withheld by both women |
| 3. Cross-purposes | Repetition against repetition: *"The handkerchief!"* against her pleas for Cassio | *"The handkerchief!"* (three times); *"I pray, talk me of Cassio"*; *"Away!"* | *"Thy solicitor shall rather die"* (III.iii): her persistence turns fatal |
| 4. What it shows | Emilia and Desdemona interpret the scene: realism against faith | *"Is not this man jealous?"*; *"They are all but stomachs"*; *"Something, sure, of state ... hath puddled his clear spirit"* | IV.i: he strikes her in public |
